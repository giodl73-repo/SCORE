## Review

I said in the first session that the bridge peak "must carry that weight through contour and harmony alone." I said the plain Eb at the peak was "harmonically tense but melodically anonymous." I said "a peak that is more melodically distinctive — a leap, an extended note, a specific rhythmic gesture — would sharpen the dramatic arc considerably."

The revision provides all three: a leap, an extended note, and a specific melodic gesture (the chromatic descent that echoes the A section).

I want to assess this not just as a list of improvements but as a compositional argument.

The bridge of a jazz standard — or of any AABA song — has a specific dramatic function: it must change something about what the final A means. In version one, the bridge changed what the A section's distinctive idea operated at — it migrated the idea from the harmonic level to the melodic level. This is a real change; the bridge was necessary.

In version two, the bridge does something additional: it demonstrates that the A section's distinctive idea works in the mediant key. The chromatic descent (F-Eb-Db) in bar 21 is the A section's inner-voice gesture (Eb-D-Db) transposed to the Gb major area. This is a developmental argument: the distinctive idea is not an Eb major idea; it is an idea that belongs to any key the piece visits. When the final A returns, the listener has heard the distinctive idea in two keys, and the A section's presentation of it in Eb major is now the third statement — the completion of a demonstration.

This is the bridge-as-necessity test (Innovation #23) applied at the highest level. In version one, the bridge was necessary because it migrated the idea. In version two, the bridge is necessary because it both migrates the idea and demonstrates its universality (within the piece's harmonic world). The final A cannot be what it is without this bridge.

Dramatic consequence: the final A in version two is a return to a home that has been demonstrated to be universal rather than arbitrary. Desirée's patient knowing (to use the "Send in the Clowns" analogy) is now grounded: the piece has shown that the distinctive idea — the long way back — belongs to all keys, and that returning to Eb major is not returning to where the piece started but returning to where the piece is most itself.

Is this too much for a jazz standard? Is this analytical content that the listener cannot hear?

I don't think so. The listener won't hear it as an argument about universality. They'll hear it as: the bridge peaked in a way that felt like the highest point of the piece, and then the final A felt like it arrived rather than merely returning. The argument is structural; the experience is emotional. Both are correct.

What I award:
- Structure: +2 from version one. The bridge now satisfies the bridge-as-necessity test at a higher level.
- Craft: +1 from version one. The bridge peak is now crafted, not merely present.
- Resonance: +1 from version one. The emotional arc is now complete; the arrival of the final A carries its full weight.
- Economy: unchanged. The revision is three targeted decisions; the bars 22-24 remain the one area of mild excess.
