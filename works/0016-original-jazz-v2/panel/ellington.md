## Review

Let me ask the question I always ask: whose music is this, and who plays it?

Version one answered: someone patient. The melody requires a player who waits. The bridge was the one moment where the composition asked for patience and didn't provide the melody to justify it — the peak was melodically plain, which asked the performer to generate the moment rather than realize it.

Version two: the composer has done the work the performer was previously being asked to do.

Let me examine each change.

The leap to F in bar 19. I want to know whose voice makes that leap. A trumpet? A tenor saxophone? On a trumpet, a major sixth upward to F — that's in the middle-upper register, where the trumpet speaks with clarity and confidence rather than effort. The F has brightness. It doesn't feel reached for; it feels arrived at. This is a player with the patience to wait for the leap and the confidence to take it when it arrives.

On a tenor saxophone, the same interval lands differently — the F is in the tenor's comfortable middle register, and the leap will sing rather than declare. Both work. The leap is idiomatic in both voices. But the character of the arrival differs: trumpet declares, tenor sings. The composition doesn't specify which; the performer will bring their voice to it, which is correct.

The sustained F. This is the most important revision. In version one, the peak passed through. In version two, it stays. The performer can do something with a two-beat sustain that they cannot do with a passing note: they can shape it, swell it, lean into it. The sustain is a gift to the performer. It says: the harmony is holding tension for you; you can hold the note and let the tension build beneath you. That's the moment where a great player makes a recording.

The chromatic descent: F-Eb-Db. This is the A section's distinctive idea brought to the melodic surface. In the original version, the distinctive idea (chromatic inner-voice descent) was only in the harmony; the bridge showed it as a chromatic melodic descent in the bridge's key area. In version two, the specific chromatic motion (the half-step descent through two notes) echoes more precisely what bars 1-2 of the A section contain. The performer who has internalized the A section's distinctive idea will feel this connection. The player who hasn't will still play the descent correctly — the notes are right — but won't know why the descent has the weight it has.

This is Craft-as-Casting (Innovation #13): the revision is designed for a player who knows what the piece is about, who has internalized the distinctive idea and can therefore recognize its echo in the bridge. A player without that knowledge will play the notes and produce something less than the full achievement. The casting question in version two is sharper than in version one.

On Economy: the revision is three targeted decisions. Nothing else changed. That's Economy in the revision process as well as in the composition: the composer found the one thing that needed to change and changed only that. This is how revisions should work.

Comparison scores:
- Structure: the bridge-as-necessity test now passes more completely. +1 from v1 (21 → 22).
- Craft: the bridge peak is now characterized. The Craft-as-Casting dimension is more precisely engaged. +1 from v1 (22 → 23).
- Originality: the revision is a Craft achievement, not an Originality achievement. Same score (12).
- Resonance: the emotional arc is now complete; the bridge peak now does the emotional work. +1 from v1 (13 → 14).
- Economy: three targeted decisions, nothing wasted. Same score (17 — the bars 22-24 chain is unchanged).
