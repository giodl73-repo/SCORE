# Performer reviews "Somewhere" from West Side Story (1957)

**Step 0 — Structural approach: DEVELOPMENTAL (modified art-song/Broadway form)**
The through-developmental form means the singer must sustain a single, accumulating emotional arc from opening to close. There is no verse-chorus reset; the performer cannot return to the beginning and take another run at the material. The arc must be continuous, and the climax must be earned by what came before it.

**Craft:** The song is well-written for the voice, with one consistent challenge that comes up in performance.

The opening interval — the tritone — is not difficult for trained singers; it is a familiar interval in the classical training canon. What is difficult is performing it so that it sounds tender rather than dissonant. The default hearing of a tritone is dissonance; the Bernstein context requires that it be heard as longing. This requires the performer to approach and execute the interval in a specific way — with particular attention to the approach note, the vowel on the interval, and the way the harmonic context (provided by the orchestra) is heard against the melodic gesture. It is not hard to sing the interval; it is hard to sing it so that the dissonance becomes tenderness.

The climax: the "Somewhere / Somehow / Someday" passage is in the upper middle range for most sopranos or mezzos — the range where the voice is most exposed and where the emotional commitment is most nakedly audible. This is correct placement: the climax should expose the performer, and it does. The challenge is sustaining three repetitions of the same phrase at the same dynamic and emotional level without dropping the commitment on the third. Most performers fall off slightly on the third "someday"; the score asks for it to be sustained at full commitment. It is performatively demanding.

The resolution section is the easiest to sing and the hardest to get right emotionally: the voice needs to come down from the climax to a genuine quiet without sounding depleted. The score supports this transition well — the melodic line descends in a way that allows the voice to settle without straining — but the emotional management required is significant.

Two points docked: one for the tritone-as-tenderness challenge (which is real, not a score error, but which I note as a significant performative demand not captured in the notation alone), and one for the third-repetition commitment problem at the climax.

**Resonance:** The song, when performed at the level it demands, produces the imagined place in the theater. The climax works on audiences consistently. The quiet resolution works. The song earns its canonical status as a concert piece — it is one of the few musical theater songs that works fully in concert performance, without requiring the dramatic context, because the music itself is complete enough to establish the emotional context it needs.

One point withheld: in productions where Maria is sung by a voice with limited upper-middle-range resonance, the climax loses some of its physical impact. The "Somewhere / Somehow / Someday" passage depends on the specific quality of the singer's voice in that range — the warmth, the openness — to create the fullness of the imagined place. With a lighter or thinner voice in that range, the imagined place is smaller. This is not a Bernstein failure; it is a performance reality. But it limits the song's Resonance consistency across different casts.
