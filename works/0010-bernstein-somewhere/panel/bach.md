# Bach reviews "Somewhere" from West Side Story (1957)

**Step 0 — Structural approach: DEVELOPMENTAL (modified art-song/Broadway form)**
Through-developmental form: harmonic language and emotional temperature build toward a climax and resolve. The form is neither sonata nor rondo nor AABA; it is through-composed in the specific developmental sense that each section earns the next by preparing the harmonic and emotional conditions for what follows. The structural goal is the climax chord and its emotional effect; everything else is preparation or resolution of that goal.

I will assess this piece as I assess all music: by examining what it is doing technically and asking whether the technical execution serves the musical argument.

**Structure:** The developmental arc is present and correctly organized. The harmonic preparation of the climax is methodical and correct: the song moves through increasingly complex harmonic territory, making each prior section less stable than the previous, until the climax chord arrives as the most complex and unresolved harmony in the piece. This is correct structural preparation.

My structural concerns are two. First: the form is somewhat underdetermined — the song moves from one emotional-harmonic section to the next without the formal clarity that I find necessary for the largest architectural scores. Developmental form requires that each section be structurally necessary to the next; in this song, some sections feel like preparation without quite earning their specific duration. The middle section (before the climax) is longer than the harmonic argument requires.

Second: the resolution after the climax is, as other reviewers have noted, slightly premature. The climax creates a harmonic-emotional state of significant complexity; the resolution does not give that state sufficient time to be fully felt before it is resolved. A longer dwell at the climax, followed by a more gradual resolution, would serve the structural goal better.

Three points docked: two for the middle-section proportion and one for the premature resolution.

**Craft:** The voice leading is sophisticated by the standards of the tradition being worked in. The tritone interval that opens the melody is handled correctly — the preparation and resolution of the tritone dissonance are textbook Baroque approach, which tells me that Bernstein understood the classical voice-leading grammar at the level where he could deploy its most challenging interval without error.

The climax chord is harmonically sophisticated. I will note, however, that it is not quite what I would call a chord that resolves correctly: it is a chord that intentionally does not resolve into its expected tonal destination, which produces the emotional effect of pointing-toward-something-unreachable. This is deliberately unresolved voice leading. I understand the intention and accept it; I cannot score it as high Craft in the classical sense because the voice leading is intentionally incomplete.

Two points docked: one for the intentionally unresolved climax chord (noting it as a deliberate choice rather than an error, but acknowledging that from a strict voice-leading standpoint it does not fully resolve) and one for some inner-voice movement in the middle section that is slightly thicker than the harmonic clarity of the argument warrants.

**Originality:** Synthesis originality is a concept I have complicated feelings about. I composed in a tradition that was deeply informed by multiple prior traditions — the Franco-Flemish counterpoint, the Italian opera tradition, the German chorale tradition — and I do not think of myself as a "synthesizer." I absorbed those traditions and composed in my own voice, which happened to contain all of them. Whether that is synthesis or simply compositional knowledge, I am not sure.

What I can say about "Somewhere": it achieves something that the classical tradition alone would not produce. The emotional directness of the melody — its singability, its immediate emotional legibility — is not a property of the classical tradition I work in. The classical tradition produces formal beauty; this melody produces immediate feeling. The combination produces something I find interesting and not quite like anything I have written.

Score 12. Genuine synthesis, genuine achievement. Not the highest Originality anchor because the synthesis is between two existing traditions rather than out of nothing.

**Resonance:** The intended effect is the creation of an imagined place that the listener inhabits knowing it is imagined. This is an unusual intended effect — most music aims to produce joy, or grief, or peace, or urgency; this piece aims to produce the specific emotional state of longing-for-a-place-that-does-not-exist. I will say: I experience something when I hear this piece that is not quite what I experience hearing anything else in my own tradition. Whether what I experience is the intended effect is harder for me to assess; the intended effect requires inhabiting a tradition of feeling that is specific to the musical theater's relationship with its audience, which is not my tradition.

Score 12. I hear something. I am not certain I am hearing what Bernstein intended me to hear, because what Bernstein intended may require the dramatic context of Act II — the audience's knowledge of Tony and Maria's situation — to produce its full effect. Without that context, I hear a beautiful and harmonically sophisticated song about a place that doesn't exist. Whether I inhabit the place the song imagines is less certain.

**Economy:** The developmental form means every section is doing accumulative work. Some sections are doing this work more efficiently than others. The middle section, as I noted, is longer than the harmonic argument requires. The resolution is also slightly extended beyond the structural necessity of the climax chord's resolution. Three points docked: two for the middle section proportion and one for the resolution extension.
