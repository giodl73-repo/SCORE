# Bernstein reviews "Somewhere" from West Side Story (1957)

**Step 0 — Structural approach: DEVELOPMENTAL (modified art-song/Broadway form)**
The song develops through a single sustained emotional arc from conditional longing at the opening to the climax's most fully inhabited expression of that longing, then to a resolving statement. The harmonic language develops in sophistication as the emotional temperature rises. The form is not AABA in any conventional sense; it is through-developmental — each section earns the next through emotional and harmonic accumulation. The climax is architecturally prepared by everything that precedes it.

I will try to be honest about a piece I have complex feelings about.

"Somewhere" is the moment in West Side Story that I most believe in — the moment where the synthesis I spent my career arguing for achieves something that I cannot achieve any other way. And it is the moment that I am most uncertain about, because I know how carefully it was constructed, and the knowledge of the construction coexists with the belief that the construction works.

**Structure:** The developmental arc is correct. The song moves from the opening conditional (There's a place for us) through the middle-section description of that place (Someday / Somewhere) to the climax's full emotional commitment to the imagined place, and then to the quiet certainty at the end that the place matters even if it does not exist. The form follows the character's emotional journey: from imagining to inhabiting to resolving.

What earns the two-point dock: the transition from the climax to the resolution is slightly rushed. The climax requires a longer dwell before the resolution arrives — the listener needs time to fully inhabit the harmonic-emotional position the climax creates before being brought back to the closing statement. The current transition feels one measure short. This is a small structural deficiency; it does not damage the song's effect; but it costs two points.

**Craft:** The voice leading is the piece's most reliable technical achievement. The tritone opening interval — which I am particularly pleased with — is handled so that the dissonance lands not as dissonance but as longing: the approach, the voice leading context, the harmonic preparation make it the most natural possible interval for this moment. This is not an accident. I spent considerable time ensuring that the interval that should feel most dissonant would feel most tender, and the voice leading achieves this.

The climax chord is also technically correct in ways that matter: the chord is reached through smooth voice leading, its polytonal character is prepared by the song's gradual harmonic sophistication, and its resolution — or non-resolution — into the subsequent passage is handled so that the unresolved quality feels intentional rather than incomplete. One point docked: the orchestration at the climax is very full, and there are moments where the inner voices are slightly muddier than the clarity of the harmonic argument warrants. Tunick's orchestration (this was my era of working with Tunick on the original) is generally excellent; at the climax, one more degree of transparency would have served the harmonic precision I was aiming for.

**Originality:** Synthesis originality. I will make the argument directly: what the song achieves — the impeccable voice leading that makes the Broadway melody possible, the sophisticated harmonic language that retains popular emotional directness, the climax chord that is harmonically precise while emotionally raw — is impossible without both traditions being simultaneously present. This is not derivation from two sources. This is the creation of a space that neither source could produce alone.

The highest Originality score would require saying something the tradition had no words for at all. I am one point short of that because the traditions I am synthesizing do have words for what I am doing — those words just cannot fully describe the synthesis. I score 14.

**Resonance:** The song achieves its intended effect: it creates, in the emotional reality of the listener, the place that does not exist in the world of the characters. The imagined place becomes real for the duration of the performance. The listener inhabits it knowing it is an imagination. This is the intended effect. Score 14.

One point withheld for the same reason as the climax-to-resolution structural note: the listener is brought back from the imagined place slightly too quickly. One measure more at the climax — one measure longer dwell in the place the song has created — would give the return to the resolution its full emotional weight. The return arrives before the listener is quite ready to be returned.

**Economy:** The through-developmental form means every measure is doing accumulative dramatic work. The arc is efficient. Two points docked: one for the climax section (slightly longer than strictly necessary; the emotional temperature holds one phrase past its structural peak before the resolution begins), and one for the extended introductory vamp that precedes the vocal entrance in some performance versions, which was not present in the original but has been added in many productions and works against the economy of the song's opening statement.

On the core question — is synthesis a form of originality or derivation? It is originality. The argument is the song. You cannot hear "Somewhere" and hear either the classical tradition or the Broadway tradition separately; you hear something that requires both to exist. That is the proof of synthesis.
