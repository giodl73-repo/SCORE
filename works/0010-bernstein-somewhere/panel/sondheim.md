# Sondheim reviews "Somewhere" from West Side Story (1957)

**Step 0 — Structural approach: DEVELOPMENTAL (modified art-song/Broadway form)**
Through-developmental form: the harmonic language and emotional temperature accumulate toward the climax and then resolve. No repeating verse structure; each section earns the next. The form is the emotional argument.

I will disclose my position: I wrote the lyric for this song. Bernstein wrote the music. This means I know the song from the inside — from the experience of writing to a melody that already existed, and of the melody being changed in response to what the lyric needed. I am not a neutral observer. But I can still be honest.

**Structure:** The developmental form works. The accumulation from opening conditional to climax is correctly paced. The climax arrives where it should arrive. The resolution resolves appropriately.

My structural concern: the song's architecture is more reliant on the dramatic context of Act II than its structure alone can support. When the song is performed in the show, with Tony and Maria's situation loaded into the audience, the song's structural progression is emotionally sufficient. When it is performed in concert, the structure must carry what the drama was carrying, and it is slightly underbuilt for that task. The song's form makes sense as a sequence of emotional positions; it does not quite make sense as an autonomous musical argument without the dramatic situation that generates those positions.

Three points docked: two for the context-dependence and one for the transition from climax to resolution, which both Bernstein and I hear as slightly premature.

**Craft:** The voice leading is sophisticated. The tritone opening interval is handled with great care — I know how much work went into that specific harmonic preparation. The climax chord is the piece's central technical achievement.

My Craft concern is, as always, about dramatic function. The song's Craft is high by classical standards and high by Broadway standards; the synthesis of the two is real. But there are moments — particularly in the middle section (the Somewhere / Somehow / Someday passage before the climax) — where the harmonic language is beautiful in the abstract without being precisely calibrated to Maria's dramatic position at that moment. Beautiful is not enough. The harmony should be what Maria is feeling, not merely what sounds beautiful at that harmonic position.

Two points docked: one for the middle-section harmonic-versus-dramatic alignment, and one for a phrase in the resolution where the melodic line rises when the dramatic position would suggest it should settle. Rising at the resolution feels like a conventional musical signal for hope rather than the specific resolution Maria and Tony have arrived at, which is more complicated than hope.

**Originality:** Synthesis as originality. I accept this argument with one qualification: the synthesis is more audible in the musical's other songs than in "Somewhere." "Cool," "America," and the "Prologue" demonstrate the synthesis at its most formally daring — the classical counterpoint against the jazz rhythms, the polytonal harmonies against the Broadway melodic idiom. "Somewhere" is the synthesis's emotional peak but not its technical peak. The technique is present; it is in service of an emotional effect rather than displayed for its own sake. This is correct for the dramatic context; it means the originality of the synthesis is slightly less legible in "Somewhere" specifically than in the show as a whole.

Score 13. Genuine and important synthesis originality; slightly less technically adventurous than the show's other demonstrations of the same principle.

**Resonance:** The song works. The imagined place becomes real for the duration of the performance. The emotional effect is achieved. Maria's longing is the listener's longing; the listener inhabits the imagined place while knowing it cannot be reached. Score 14.

Point withheld for the same reason I noted in the dramatic-functional Craft concern: the harmony at a few moments in the middle section is more generically beautiful than specifically Maria's. The listener hears something very moving; what they hear is not quite as precise as what Maria would feel.

**Economy:** The through-developmental form means everything is doing accumulative work. The song is as long as it needs to be in structural terms. Two points docked: one for the middle-section harmonic over-richness (one or two harmonies that are more elaborate than the dramatic moment requires) and one for a passage in the resolution that extends the closing statement by one phrase beyond what the dramatic argument needs.

On the lyric-note unity question from work 0007: "Somewhere" achieves this unity at the highest level at the climax, where the lyric names the place and the music creates it simultaneously. The middle section achieves it somewhat less completely — the lyric and the music are both beautiful but slightly in parallel rather than constituting exactly the same argument. The climax is the fullest instance of lyric-note unity in the show. The approach to the climax is very good but not quite as unified.
