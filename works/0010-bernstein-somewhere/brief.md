# Brief: "Somewhere" from West Side Story (1957)

## Subject

"Somewhere" is Tony and Maria's dream-sequence song from Act II of West Side Story (1957), with music by Leonard Bernstein and lyrics by Stephen Sondheim. It arrives in the musical after the rumble — after Bernardo has been killed, after Riff has been killed, after the moment when the story's tragic outcome has been set in motion and cannot be stopped. Tony and Maria imagine a place where they could be together. The place does not exist. The song names the place (Somewhere), describes it (There's a place for us), and acknowledges the distance (Someday / Somehow / Somewhere). The entire lyric is about a place that does not exist.

The music achieves something precise: it creates, in the emotional reality of the listener, the place that does not exist in the world of the characters. The song makes the imagined place real for the duration of its performance, and the listener inhabits that place, knowing it is an imagination, while inhabiting it.

The harmonic language of the song is neither purely tonal nor purely polytonal. The opening interval (E-flat to D) — across the break between "There's a" and "place for us" — is a tritone, the interval traditionally considered most dissonant in tonal music. But Bernstein deploys it with such care, in such a harmonic context, that it sounds like the most natural possible approach to the phrase. This is the synthesis in action: the classical tradition's most dissonant interval given the emotional weight of a Broadway melody's most tender moment.

The climax chord — at "Somewhere / Somehow / Someday" — is the most celebrated single harmonic moment in the score: a chord that expresses the longing for a place that does not exist by being a chord that is real in sound but unresolved in function, present as harmony but pointing toward something harmonic that it never becomes. It is an emotionally precise harmonic moment.

## Audience

**Original audience:** 1957 Broadway audience who experienced West Side Story at a moment when the American musical was in transition. The show's combination of classical formal ambition, jazz rhythm, and popular melodic idiom was not universally celebrated in 1957; critics debated whether Bernstein had produced a masterpiece or an overambitious hybrid. "Somewhere" specifically was not the show's initial commercial hit; "Maria," "Tonight," and "America" were more immediately popular. "Somewhere" became its canonical moment retrospectively, as the show's reputation grew.

**Contemporary audience:** Listeners for whom "Somewhere" is one of the canonical statements of longing in the American musical tradition — frequently performed at concerts, at graduations, at state occasions, as a free-standing expression of hope for a better place. The song is now more famous as a separate concert piece than it is in its original dramatic context, which creates an interesting tension: as a concert piece, its intended effect (the longing for a place that doesn't exist) must be achieved without the two-act dramatic context of Tony and Maria's situation.

## Tradition

West Side Story occupies an explicit position at the intersection of two traditions: the Broadway musical and the European classical tradition. This was Bernstein's conscious project across his career — demonstrating that jazz rhythms, classical voice leading, and popular melody could be simultaneously present in a single work without diluting each other. "Somewhere" is the purest instance of this synthesis in West Side Story: its classical voice leading is impeccable; its melodic contour is directly in the Broadway tradition; its harmonic language borrows from both without fully belonging to either.

The synthesis tradition demands: that the classical and popular elements not simply alternate but genuinely interpenetrate — that the classical voice leading makes the popular melody possible rather than constraining it; that the popular melodic idiom gives the classical harmony an emotional directness it would not achieve in the concert hall alone. When synthesis works, neither tradition is diluted by contact with the other; both are enriched.

## Core Question

**Is synthesis (borrowing simultaneously from two traditions to create something neither could produce alone) a form of originality — or derivation from two sources?**

The question is genuinely contested. On one view, synthesis is the highest form of originality: it requires mastery of both traditions, plus the creative judgment to recognize which elements of each tradition, in which combination, produce something impossible within either tradition alone. A synthesizer must be more comprehensively skilled than a specialist.

On another view, synthesis is derivation in two directions rather than one: the composer who borrows from two traditions is drawing on both rather than creating from neither. The argument against synthesis originality is that the composer's job is to make something new, not to make something made of old things well-assembled.

The panel will need to navigate this tension across the Originality dimension specifically, with secondary implications for Craft (does synthesis require a distinct mode of Craft assessment?) and Resonance (does the synthesis affect which listeners can receive the work's intended effect?).

## Open Questions

1. What is "Somewhere" in isolation? The song is structurally embedded in West Side Story; its dramatic context (Tony and Maria after the rumble, before the end) determines much of its emotional weight. When performed in concert as a free-standing piece, it must achieve its effect without the dramatic context. Does it?

2. The dream-sequence framing. In the original production, "Somewhere" was a dream sequence — literally staged as Tony and Maria's shared imagination of a better world. The staging acknowledged that the place doesn't exist while creating it for the audience. Does this acknowledgment of impossibility make the song's Resonance more honest or more melancholy?

3. Synthesis and Craft mode. The Craft anchors currently assess technical excellence against the tradition being worked in. "Somewhere" works in two traditions simultaneously. Should it be assessed against Broadway Craft standards, classical Craft standards, or a synthesis-specific standard that asks whether the classical and popular elements genuinely interpenetrate (rather than merely coexisting)?

4. The climax chord's emotional precision. Bernstein's climax chord at "Somewhere / Somehow / Someday" has been analyzed by music theorists as a specific polytonal or bitonal harmony. What gives it its specific emotional quality — its sense of longing for something real but unreachable? This is the Innovation #22 question.
