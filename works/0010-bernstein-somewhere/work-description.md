# Work Description: "Somewhere" from West Side Story (1957)

## The Dramatic Context: After the Rumble

"Somewhere" arrives in the most vulnerable structural position in the show. The rumble has occurred. Riff and Bernardo are dead. The romantic possibility that the first act established — Tony and Maria's love across the gang boundary — has been made catastrophically more difficult by Tony's killing of Maria's brother. The show has established, with brutal narrative logic, that there is nowhere for Tony and Maria to go. The song asks: what if there were?

This is not an evasion of the tragic situation. The song is entirely aware that the "somewhere" it imagines does not exist. The lyric acknowledges the distance: "Someday / Somehow / Somewhere" is explicitly conditional and future-tense — not a present-tense affirmation but a hope about a place that doesn't yet, and may never, exist. The song is sung from inside the tragedy, not as an escape from it.

The music creates the imagined place in sound while the lyric names the distance from it. The listener occupies both positions simultaneously: they are in the sound of the place (the harmonic warmth, the broad melodic arc, the sustained orchestral support) and in the knowledge that the place is impossible (the conditional lyric, the dream-sequence staging, the two-act context of the show's tragic arc). This simultaneous occupation is what makes "Somewhere" one of the most emotionally sophisticated moments in the musical theater.

## The Opening Tritone: Classical Dissonance in Broadway Context

The melody of "Somewhere" opens with a specific and unusual intervallic choice: the span from the pickup note to the first structural note is a tritone (or an interval that functions as a tritone in context). The tritone — the augmented fourth, or diminished fifth, the "diabolus in musica" of medieval theory — is the most harmonically dissonant interval in the Western tonal system. It divides the octave exactly in half and has no natural harmonic resolution direction. In strict counterpoint, it is avoided.

Bernstein places this interval at the beginning of "Somewhere" and makes it tender. The opening phrases of the song transform the tritone from dissonance to longing: the interval that implies irresolution, unreachability, the gap that cannot be comfortably filled, becomes the sound of reaching toward something just out of reach. This is the synthesis in technical operation: the classical tradition's most dissonant interval given the emotional character of the Broadway tradition's most tender moments. The dissonance and the tenderness are not in conflict; they are the same thing.

This technical choice argues, through the song, that longing is the emotion of the tritone — that the interval that cannot comfortably resolve is the precise harmonic shape of longing for a place you cannot reach.

## The Harmonic Language: Synthesis in Sustained Operation

The song's harmonic language throughout sustains the synthesis between classical and popular idioms. The voice leading is classical: the inner voices move smoothly, the dissonances are carefully prepared and resolved (or deliberately left unresolved, which is also a classical choice), the part-writing is sophisticated in the way that distinguishes Bernstein's Broadway work from most of its contemporaries.

The melodic contour is Broadway: broad, singable, emotionally direct, capable of being remembered after a single hearing. The melody is what a non-musician would carry out of the theater; the harmonic language is what a musician would notice and remember. Both are present simultaneously.

The neither-purely-tonal nor purely-polytonal harmonic language is the synthesis's most specific achievement. Bernstein does not alternate between classical and popular harmonies; he creates harmonies that participate in both simultaneously — chords that would be analyzed differently by a classical theorist and a jazz musician, both analyses being correct, the chord doing different work for each listener.

## The Climax Chord: Harmonic Emotional Precision

The song's structural climax — the moment that both the melody and the harmonic language have been building toward — arrives at the sustained repetition of "Somewhere / Somehow / Someday." The chord at this moment has been analyzed as a polytonal or bitonal harmony, a chord that simultaneously suggests two different tonal centers without fully resolving into either.

What gives this chord its specific emotional character is not its polytonal structure per se but a more precise musical quality: it is a chord that is real (present in sound, sustained, harmonically specific) while pointing toward something it does not become (unresolved in function, referring to a tonal resolution that the song never quite provides). The chord exists in the music the way the place "somewhere" exists in the lyric: present as an imagination, pointing toward a reality it cannot reach.

This is harmonic emotional precision in the precise sense the brief names: a chord that expresses a specific, nameable emotional state (longing for a real but unreachable place) by being harmonically real but functionally pointing-beyond-itself, present but not arrived, here but not there.

## Bernstein's Synthesis Philosophy in Action

Throughout his career, Bernstein argued that the energy of jazz rhythms in a symphonic context is not a category violation but an enrichment; that the melodic clarity of the Broadway show tune has something to teach the academy; that synthesis across traditions produces something neither tradition could produce alone. "Somewhere" is his strongest demonstration of this argument.

The song does something that neither the classical tradition nor the Broadway tradition could produce alone:

The classical tradition alone could produce: a song with impeccable voice leading, sophisticated harmonic language, and structural rigor. It could not produce: a melody this immediately singable, this emotionally direct, this capable of being remembered and carried by a non-musician listener.

The Broadway tradition alone could produce: a melody this broad and singable, this emotionally legible, this theatrically effective. It could not produce: harmonic language this sophisticated, voice leading this careful, a climax chord with this level of harmonic-emotional precision.

Together, they produce: a song with impeccable voice leading AND a singable melody; a sophisticated harmonic language AND immediate emotional legibility; a climax chord of technical precision AND raw emotional impact. The synthesis produces something that is impossible without both traditions being simultaneously present.

This is Bernstein's argument for synthesis as a form of originality: not derivation from two sources, but the creation of a space that requires both sources to exist.
