# Work Description: "Cross Road Blues" (1936) — Robert Johnson

## The Recording

"Cross Road Blues" is a solo blues performance by Robert Johnson, recorded ca. June 1936 in a San Antonio hotel room converted into a makeshift recording studio. Johnson plays guitar and sings simultaneously, producing both the vocal lines and the guitar responses that together constitute a dialogue no one else is present to complete. The recording runs approximately two and a half minutes and contains four choruses over the 12-bar blues form — though "twelve bars" is a nominal description, because Johnson stretches and compresses the bar lengths freely, and the count is more felt than counted.

## The Form

The 12-bar blues form in E minor (or, more precisely, an ambiguous modal space that alternates between E and a relative major area without fully committing to either). The harmonic movement: tonic (four bars), subdominant (two bars), return to tonic (two bars), dominant/subdominant movement (two bars), return to tonic (two bars). This is the skeleton; what Johnson does with it varies chorus by chorus, phrase by phrase, measure by measure.

The structure is simultaneously fixed and elastic. The form is the container; the content — the specific slide lines, the vocal inflections, the timing of the guitar responses — emerges from performance. This is DEVELOPMENTAL in the sense that the blues form provides the architectural container, and IMPROVISED/FOUND in the sense that the specific realization of each chorus is discovered in real time. The rubric categorizes this as a mixed approach.

## The Technique

**Slide guitar**: Johnson uses a bottleneck slide (possibly a broken glass neck from a bottle) to produce pitches that float between the frets. The slide creates continuous pitch movement — portamento, glissando — between notes, rather than the discrete pitch relationships of fretted playing. This allows Johnson to play the "blue notes" — the flattened third and seventh that define the blues' characteristic sound — not as chromatic approximations but as exact microtonal inflections. The slide is an instrument of precision, not imprecision; the pitches Johnson produces are where he intends them to be.

**Vocal technique**: Johnson's voice uses deliberate breaking and bending. A note that might be sustained through its duration in Western classical singing is cut short, bent upward or downward, or released into a vocal break. The break — the crack in the voice — is not a technical failure; it is an expressive tool as specific as a bow pressure marking in a string part. When Johnson's voice breaks on certain syllables, he has placed the break where the music needs it.

**Intra-performer call-and-response**: The vocal phrase states the melodic and textual line; the guitar responds in the space between vocal phrases. The response is not decorative; it is a dialogue partner, commenting on, completing, or contradicting what the voice has said. The effect is of a conversation conducted by a single person who contains both voices — a technique that requires the guitar and voice to function as separate compositional voices even though they originate from the same musician.

**Elastic time**: Johnson's pulse is not metronomic. The bar lengths expand and contract — a phrase that needs more time to complete gets more time; a phrase that arrives at its destination early ends early. The underlying pulse is present throughout; what changes is the relationship between the phrase rhythm and the bar rhythm. This is a specific technical skill: the ability to reshape the temporal container without losing the listener's sense of pulse.

## The Text

The lyric: a man stranded at the crossroads as the sun goes down, trying to flag a ride, unable to get one. He calls on multiple figures (Willie Brown, a woman who is absent, an unnamed "rider") without response. The sky darkens. Nobody stops.

The lyric operates simultaneously as literal narrative (a man genuinely trying to hitch a ride at dusk) and as something darker — the crossroads in Delta blues culture was associated with supernatural transaction, the meeting of the sacred and the profane, the place where choices are made that cannot be unmade. Johnson does not explain the mythology; he uses the crossroads as if its resonance is understood.

## The Effect

A solo blues performance by one musician performing both voices of a dialogue, using a slide guitar technique that produces pitches unavailable on a standard instrument, with a vocal delivery that uses breaks and bends as expressive tools rather than decorative color, over a 12-bar blues form that is present as a formal container but continuously stretched and reshaped by what the music needs at each moment.

The cumulative effect is of restlessness, urgency, and isolation. The man at the crossroads cannot stop moving, even within the confined space of a two-and-a-half-minute blues performance. The music enacts what the lyric describes: the inability to rest, the darkness coming down, the calls that go unanswered.
