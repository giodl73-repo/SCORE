# Miles Davis — "Cross Road Blues"

**Step 0 — Structural approach:** DEVELOPMENTAL + IMPROVISED/FOUND (mixed). The blues form provides the container. Each chorus finds its specific shape in performance. Both designations are real; neither is sufficient alone.

---

## Structure /25

**Score: 20**

The blues form is the structure and is handled correctly — meaning Johnson never loses it, never forgets where he is, never arrives somewhere the form doesn't support. The elastic bar lengths are not structural failures; they are structural choices, and each choice is justified by what the music is doing at that moment. When the vocal phrase needs another beat to complete itself, it takes the beat, and the form accommodates it.

What prevents a higher score is the ceiling imposed by the form itself. The 12-bar blues form, even handled brilliantly, has a structural ceiling below the architectural complexity of, say, through-composed forms. Johnson works within the container completely. But the container is small.

The intra-performer call-and-response is structurally elegant: by performing both voices of the dialogue, Johnson creates a self-contained structure that neither needs nor permits another musician. The piece is architecturally complete as a solo performance. That's a structural achievement.

Score: **20**. Strong structure within the form; appropriate elasticity; intra-performer dialogue complete. Points withheld for the structural ceiling of the form.

---

## Craft /25

**Score: 22**

*Non-Western Craft criteria applied. Delta blues tradition standards: slide precision, vocal inflection control, intra-performer timing, elastic pulse control.*

The slide technique is precise. The notes Johnson plays with the slide are not approximations of tempered pitches; they are specific microtonal inflections, placed where they are intended. This is a high Craft achievement in a technique that most practitioners deploy imprecisely.

The vocal breaks are deliberate. I've heard singers who break their voice accidentally and singers who break it on purpose. Johnson breaks it on purpose. The placement of the break — not after the note sustains but at the moment the note needs to change — is controlled technique, not uncontrolled emotion. These are not the same thing. Uncontrolled emotion produces random breaks. Controlled technique produces breaks where the music needs them.

The intra-performer timing — the ability to leave enough space for the guitar response without dragging the vocal phrase, and to complete the guitar response without rushing back to the vocal — is a Craft achievement that most solo performers cannot manage cleanly. Johnson manages it cleanly throughout.

Small deduction: the elastic time, while structurally justified, occasionally creates moments where the pulse's underlying stability is slightly ambiguous — where a listener without blues listening experience might lose the thread. This is a minor issue in a tradition where elastic time is expected, but it is a real one.

Score: **22**. Near the top of the Improvised Craft anchor band. Slide precision, vocal control, intra-performer timing, elastic pulse — all above the tradition's average.

---

## Originality /15

**Score: 12**

Johnson is working within a tradition — the Delta blues — that has formal conventions and well-established vocabulary. The 12-bar form, the I-IV-V, the blue notes, the call-and-response — all of these are the tradition. Johnson's originality is interpretive: what he does with the received vocabulary is distinctive enough to constitute a voice, but the vocabulary itself is the tradition's.

The intra-performer call-and-response is more developed in Johnson than in most of his contemporaries — the guitar lines are more harmonically sophisticated, more melodically complete, more architecturally aware. This is the primary original contribution: taking the blues form's structural potential and realizing it more completely than anyone else working in the same tradition at the same moment.

The vocal delivery — the specific way Johnson uses breaks and bends — is also his own, not borrowed from any single predecessor. The voice is recognizable.

Score: **12**. Strong interpretive originality within the tradition. The form is received; what Johnson does within it is his. Not the founding gesture of a new form, but the highest realization of an existing one.

---

## Resonance /15

**Score: 13**

The intended effect is clear: restlessness, urgency, isolation, the darkness coming down. The music produces these effects.

The slide guitar lines have a quality of never quite settling — they're always moving toward or away from something, never resting on a stable pitch. This enacts the restlessness of the lyric. The man at the crossroads can't stay; the music can't stay either.

The vocal breaks produce the urgency. A smooth, sustained vocal line would be a different emotional communication — more resigned, perhaps, or more dignified. The breaking voice communicates something closer to desperation without tipping into sentimentality.

The one-point deduction: the recording's sonic quality — the inherent limits of 1936 recording technology — creates some distance between the music and the listener that would not be present in a live performance context. This is not Johnson's failure; it is a condition of the historical record. But it affects the Resonance score.

Score: **13**. The intended effect arrives clearly. Small deduction for recording-mediated distance.

---

## Economy /20

**Score: 17**

Nothing is wasted. The guitar responds when the voice pauses; the voice picks up when the guitar's response is complete. No phrase overstays its welcome. The elastic time takes what it needs and no more.

The repetition structure of the blues form — four choruses over the same 12-bar pattern — is the tradition's economy. This is not repetition without evolution; each chorus finds its own shape while using the same container. The choruses accumulate rather than repeat.

Small deduction: two and a half minutes may actually be slightly short for what the music is doing — the intra-performer dialogue is rich enough that more development would not be waste. Johnson stops where the recording medium requires him to stop, not necessarily where the music is finished.

Score: **17**. Highly economical within the form. Each element earns its place. Minor deduction for medium-imposed brevity.

**Miles's note on Innovation #33 (Intra-performer call-and-response):**

The interesting structural question here is what to call what Johnson is doing. He's performing two voices of a dialogue — but not sequentially, not in alternation, not with enough space between them to be two separate musicians. The guitar and voice occupy the same performer, the same instrument complex, the same temporal space. The "response" happens in the space the voice leaves, not after the voice is done. This is a structural type that the existing Step 0 categories don't fully capture. The blues form provides the container; the intra-performer dialogue provides the content architecture within that container. Both are real. Neither is sufficient alone. The mixed designation is right, but the rubric might need language for the specific case where one performer contains both structural voices.
