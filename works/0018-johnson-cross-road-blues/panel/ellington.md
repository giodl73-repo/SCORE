# Duke Ellington — "Cross Road Blues"

**Step 0 — Structural approach:** DEVELOPMENTAL + IMPROVISED/FOUND (mixed). The 12-bar blues form is the architectural container. Within it, each chorus is discovered in performance. The container is fixed; the content is found.

---

## Structure /25

**Score: 19**

The structure is exactly right for what it is — a solo blues performance using the 12-bar form as a container within which each chorus develops its own character. Johnson never loses the form; he stretches it, compresses it, reshapes it, but always in relationship to it, never departing from it.

What I notice is the dialogue structure within the solo format. By performing both the vocal line and the guitar response, Johnson creates an internal structure that would, in an orchestra, require two separate voices. The way he calibrates the space between vocal phrase and guitar response — leaving exactly enough room for the guitar to speak without letting the momentum drop — is an orchestration problem solved by a single musician. That's a structural achievement I recognize from my own work, approached from an entirely different direction: I write for specific voices; Johnson contains the specific voices within himself.

The form's ceiling limits the score. Four choruses over 12 bars is a container, and it's a small one. But within it, Johnson builds consistently from one chorus to the next, increasing the urgency of the guitar responses as the vocal narrative darkens. There's arc. It's not an accident.

Score: **19**. Well-organized within the form; internal arc present; intra-performer dialogue structurally elegant. Constrained by the formal ceiling.

---

## Craft /25

**Score: 22**

*Non-Western Craft criteria applied. Delta blues tradition: slide precision, vocal inflection, intra-performer timing, elastic pulse.*

Johnson's guitar is a specific instrument — not "guitar" generically but this slide-guitar technique, this bottleneck approach, this microtonal vocabulary. I know what it means to write for a specific instrument rather than for "an instrument." Johnson doesn't write for guitar; he plays a specific version of guitar that no one else played in quite this way.

The slide work is precise. The pitches between the frets — the blue notes in their microtonal specificity — are placed with intention. This isn't random slide movement; it's controlled, purposeful, exactly located. Compare it to imprecise slide work, which wavers around pitches rather than landing on them, and the difference is clear. Johnson lands.

The vocal breaks. Every voice breaks sometimes. What Johnson does is make the break serve the music — he places the break where the phrase needs to change direction, where the emotion has reached its peak, where sustaining the note would be less truthful than releasing it. This is Craft: knowing where to break.

The intra-performer timing is exceptional. The space Johnson leaves between vocal phrase and guitar response is calibrated to allow the guitar to speak without rushing and to conclude without dragging. This is the timing question I face every time I balance a soloist against the orchestra. Johnson solves it by being both the soloist and the orchestra, which requires the same judgment applied internally.

Score: **22**. High by the tradition's standards. The slide precision, the vocal control, the timing — all above average, and the intra-performer balance is a Craft achievement that would be recognizable in any tradition.

## Originality /15

**Score: 12**

Johnson is the finest practitioner of a tradition, not the inventor of it. The 12-bar form, the call-and-response structure, the slide technique, the vocal breaks — all of these are the tradition's vocabulary. What Johnson contributes is the realization of the tradition's potential at a level no contemporary was matching.

The specific harmonic complexity of Johnson's guitar work is slightly ahead of what his peers were doing — the turnarounds are more sophisticated, the chord voicings are denser, the relationship between guitar and vocal harmonically more complex. Whether this represents formal training or independent development, it represents a compositional intelligence that is Johnson's own.

The intra-performer call-and-response, realized with this degree of dialogue clarity, is Johnson's specific contribution to the tradition. Other players did both guitar and voice simultaneously; none created as clear and complete an internal dialogue structure. That's the originality.

Score: **12**. High interpretive originality. The form is received; the realization is his.

---

## Resonance /15

**Score: 13**

The restlessness is the point, and the music is restless. The slide never fully settles; the vocal never fully sustains; the pulse stretches and contracts without finding stability. A man at a crossroads who can't get moving, enacted by a music that can't stand still.

The blues communicates differently than the orchestral music I know best. The communication is more direct, more physical — the blue notes bypass harmonic argument and go straight to something that isn't processed as harmony at all. This is not a lower form of resonance; it is a different kind, operating at a level that my orchestral palette, for all its richness, does not always reach.

The darkness builds across the choruses. By the final chorus, the guitar responses are more urgent, the vocal breaks more frequent, the overall atmosphere darker. Johnson manages a narrative arc inside a two-and-a-half-minute blues. That's not nothing.

Score: **13**. The intended effect arrives consistently. The recording distance costs one point.

---

## Economy /25

**Score: 17**

No waste. The guitar speaks when the voice pauses; the voice speaks when the guitar has finished. Neither crowds the other. The elastic time is precise: it takes what the phrase needs and returns to the pulse without overstaying.

The four-chorus structure is the blues form's economy — not a shortcoming but a convention, and the convention serves economy well. Each chorus does something the previous chorus could not.

Score: **17**. Clean and economical throughout.

**Ellington's note on Innovation #33:**

What Johnson is doing is the most reduced version of what I spend my career doing: building a dialogue between specific voices. I build a dialogue between Cootie Williams and Ben Webster and Jimmy Blanton — specific people, specific sounds. Johnson builds a dialogue between his voice and his guitar. The scale is completely different; the principle is the same. The voice calls; the guitar responds. The guitar has a personality distinct from the voice — it is more harmonically sophisticated, more melodically searching, less committed to the lyric's direction. That's a dialogue, not accompaniment. The rubric's Step 0 should have a way to name what happens when one performer contains both sides of a dialogue — it's neither purely developmental nor purely improvised; it's a third thing.

**Ellington's note on Innovation #34:**

The question of what counts as Craft excellence is the right question for this recording, and the rubric's v1.3 Non-Western Craft Vocabulary note is on the right track. But I want to be specific about what Johnson is demonstrating. The vocal break, applied with precision, is as demanding as any bel canto ornament. The slide pitch, placed with intentionality, is as demanding as any double-stop in the string tradition. The elastic time, maintained without collapsing the pulse, is as demanding as any rubato in the piano tradition. None of these are lesser versions of Western classical Craft; they are equivalent achievements in a different technical language. The rubric should score them accordingly — which I believe it does, and I am scoring at the high end of the Craft anchor for that reason.
