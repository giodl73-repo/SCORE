# Brief: "Being Alive" from Company (1970)

## Subject

"Being Alive" is the eleven o'clock number from Stephen Sondheim and George Furth's musical Company (1970), directed in its original production by Harold Prince. It is Bobby's climactic solo: the last major song in the show, arriving at the point where the musical has spent two acts presenting Bobby — a single man surrounded by married friends, watching their relationships with amused, wary distance — and now demands that he answer the question the show has been building toward: do you want to live alone, or do you want someone to hold you too close, hurt you too deep, make you aware of being alive?

The song begins in Bobby's characteristic mode: interior, careful, intellectually aware of the argument being made but not yet inside it. The musical setting is slow, contained, self-possessed. Then something shifts. The temperature rises incrementally. By the climax, Bobby is no longer analyzing the experience of intimacy from outside; he is demanding it, from a fullness of desperation that the entire show has been loading into him. He reaches a moment of loud, urgent, almost embarrassing need — and then comes out the other side to something quieter and more resolved: "Somebody hold me too close / Somebody hurt me too deep / Somebody sit in my chair / And ruin my sleep / And make me aware / Of being alive." The lyric is the same as the one he began with, but it means something different now. He has earned it.

The song makes the same argument in music and in words simultaneously. The melodic line rises in stages; the harmony becomes more complex and harmonically urgent as the emotional temperature rises; the orchestration (Jonathan Tunick's original) builds from sparse solo piano to full orchestra at the climax. Music and lyric are not parallel tracks: they are the same argument. When the lyric says "somebody hold me" and the music reaches upward and outward, the music is holding as well as asking to be held.

## Audience

**Original audience:** 1970 Broadway audience who had watched Bobby for two acts. They arrived at "Being Alive" knowing Bobby — his wit, his deflection, his genuine warmth toward the couples he observes, his inability or unwillingness to commit to intimacy. The song works for them because they have invested in Bobby and need him to break open. The audience's two-act investment is what gives the climax its weight.

**Contemporary audience:** Musical theater practitioners and students who study the song as the paradigm case of the eleven o'clock number — the song that arrives when the show's dramatic argument must be concluded and the central character must transform. The song is also frequently performed out of context, as a concert piece or audition piece, where it must work without the two-act investment. The question of whether it works out of context is a real one: the climax is partly fueled by dramatic context, and removed from that context it may peak too easily.

## Tradition

The musical theater song form of 1970, as shaped by Sondheim's training under Oscar Hammerstein: the AABA form (verse-verse-bridge-verse) as a default structure that can be modified or abandoned when the dramatic content requires it. "Being Alive" is a modified AABA — the repeating A section is a thematic and musical refrain ("Somebody hold me too close") that appears at different emotional registers as the song develops; the bridge is a dramatic shift in emotional position, not merely a musical contrast. The form serves the dramatic function.

The tradition's demands: every note and every word must serve the character in the moment; the form must emerge from the dramatic content rather than being imposed on it; the song must accomplish a dramatic function that dialogue cannot accomplish — the climactic emotional transformation that requires music because language alone is insufficient. The eleven o'clock number has a specific dramatic function: it must be the moment when the show's central dramatic question is answered, when the central character arrives at whatever understanding the musical has prepared them for.

Sondheim meets these demands and adds his own: the lyric and the music must make the same argument simultaneously, such that neither could achieve the effect alone.

## Core Question

**In musical theater, does Craft require dramatic function — is technically correct music that fails the character a Craft failure?**

This is Sondheim's central craft conviction: every technical choice — harmonic, rhythmic, melodic — must serve the character in the moment. A beautiful harmony that does not reveal something about Bobby's psychology at that specific moment in the show is not just neutral; it is a failure, because it occupies musical space that should have been doing dramatic work. By this standard, Craft is not separable from dramatic function. The most technically polished song that does not serve its character is less well crafted than the simpler song that nails the character's truth.

The question for the panel is whether this standard is correct — whether the rubric's Craft dimension should incorporate dramatic function for musical-theater works — and if so, how to assess "Being Alive" against it.

## Open Questions

1. Does "Being Alive" work out of context? The climax draws its weight partly from two acts of Bobby. Removed from that context — performed in concert or as an audition piece — the same musical gestures arrive with less prepared fuel. If the song works fully only within the show, is that a limitation or a measure of how well it is integrated into its dramatic structure?

2. The AABA refrain as dramatic device. The repeating lyric ("Somebody hold me too close") is heard at least three times at different emotional temperatures. This is economical — one lyric doing multiple dramatic jobs — but is it also the song's central formal argument: that the same words can mean different things at different temperatures, and that music is the medium in which the difference is felt?

3. What is the status of Jonathan Tunick's orchestration? Tunick's orchestration was integral to the original production; Sondheim and Tunick worked together closely throughout Company. The orchestration — its specific choices about which instruments carry which gestures, how the buildup is paced, how the climax is sonically supported — is not the composer's sole work, but it is the piece as heard. Should the orchestration be scored separately from the song, or as the song?

4. The "I'm not frightened" moment. Late in the song, there is a passage where Bobby sings "I'm not frightened" — which, dramatically, is the moment where the song could have ended prematurely, where the character has almost convinced himself he doesn't need intimacy. The music at this point briefly stabilizes at a false plateau before the real climax arrives. This is a sophisticated dramatic-musical move: the false resolution is structurally necessary for the real one to feel earned. Does the rubric's Structure dimension have adequate language for this kind of false-resolution preparation?
