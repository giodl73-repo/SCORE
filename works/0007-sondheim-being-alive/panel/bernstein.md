# Bernstein reviews "Being Alive" (1970)

**Step 0 — Structural approach: DEVELOPMENTAL (modified AABA song form, musical-theater)**
AABA with the A section deployed at varying emotional temperatures. The development is in the character's emotional position rather than in the harmonic or thematic material itself. The bridge functions as false plateau before the climax, which is a sophisticated dramatic-structural device. The form develops through Bobby's transformation.

"Being Alive" is the eleven o'clock number that almost breaks the show's ironic frame, which is exactly what it needs to do. Company is a show that keeps its emotional distance, that observes marriage and intimacy with a rueful, brilliant, slightly chilly intelligence. Then "Being Alive" arrives, and the show that has been keeping its distance suddenly cannot maintain it. Bobby breaks open, and through Bobby, Company breaks open.

This is structurally the most demanding thing a musical can ask of a song: close the show's ironic distance, not by abandoning it (Bobby remains self-aware throughout) but by acknowledging that awareness is not protection. The song asks Bobby to want something desperately, in public, without irony. And it delivers this.

**Structure:** The development is correct. The architecture of the false plateau is particularly admirable — it is the moment that, in West Side Story terms, I would think of as the moment before "Somewhere," when Tony and Maria need the audience to feel that their togetherness is impossible before we can fully feel what their imagination of it means. Bobby's false plateau — the moment where he almost convinces himself he doesn't need this — is the structural preparation for the climax's weight. Without the pullback, the final reaching is merely loud. With it, the reaching is necessary.

Two points docked for the same structural observation Sondheim makes: the false plateau is slightly longer than it needs to be. I add a second structural observation: the recovery from the false plateau to the climax is slightly abrupt. The music accelerates through the transition in a way that leaves the listener one beat behind the dramatic event. This is a small deficiency; it does not damage the song's effect; but it is audible to me as a missed connection between musical and dramatic timing.

**Craft:** The harmonic language is the song's primary technical achievement. The opening section's unresolved chords are a specific technical choice: they use the kind of harmonic ambiguity that I deployed in West Side Story precisely to represent psychological states that have not yet resolved. Bobby's harmonies are unresolved because Bobby is unresolved. This is not word-painting; it is a deeper integration of harmonic language with dramatic psychology.

The climax chord — the moment of maximum harmonic tension — is exactly right. It is the chord that produces physical response in the listener (slight tension in the chest, a heightened sense of alert waiting) precisely when Bobby is at maximum emotional heat. Harmonic and dramatic climaxes coincide. This is high Craft.

One point docked: the orchestration of the climax, in Tunick's original arrangement, is very good but slightly denser than necessary. At the peak, the orchestra is so full that Bobby's voice must push to be heard above it. The orchestra is there to support the voice; at the climax it briefly competes with the voice. This is a performance reality that Sondheim may have accepted and I might have addressed differently.

**Originality:** The song is distinctively Sondheim's — the prosody, the harmonic intelligence, the structural sophistication of the false plateau — and it makes a contribution within the musical theater tradition of using song form as dramatic architecture rather than melodic delivery vehicle. I score 13. The song does not say something the tradition had no words for; it says something the tradition could have said and says it with exceptional clarity and craft.

**Resonance:** The song achieves its effect. I have watched productions where "Being Alive" produced visible weeping in audiences who had watched Bobby for two acts, and I understand why: the song uses every technical device available to make Bobby's breakthrough feel real. The climax lands. The final quiet statement lands. The show earns its emotional resolution.

Point withheld because the show's ironic frame, which is its greatest strength, is also a slight limitation on the song's full resonance. Company has spent two acts being very smart about relationships. "Being Alive" asks us to feel what we have been watching being analyzed. The transition from watching to feeling is not fully smooth; there is a brief moment where the intellect and the emotion are slightly out of alignment before the emotion takes over. This is not a failure of the song — it may be a structural inevitability given what the show has built before it — but it costs one Resonance point.

**Economy:** Very high. The repeating lyric does three jobs economically. The false plateau earns its length. Two points docked: one for the slightly-overlength false plateau (agreeing with Sondheim on this) and one for a middle section in the verse development where the harmonic rhythm slows in a way that creates a slight marking-time effect before the next dramatic beat arrives.

The central innovation question: does the rubric's Craft dimension need dramatic function built in for musical theater? Yes. Sondheim's craft is constituted by dramatic function; a technically correct song that fails Bobby is a Craft failure by the standard that governs this work. The rubric should acknowledge this.
