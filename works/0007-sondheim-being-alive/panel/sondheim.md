# Sondheim reviews "Being Alive" (1970)

**Step 0 — Structural approach: DEVELOPMENTAL (modified AABA song form, musical-theater)**
The AABA form is modified so that each return of the A section represents a different emotional register — the same words at different temperatures. The form develops through the character's emotional position, not through thematic or harmonic transformation in the abstract. The bridge contains the false plateau that makes the final A climax achievable. Every formal element serves the dramatic function.

I will be precise, because precision is the only honest form of self-assessment.

**Structure:** The form earns itself. The modified AABA works because the A section's words are designed to carry different meanings at different temperatures — "Somebody hold me too close" is a fantasy at the opening, a demand at the climax, and a resolution at the end, and the music changes the temperature without changing the words. This is the right formal choice for this dramatic material. A through-composed song — one that moved forward without the repeating A — would not accomplish the same thing: it would lose the layering that comes from the listener hearing the same words mean something different each time.

The false plateau earns its two points of deduction. Not because it fails — it works exactly as intended — but because in retrospect I hear it as marginally longer than necessary. The moment where Bobby pulls back from the full heat is dramatically essential; the precise duration of that plateau could have been shorter by one phrase without loss. This is a small thing. But economy at the musical-theater level requires that every phrase earn its dramatic place, and one phrase at the false plateau could have been cut.

**Craft:** I will say what I think Craft means in musical theater, because I think the rubric needs to hear it stated clearly. Craft in musical theater is the achievement of a precise dramatic effect through precise musical choices. It is not the quality of the voice leading in the abstract, or the sophistication of the harmonic language in the abstract. It is whether the voice leading serves Bobby at this moment — whether the harmonic choice reveals something true about his psychology — whether the rhythmic phrasing matches the way Bobby's character would phrase these words.

By this standard, "Being Alive" achieves high Craft. The opening harmonic unresolvedness is Bobby's unresolvedness. The rising melodic intervals at the climax are Bobby's reaching. The harmonic tension at the top of the climax coincides precisely with the dramatic temperature peak. These choices are not illustrative; they are constitutive. The music does not describe what Bobby is feeling; it is what Bobby is feeling, in the medium of sound.

One point docked because there is a moment in the transition from false plateau to final climax where the harmonic movement is slightly faster than Bobby's dramatic position warrants. The music arrives at the climax half a beat before Bobby has fully committed. This is a small misalignment. In most musical theater of 1970 it would not be noticed. I notice it because I wrote it.

**Originality:** The song is original within the Sondheim catalog and within the musical theater of its moment. The layered-temperature repeating lyric is not new in principle — Hammerstein used structural repetition with meaning variation. But the specific use of it here — with the false plateau as the mechanism that makes the final variation meaningful — is a synthesis I had not used in quite this way before. The dramatic-functional Craft argument is also, to my knowledge, not stated this explicitly in any other musical theater work of the period. I score 13: genuine contribution within the tradition, without claiming to have said something the tradition had no words for at all.

**Resonance:** The song works. The intended effect — watching Bobby break open, reaching for what he has spent two acts avoiding wanting, arriving at a quiet determination that is different from his opening ironic distance — is achieved. The climax lands. The final quiet restatement of the opening lyric lands. A listener who has watched the show arrives at this song with two acts of loaded fuel, and the song uses it. I score 14. The point withheld: in the concert performance context, removed from the show, the song does not always use what it carries as efficiently, because there is no loaded fuel — the audience has not invested two acts. The song is slightly more dependent on dramatic context than I would prefer.

**Economy:** The form is economical. One lyric doing three dramatic jobs. One melody that changes its meaning by changing its temperature. The false plateau is structurally necessary; the climax is structurally necessary; the quiet resolution is structurally necessary. One point docked for the slightly-too-long false plateau. Nothing else to dock.

On the core question — does Craft in musical theater require dramatic function? Yes. A technically correct song that fails the character is a Craft failure. This is not a controversial position in musical theater; it is the foundational principle of the tradition. The question is whether the SCORE rubric captures it, and I think the answer is: not yet, not fully. The rubric's Craft dimension is written as if technical excellence is the primary criterion, with dramatic function as a secondary consideration. For musical theater, the relationship should be reversed: dramatic function is the primary criterion, and technical excellence is in service of it.
