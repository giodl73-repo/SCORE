# Work Description: "Being Alive" from Company (1970)

## The Dramatic Setup: What Two Acts Have Built

"Being Alive" arrives at the end of a show about Bobby's refusal to commit to intimacy. Company is structured as a revue of sorts — Bobby visits various married couples and observes their relationships with affectionate distance — but the thread running through every scene is the question the show never lets Bobby answer until this song: why is he still alone, and does he want to stay that way?

The genius of Furth and Sondheim's construction is that they do not frame Bobby's aloneness as a problem. Company is not a show about a lonely man learning to connect; it is a show about a man who has successfully avoided connection and has now been arranged, by two acts of theatrical exposure, into a position where the question can no longer be avoided. "Being Alive" is not the moment Bobby decides to change. It is the moment Bobby finally articulates, with full emotional heat, what he has been refusing to want.

This context is the fuel in the engine. Without it, the song's climax is a very good climax. With it, the climax carries two acts of loaded dramatic energy and produces something beyond a very good climax.

## The Musical Structure: Modified AABA

The song opens with a repeated melodic figure — descending, questioning, harmonically unresolved — set to the words "Somebody hold me too close / Somebody hurt me too deep." The melody is modal and ambiguous; it does not cadence firmly, which reflects Bobby's ambivalence. He is saying the words but not yet meaning them in his body.

The A section repeats with slight variation. Bobby is circling. The words return; the music returns. He is not there yet.

The bridge — the "B" section — is a departure. Here, Bobby begins to argue against himself, reaching the false plateau of "Alone is alone, not alive" before arriving at the crucial line "Somebody need me too much / Somebody know me too well / Somebody pull me up short / And put me through hell / And give me support / For being alive." The harmonic language at the bridge becomes more dissonant and more urgent; the melodic line rises above where it has been.

The climax is not a return to the A section in the conventional AABA sense. It is a return to the A section's words at a completely different emotional temperature. The lyric is the same; the character who sings it is not. The music rises, the dynamics build to loud, the orchestration fills the theater, and Bobby finally reaches for what he has spent two acts avoiding wanting.

The resolution — the final section — drops back to quiet and simplicity. The repeat of the opening lyric at the end is the same words a third time, but now they are not questioning. They are a statement of what Bobby wants. The temperature has passed through hot and come out the other side to something clear and resolved.

## The Lyric-Note Unity

The most technically sophisticated element of "Being Alive" is the unity between lyric content and musical gesture. Consider the climactic phrase "Somebody hold me too close." On first hearing, with the music at its lowest temperature, this is a question or a fantasy — imagined holding, not yet desired. At the climax, the music reaches upward and outward as Bobby sings it — the melodic interval opens, the harmony reaches for a chord that is not yet resolved — and the music is not illustrating the desire for holding; it is enacting the reaching-out gesture that holding is. The music holds as the lyric asks to be held.

This is not word-painting in the conventional musical sense (rising melodic lines for ascending images, falling lines for descending ones). It is something more sophisticated: the music and lyric are making the same gesture simultaneously, in different media, such that together they produce an experience neither could produce alone. A listener who reads the lyric without the music understands what Bobby wants but does not feel the reaching. A listener who hears the music without the lyric feels the emotional gesture but does not know its object. Together, they produce both knowing and feeling simultaneously.

## The False Plateau: "I'm Not Frightened"

Late in the song, there is a passage where Bobby sings something like "I'm not frightened" — the moment where the character pulls back from the full heat of the climax and briefly stabilizes at a position of apparent self-sufficiency. The music at this point settles at a false plateau: the harmonic tension releases slightly, the dynamic drops, the melody sits.

This false plateau is the most structurally sophisticated moment in the song, and the easiest to underestimate. It is not simply a dynamic contrast for its own sake. It is the dramatic moment where Bobby has almost talked himself out of what the song is building toward — where the old Bobby, the one who has successfully avoided intimacy for two acts, briefly reasserts himself. The song then must recover from this pullback and make the final climax feel more earned than it would have been without the false plateau.

The false plateau is what gives the actual climax its full weight. A song that builds continuously to a climax is predictable; the listener prepares for the peak and the peak arrives as expected. A song that pulls back before the climax forces the listener to feel the character's ambivalence and then the resolution of that ambivalence. The climax after the false plateau is not just higher dynamically; it is higher dramatically.

## Craft as Dramatic Function

Sondheim's central craft principle — that every note and every word must serve the character in the moment — is implemented throughout "Being Alive" at a level that is difficult to find in comparable musical theater of the period.

The harmonic language tracks Bobby's emotional state. The opening section's unresolved harmonies are not simply "sad" or "searching" — they are the harmonic equivalent of Bobby's position at the start: he knows the words but has not yet committed to them in his body. As the song develops and Bobby moves from awareness to desperation, the harmonic language becomes more dissonant, more urgent, more willing to use chords that create physical tension in the listener. The climax chord — the moment of maximum harmonic tension — arrives exactly when Bobby is at maximum emotional temperature. Harmonic and dramatic climax coincide.

This is what Sondheim means by lyric and music as the same argument. The harmony is Bobby's psychology, made audible.

## What Coltrane Hears

The panel will include Coltrane (20% weight), whose focus is on the spiritual urgency that makes technique irrelevant and on whether the musician — or here, the character — is genuinely searching for something the audience also needs. Coltrane's lens on "Being Alive" will be attentive to whether the song's climax achieves that quality of genuine searching, or whether it produces a technically accomplished climax that is emotionally competent without being emotionally necessary.

This is a meaningful question for a song this carefully constructed. The very care of the construction — the false plateau, the modulating A sections, the precise calibration of harmonic and dynamic buildup — could produce a climax that is profoundly moving or a climax that feels engineered. Whether Sondheim's engineering achieves genuineness or merely the appearance of genuineness is the question Coltrane will push hardest.
