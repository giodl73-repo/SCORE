# Brief: Well-Tempered Clavier, Prelude No. 1 in C Major, BWV 846 (1722)

## Subject

The Prelude No. 1 in C Major opens Book I of the Well-Tempered Clavier, Bach's comprehensive demonstration that all 24 major and minor keys, in the equal-tempered system, can bear the full formal and expressive weight of a keyboard prelude and fugue. The WTC is simultaneously a pedagogical work (demonstrating equal temperament), a compositional demonstration (proving that every key has its own character), and a philosophical argument (that the musical system itself is not arbitrary but complete).

The Prelude is approximately 35 measures. It has no melody. Every bar is one harmony, arpeggiated in a consistent broken-chord pattern: the right hand plays the upper voices of each chord in a repeated sixteenth-note figure; the left hand sustains the bass note. The harmonic sequence is complete and classical: beginning in C major, moving through the full vocabulary of tonal harmony (subdominant, dominant, secondary dominants, a moment of chromatic color in the diminished seventh at measure 23), and returning to the C major tonic with a final extended pedal point on the dominant before the conclusive tonic chord.

There is no melody in any conventional sense: no note or sequence of notes that can be sung or played separately as a tune. The melodic logic — if it exists — is harmonic: the ear follows the chord changes rather than a single melodic line. The Prelude argues, through 35 bars of evidence, that this is sufficient for complete musical expression.

This argument is a structural self-proof: the Prelude proves that harmony without melody can constitute complete musical expression by being a piece without melody that constitutes complete musical expression. The proof is the work.

## Audience

**Original audience:** Keyboard students and sophisticated amateurs who would work through the WTC as a compendium of keyboard technique and musical knowledge. For Bach's Leipzig students, the Prelude served as a demonstration of broken-chord technique and as the opening statement of the WTC's systematic argument. The Prelude's simplicity of texture made it pedagogically accessible while its harmonic richness rewarded careful attention.

**Contemporary audience:** Pianists, music students, and listeners who know the piece through centuries of performance and through Charles Gounod's addition of a sustained melody over Bach's harmony ("Ave Maria," 1853), which is either the most famous response to the Prelude or the most famous misunderstanding of it, depending on one's position. The Prelude is one of the most frequently performed and recognized pieces in the keyboard repertoire; its opening measures are among the most familiar sounds in Western music.

The Gounod Ave Maria is contextually important for this review: it proves, inadvertently, the Prelude's argument. When Gounod adds a melody, he is adding something that was already there — not missing, but present in latent form. The harmony already contains the melody; Gounod makes one of the latent melodies explicit. This supports rather than contradicts the Prelude's claim that harmony alone can constitute complete expression.

## Tradition

The keyboard prelude tradition of 1722: the prelude as an improvisatory, single-affect piece that precedes and prepares the more formal fugue. The prelude was traditionally the freer, more exploratory form — the place where the composer demonstrated keyboard idiom, harmonic imagination, and the specific character of the key. Preludes ranged from brief introductory gestures to extended character pieces.

The WTC Prelude No. 1 is unusual within the tradition in its structural simplicity: every bar is one chord, one pattern. Most preludes of the period have more textural variety, more explicit melodic content, more improvisatory freedom. The rigidity of the one-chord-per-bar pattern is an extreme formal constraint that the Prelude transforms into an expressive resource rather than a limitation.

The Prelude argues that harmony without melody can constitute complete musical expression. It demonstrates this by being a piece without melody that constitutes complete musical expression. This is the same structural self-proof category as the Crab Canon (0001) and the Beethoven Fifth (0003), but achieved through a different mechanism: not palindromic structure (0001) or developmental argument (0003), but harmonic completeness without melodic content.

## Open Questions

1. Is the absence of melody a choice or a consequence? One reading: Bach chose to write a prelude with no melody, demonstrating that harmony alone suffices. Another reading: the broken-chord texture, consistently maintained, does not produce a melody as a byproduct of its consistent pattern; the absence of melody is a consequence of the formal choice rather than a deliberate anti-melodic decision. Which reading is correct matters for how we assess the Originality of the harmonic-completeness argument.

2. What does Miles hear? Miles, whose work in Kind of Blue is precisely about the freedom of melodic invention within a tonal field, will hear the WTC Prelude as a sustained tonal field with no melodic invention — a modal stasis without the modal framework. His response may be: "Where is the melody?" Or it may be: "The harmony is the melody." The two positions produce different Resonance scores.

3. The Gounod problem. Gounod's Ave Maria adds a melody to the Prelude's harmony. Whether this is desecration or demonstration is a genuine question: does Gounod's melody prove that the Prelude needed one (and therefore fails on its own terms), or does it prove that the harmony is so complete it can support any melody (and therefore confirms the Prelude's sufficiency)?

4. C major as the tonal argument. The Prelude is in C major — the simplest key, the white-key home, the key with no accidentals. This is the correct key to open the WTC: it demonstrates that the key with the least complexity can bear the full weight of a prelude-and-fugue pair, which is the opening move of the WTC's systematic argument. The key choice is therefore structural, not arbitrary. Does this key-choice economy merit separate assessment?
