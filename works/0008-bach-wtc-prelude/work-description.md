# Work Description: WTC Prelude No. 1 in C Major, BWV 846

## The Structural Formula: One Chord per Bar

The Prelude's entire formal architecture rests on a single constraint: every bar contains exactly one harmony, and that harmony is arpeggiated in the same broken-chord pattern throughout. The right hand plays the upper voices in a repeated sixteenth-note figure (typically transcribed as five notes per group, two groups per measure); the left hand sustains the bass note. The texture is consistent from bar 1 to bar 34. Nothing changes except the harmony.

This is an extreme formal constraint. In the prelude tradition, it is unusual: most preludes have at least some variation in texture, some relief from the consistent pattern. Bach has chosen — or discovered — that the consistent pattern can bear the full harmonic weight of 35 measures without becoming monotonous, provided that the harmonic content changes with sufficient interest.

The constraint is also an economy: one decision (the arpeggio pattern) is made once and then not reconsidered. The compositional attention is freed to focus entirely on the harmonic sequence, because the textural question is settled. This is maximum harmonic focus at minimum textural complexity.

## The Harmonic Journey: 35 Bars of Evidence

The harmonic sequence of the Prelude is the Prelude. It is worth describing in some detail, because every bar is doing specific harmonic work.

**Bars 1-4: Tonic establishment.** C major, unambiguous. The opening four bars establish C major as the home key with complete clarity. Root position tonic, then first inversion, then a brief ornamental passing harmony, then tonic again. The listener is anchored.

**Bars 5-11: Harmonic motion begins.** The harmony begins to move: through the subdominant (F major), through a secondary dominant approach to the dominant, through a brief excursion to the mediant and submediant regions. The melody that does not exist is being implied: each chord change is a step in a harmonic journey that the ear follows as it would follow a melodic line.

**Bar 23: The chromatic moment.** The diminished seventh chord at bar 23 (or in some analyses, the chromatic alteration of bar 22-23) is the harmonic climax of the piece — the point of maximum tension, the furthest the harmony has traveled from the tonic. This is the Prelude's equivalent of a melodic climax: the moment of greatest harmonic intensity, which creates the maximum need for resolution.

**Bars 24-32: Return and dominant preparation.** The harmony moves back toward the tonic through a sequence of return harmonies. The harmonic journey is completed: what was departed from is returned to, the resolution prepared through a long dominant pedal point.

**Bars 32-35: The pedal point and final cadence.** Bach extends the dominant pedal point beyond what the harmonic sequence requires, sustaining a dominant-seventh sonority against the continuing arpeggio pattern before the final tonic resolution. This extension is the structural equivalent of the Beethoven Fifth's long coda: it continues not because it needs to, but because the resolution must be fully prepared before it is given. The pedal point earns the final C major chord.

## Harmony Without Melody: What the Ear Does

When you hear the WTC Prelude No. 1, you follow the harmony. This is a specific kind of listening that is different from melodic following: instead of tracking a single line that moves through time, you track the change from one harmonic state to the next, hearing each bar as a place the music is in rather than as a step the melody takes.

The harmonic journey has the same structural properties as a melodic arc: it begins (C major), it moves away from the beginning (through the harmonic sequence), it reaches a point of maximum distance (the chromatic moment), and it returns (through the dominant preparation and final cadence). The shape of the harmonic journey is the shape of the Prelude. Without melody, the shape is fully present.

This is the Prelude's argument: harmonic shape is sufficient for musical expression. You do not need a melody to follow music in time. You can follow harmony. The piece demonstrates this by being the demonstration.

## The Gounod Corollary

In 1853, Charles Gounod wrote "Ave Maria" by adding a sustained melody over the Prelude's harmony. The result became one of the most popular pieces of the nineteenth century. The question it raises for the Prelude is unavoidable: does Gounod's melody fill a gap in the Prelude, or does it add something that the Prelude did not need?

The panel's reading — which the brief anticipates and the reviewers will confirm or complicate — is that Gounod's melody is latent in the Prelude's harmony. The harmonic progression already implies a melodic arc; what Gounod does is make one version of that arc explicit. The Prelude already contains the melody; Gounod chooses to sing it. This is confirmation of the Prelude's harmonic completeness, not exposure of its melodic deficit.

If this reading is correct, the Prelude's argument is even stronger than it appears: a piece without melody is so harmonically complete that any melody can be added to it, not because it is harmonically neutral, but because it is harmonically complete enough to support melodic content it does not require.

## Economy: The Prelude's Length and the Pedal Point

The Prelude is 35 bars. For a piece with no melody and one textural idea, this might seem long. It is not long. The 35 bars are exactly the minimum required for the harmonic journey to complete: the establishment, the departure, the chromatic moment, the return, and the pedal-point preparation of the final cadence.

The pedal point (bars 32-35) is the most potentially excessive element — the place where a less confident composer might have been shorter. Bach extends the dominant pedal three full bars beyond the point where the harmonic resolution could have arrived. This is deliberate: the pedal point earns the final C major chord by making the dominant's resolution feel earned rather than given. Three bars of dominant tension produces a C major chord that has been fully deserved. The length is Economy at its most exact: exactly as long as the resolution requires.

## What the WTC Prelude Opens

The Prelude opens the WTC, which means it is the first statement of the WTC's systematic argument: that all 24 keys are equally capable of bearing musical weight. Opening with C major — the simplest key, the white-key home — is the correct move: it demonstrates that the most familiar, least exotic key can sustain a complete musical statement, which prepares the listener for the claim that every key, including the most remote, can do the same. The C major Prelude is the WTC's opening argument. It does not need to be extraordinary; it needs to be complete. It is complete.
