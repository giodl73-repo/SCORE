## The Hindustani Tradition

Hindustani classical music is the North Indian classical tradition, one of the two major classical traditions of the Indian subcontinent (the other being Carnatic, the South Indian tradition). The Hindustani tradition is organized around the raga — a melodic framework with specific pitches, characteristic phrases, and performance conventions — and the tala — a rhythmic cycle that organizes the metered sections of a performance.

A raga is not a scale or a mode, though it has scalar properties. It is closer to a personality or a grammar: a set of notes with rules about how those notes must be approached and departed, which notes should be emphasized, which should be avoided, what phrases are characteristic of the raga's identity, and what time of day and emotional state the raga is appropriate for. A raga is also a living tradition: its canonical performances, its great practitioners, and its relationship to the seasons, the hours, and the emotional states it governs constitute a body of knowledge transmitted between generations of musicians.

## Raga Yaman

Yaman (also known as Kalyan) is one of the most foundational ragas in the Hindustani tradition. It belongs to the Kalyan thaat (the Kalyan parent scale), which uses all seven notes with a raised fourth (Ga-Ma#, or a Lydian raised fourth in Western terms). The raga is associated with the first hours of evening — specifically the early-evening period, as the light changes from day to night — and with a quality of longing, romantic beauty, and expansive feeling.

**Scale:** Sa Re Ga Ma# Pa Dha Ni Sa (equivalent to: C D E F# G A B C, a Lydian mode)

**Characteristic phrases:**
- The ascent emphasizes Ga-Ma#-Pa-Ni (the raised fourth is highlighted in ascent)
- The descent uses all seven notes but dwells on Ni-Dha-Pa (the upper register notes)
- Characteristic ornament: a meend (glissando) from Ga to Ma# — the slide into the raised fourth
- The note Ma# (F#, the raised fourth) is the raga's defining note: its prominence in the ascent identifies the raga immediately

**Avoidance:** Ma (the natural fourth, F natural) is omitted. The absence of the natural fourth and the presence of the raised fourth is what distinguishes Yaman from other ragas using similar pitch collections.

## Structure of a Raga Yaman Performance

### Alap (Unmetered Exploration)
The alap of Raga Yaman begins in the lowest register, establishing the drone foundation (Sa, the tonic) and slowly introducing the raga's characteristic notes one by one. The performer reveals the raga's identity through the relationship between notes — through the meend from Ga to Ma#, through the emphasis on Ni in the upper register, through the particular way the descending phrase from Ni through Dha-Pa-Ga arrives at the tonic.

The alap has its own structure: the performer moves from the lower register (mandal saptak) through the middle register (madhya saptak) to the upper register (taar saptak), revealing the raga's emotional character at each level. There is no rush. A great alap of Raga Yaman can last twenty minutes or more; in each minute, the raga's personality is deepened rather than merely continued.

The alap is improvised, but improvisation in this context does not mean "freely invented." It means "realized from a grammar" — the raga's grammar determines which choices are available, and the performer's wisdom determines which available choices to make at each moment. A wrong choice — a phrase that contradicts the raga's grammar — is not merely a technical error; it is a misrepresentation of the raga's identity.

### Jor
The alap develops internal rhythmic regularity — the performer's phrases begin to pulse with a consistent beat — without the arrival of the tabla. The jor bridges the unmetered alap and the metered gat.

### Gat + Improvisation
When the tabla enters, the performance moves into metered time. A composed gat (a short melodic composition) is stated and becomes the frame for improvisation. The improvisations — taans (fast runs), bol-taans (rhythmic patterns), and other forms — must all return to the gat as their structural anchor. Improvisation here is structured by both the raga's melodic grammar and the tala's rhythmic cycle.

## Ravi Shankar

Ravi Shankar (1920-2012) was one of the most celebrated sitarists in the Hindustani tradition and the primary figure responsible for introducing Indian classical music to Western audiences. He studied with his guru Allauddin Khan for many years, living in his household as a disciple (a shishya) in the traditional gurukul system. His playing is distinguished by the depth of his alap, the rhythmic sophistication of his gat improvisations, and the extraordinary singing quality of his phrase-making.

His collaborations with George Harrison introduced the sitar and Indian classical music to popular-music audiences in the 1960s. His friendship with Coltrane, with whom he shared a deep mutual respect, connected the Hindustani tradition to the American jazz avant-garde's spiritual searching.
