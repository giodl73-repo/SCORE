## Step 0 — Structural Approach

**MODAL/STATIC with DEVELOPMENTAL sections**

Historically, the raga form has developed over approximately two millennia in the Indian subcontinent. The current structure — alap, jor, jhala, gat — is a product of the Mughal period's interaction with older Vedic musical traditions, systematized in the gharana (school) system from roughly the 18th century onward. Shankar's performance inherits this historical structure while demonstrating its living character.

## Review

I want to place this performance in two historical contexts simultaneously: the history of the Hindustani tradition and the history of Western reception of Indian classical music.

**Within the Hindustani tradition:**

Raga Yaman (Kalyan) is one of the oldest and most established ragas in the Hindustani system. It appears in treatises from the medieval period and has been performed by every major gharana. Its canonical status within the tradition means that a great performance of Yaman is assessed against centuries of accumulated performance wisdom — the standard includes not just what was played but what every great player before has played.

Shankar studied with Allauddin Khan, the founder of the Maihar gharana, in the traditional gurukul system — living as a disciple in the master's household for years. This training placed Shankar in a continuous transmission line from the great masters of the 19th century. His performance of Raga Yaman is assessed within this tradition as a living continuation of that transmission.

What distinguishes Shankar historically: he was the first major Hindustani classical musician to develop a substantial international audience without compromising the tradition's standards. Many musicians who introduced Indian classical music to Western audiences did so by simplifying — shortening the alap, adapting to Western concert contexts, limiting the tala complexity. Shankar maintained the full traditional form while presenting it to audiences who had no prior experience of it. This is a historical achievement of a specific kind: expanding the tradition's audience without contracting its depth.

**Western reception: 1960s-1980s:**

The historical context of Shankar's canonical performances for Western audiences includes the following significant moments:

1. The 1955-1965 period of growing interest in world music in American and European circles
2. The Shankar-Coltrane connection (Coltrane studied briefly with Shankar, and the influence is present in Coltrane's modal explorations and spiritual searching)
3. The Shankar-Harrison collaboration (1966-1974), which introduced the sitar to popular music audiences
4. The 1971 Concert for Bangladesh, which placed Indian classical music in the context of political activism

In each of these contexts, Shankar maintained the integrity of the raga performance while engaging with the Western audiences and institutions that had no prior framework for it.

**The rubric's applicability:**

From a historical perspective, the most significant question this work raises is whether the SCORE rubric — developed initially for Western music — can assess non-Western music without imposing Western criteria. The historical evidence is that Western attempts to assess non-Western music have consistently distorted it by applying the wrong standards.

The appropriate response: assess each dimension against the tradition's own criteria, explicitly stated. The Craft dimension requires the most significant adjustment; the Economy, Resonance, and Structure dimensions are more readily interpretable in tradition-specific terms if the tradition's grammar is understood.
