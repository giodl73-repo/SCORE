## Review

What I want to talk about is economy.

The alap. Single note. Drone. Then one note of the melody. Then silence. Then another note.

This is the most economical music I have heard. Not economical in the sense of brief — a great alap can last thirty minutes. Economical in the sense that nothing is played before it must be played. The musician is not filling time. The musician is waiting for the right moment and then playing exactly what the right moment requires.

This is what I tried to teach my musicians. "Don't play what you know." What I meant was: don't reach for the prepared phrase because you need to say something. Wait until the music is ready to be said. If the silence is right, leave it as silence.

Shankar doesn't fill silence. He inhabits it. The space between phrases in the alap is not empty space — it is the previous phrase still resonating, still being heard, still being received by the listener. The next phrase doesn't arrive until the previous one is finished doing its work. That is economy.

I also want to talk about one note. The meend from Ga to Ma# — the glissando that arrives at the raised fourth, the note that identifies Yaman. This arrives once per phrase cycle, in a specific position, after specific preparation. When it arrives, it says everything the raga says. Not because of what it is — it's a slide between two notes — but because of where it arrives, when it arrives, after what preparation. One gesture, exactly placed, doing the entire work of establishing the raga's identity. That is a note worth its place.

On the gat: the tabla brings the rhythm in. The improvisation begins. Now we're in rhythmic space, and the economy question changes. In rhythmic space, economy means: does each phrase serve the tala (the rhythmic cycle)? Does the improviser play with the cycle and against it without losing it? Does the return to the gat arrive where it should? I hear Shankar navigate the rhythmic space with the same patient economy he brings to the alap. He doesn't rush the cycle. He arrives on the beat he intends to arrive on.

On Craft: I'll say what Pärt and Trane will say more completely — the Craft here is not our Craft. The vocabulary is different. But I recognize the discipline. The economy is discipline. The patience is discipline. The decision not to play is discipline. I can score this as high Craft even without the vocabulary for what the specific techniques are, because the discipline that produced the economy is Craft in any tradition.

One thing I find most interesting: the alap has no written score. There is no notation of what the alap will be. The performer knows the raga's grammar; the listener knows the raga's grammar; the performance is the meeting of two knowledges in real time. The alap is architecture discovered in the moment of building it. This is improvised structure — Innovation #12 (Real-Time Architecture from the founding sprint, work 0005) — but more structurally constrained than jazz improvisation. The jazz improviser navigates changes; the raga performer navigates a grammar. Both create real-time architecture. The raga grammar is stricter; the modal field is deeper.
