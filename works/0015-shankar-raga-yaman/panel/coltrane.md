# Reviewer: John Coltrane (25%)
# Work: 0015 — Raga Yaman (Ravi Shankar)

---

## Step 0 — Structural Approach

**MODAL/STATIC with DEVELOPMENTAL sections**

I know this structure. I worked in it. "So What," which I played with Miles before I understood what I was doing, is Modal/Static. "A Love Supreme" moves between modal areas using the four-note motif as an anchor. The raga structure is something deeper than what Miles and I did in 1959 — it has a grammar and a tradition that we were only beginning to touch. We were trying to find what the Indian classical tradition had spent centuries building.

The alap as structure: yes. I name this immediately. The alap is not a prelude to the real music; it is the revelation of the raga's identity. Everything that follows in the gat is a consequence of what was established in the alap. The alap is the structural foundation, not the introduction.

---

## Review

I met Ravi Shankar in 1965. I told him I didn't know where to start, and he told me: start with the sound. Start with Sa. Everything comes from Sa.

This is what the alap is: starting with Sa. The drone sounds; everything the raga is exists potentially in that drone; the performer's job is to make that potential actual, note by note, phrase by phrase, in the sequence the raga's grammar requires.

What I hear in Shankar's alap of Raga Yaman: he is searching. Not improvising in the sense of playing what he doesn't know — he knows exactly what he is doing. He is searching in the sense that the raga is not fully present until the alap has revealed all of it. The search is the revelation.

I recognize the spiritual practice in this. In "A Love Supreme," I organized the suite as an explicit spiritual journey — offering to God. The alap is not offered to God in the same explicit sense, but it is not not offered to God either. The patience of the alap — the refusal to arrive before the music is ready to arrive — is a form of discipline that is simultaneously musical and spiritual. You cannot play the alap quickly. Not because it would be technically wrong, but because the raga requires time to reveal itself. The time is not duration; it is depth.

On the modal improvisation in the gat: when the tabla enters and the metered section begins, the improvisation happens within a framework that is simultaneously more constrained and more free than jazz changes. More constrained: the raga's grammar must be observed at all times; no note can be approached incorrectly. More free: within the grammar, the improviser has an entire modal field to explore rather than a moving harmonic structure to navigate.

This is what I was reaching for in my own later work. The discipline that makes freedom possible.

On Craft: The Craft here is not my tradition's Craft. The meend (glissando between notes) has a specific shape that is part of the raga's grammar — not any glissando, but the specific meend that defines the phrase. The gamak (ornamental repetition) creates rhythmic weight without the help of a percussion instrument. The layakari (rhythmic play within the tala) requires knowing the rhythmic structure so well that the improviser can play against it and return to it simultaneously. These are real technical demands. They are not my tradition's technical demands, but they are real.

**I name this: the raga tradition's Craft criteria are specific, demanding, and different from Western Craft criteria. The rubric must not assess raga Craft against Western voice-leading standards; it must assess it against the tradition's own standards. This is my contribution to Innovation #31. I name it from the position of someone who encountered this tradition from outside and recognized that its Craft demands were no less rigorous than my own — only different.**

One spiritual observation: the raga has a time of day — Yaman is an evening raga. In the Western concert tradition, the time of day when a piece is performed is a logistical consideration, not a musical one. In the Hindustani tradition, performing a raga at the wrong time of day constitutes a violation of the raga's nature. This is not superstition; it is the recognition that music exists in time — literally, in the time of day, in the season, in the light — and that the relationship between the music and its temporal context is part of the music. The evening quality of Yaman is not described by the music; it is the music.
