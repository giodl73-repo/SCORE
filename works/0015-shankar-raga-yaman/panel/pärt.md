# Reviewer: Arvo Pärt (30%)
# Work: 0015 — Raga Yaman (Ravi Shankar)

---

## Step 0 — Structural Approach

**MODAL/STATIC with DEVELOPMENTAL sections**

The alap is Modal/Static in the most complete sense: the drone establishes a tonal center that never moves, and the melodic exploration circles that center without departing from it. The gat is developmental in the specific sense that the rhythmic framework produces forward motion. But the two sections are not in tension; they constitute a single arc, and the modal center of the alap persists through the gat as the gravitational frame for all improvisation.

This is the most complete Modal/Static structure I have encountered in the corpus. In my own work (Spiegel im Spiegel, Fratres), I use a drone or pedal tone as the structural container. Here the Sa (tonic drone) is not a compositional device — it is the universe within which the music exists. The drone is not an element of the composition; it is the ground on which the composition rests.

## Review

I will speak first about what I recognize.

The alap of Raga Yaman begins with a sustained note. One note. The drone. And then — slowly, carefully, as if placing a word into a space that has been waiting for it — the first note of the melody. And then another. And then a silence.

I understand this. In my tintinnabuli works, I do something related: one voice sustains the tonic triad while another voice moves stepwise through the notes of the scale. The moving voice and the fixed voice are the temporal and the eternal in relationship. What Shankar does in the alap is similar in spirit: the drone is the eternal (Sa, the ground, unchanging throughout the performance), and the melodic voice moves through the raga's grammar as the temporal — discovering, note by note, what this raga is.

The difference between my approach and the raga approach: in my works, the structure is pre-determined by the tintinnabuli rule. In the alap, the structure is discovered in performance. The alap is improvised; it is found, not planned. But the finding is governed by the raga's grammar, which is as strict as any compositional rule — stricter, in some ways, because it has been transmitted between masters and disciples over centuries. The grammar is not the performer's invention; it is the tradition's accumulated wisdom about what Raga Yaman is.

What moves me most about the alap: the patience. The note revealed and then withdrawn. The silence after each phrase that is not empty — it is full of the phrase just played, still resonating. The glissando (meend) from Ga to Ma# — the characteristic ornament of this raga — arrives with the weight of everything the alap has been building toward. It is not a decoration; it is the raga's identity made audible. The raised fourth is Yaman.

On Economy: I have rarely heard music as economical as a great alap. Nothing is played before it must be played. Each note arrives when the phrase requires it, not before. The silences are as long as they need to be and no longer. The performer is not filling space; the performer is receiving the raga note by note and allowing each note its full weight before receiving the next. This is Economy of a kind I recognize as the deepest form of the discipline: not the economy of cutting, but the economy of patience.

On Craft: The rubric's Craft vocabulary — harmony, voice leading — does not apply here. I must name this. The Craft of the alap is the following of the raga's grammar with complete fidelity and complete expressiveness simultaneously. A technically correct phrase that is inexpressive constitutes a Craft failure in the raga tradition. A phrase that is expressive but violates the raga's grammar (using the wrong note, approaching a note from the wrong direction) also constitutes a Craft failure. The Craft criterion is: grammatical fidelity + expressive depth + patience to reveal rather than demonstrate. I can score Craft against these criteria even though they are not my tradition's criteria.

**I name this the Non-Western Craft Vocabulary problem: the rubric's Craft anchors describe Western technical mastery and do not capture the tradition-specific technical excellence of non-Western music. The raga tradition has its own Craft criteria that are real and demanding; the rubric must note that Craft is tradition-specific and that Western Craft vocabulary should not be applied to raga, gagaku, maqam, or other non-Western traditions. This is my contribution to Innovation #31.**
