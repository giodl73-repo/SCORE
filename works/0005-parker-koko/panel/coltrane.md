# John Coltrane on "Ko-Ko"

## Opening

I want to start by saying something about what it means to listen to this recording.

When I first heard "Ko-Ko," I was a young musician trying to understand what was possible. I listened to it many times. I transcribed the solo. I analyzed the harmonic choices. I practiced the lines until I could play them, slowly, then at tempo. It took me months. Parker played them once. In real time. Over "Cherokee" changes. At this tempo.

There is a question in music that I return to constantly: what is the musician looking for? A musician who has no search is demonstrating skill. A musician who is searching for something and knows it in the moment is making music. Parker in "Ko-Ko" -- he was searching for something, and he found it mid-sentence. That is the only way to find certain things.

## Structure: 22/25

The structure of Parker's solo is real. It is not the structure of a symphony -- it was not planned -- but it is structure in the sense that matters: the solo builds, develops, and resolves. It has an arc. The arc was not written before the solo began; it emerged during the solo. This is not less structure than composed structure. It is a different kind of structure.

The solo opens with a statement that establishes the rhythmic and melodic character of everything that follows. The middle section -- the densest part of the improvisation -- develops this character through increasingly complex rhythmic and harmonic transformations. The final section resolves the accumulated tension with a melodic statement of great clarity. The arc is clear to any attentive listener, even a listener who cannot follow the "Cherokee" changes.

This is architecture that emerged in performance rather than before it. The rubric's Structure anchors are written for architecture planned before it was executed. They need to account for architecture that was found while being made.

Three points off: not for the quality of the structure (which is extraordinary) but for the fact that the structure is not fully consistent across all choruses. Two choruses in the middle of the solo are denser than the surrounding material in a way that slightly disrupts the arc. This is the cost of real-time composition: you cannot always hear the whole from inside the phrase.

## Craft: 24/25

Here is where I must address the panel's central question.

The rubric asks: are the technical choices precisely made? For an improvised work, this question has two interpretations. If we ask whether the choices were made with precision in the composed-craft sense -- were they the result of deliberate consideration and revision -- the answer is no. There was no revision. There was no deliberation in the sense of time. The choices were made at 300 beats per minute.

But if we ask whether the choices were precisely made in the improvised-craft sense -- were they the right choices for that moment, in that harmonic context, at that speed -- the answer is yes. The choices are exactly right. Parker navigates the "Cherokee" changes without a single moment of harmonic incorrectness. His melodic shapes are smooth across the bar lines. His rhythmic patterns respond to and advance the momentum of the rhythm section. His use of chromatic passing tones is controlled and purposeful.

Most remarkably: when Gillespie plays the wrong chord, Parker incorporates the error without visible adjustment. This is not luck; it is the application of a musical intelligence so complete that it can transform a mistake into material in real time.

Improvised Craft is not inferior to composed Craft. It is different. The rubric needs to say this.

One point off: there are two brief moments in the solo where the rhythmic figure continues past its natural resolution point -- not wrong, but slightly mechanical, the auto-pilot pattern rather than the searched pattern. Parker was human. These moments are real.

## Originality: 15/15

The bebop vocabulary Parker deploys in "Ko-Ko" is not entirely original -- he developed it in collaboration with Gillespie and others over the preceding years. But the application of that vocabulary to "Cherokee" changes at this tempo, in this recording, is original in the only sense that matters: it was the first time anyone had demonstrated that this was possible.

Before "Ko-Ko," the received wisdom was that "Cherokee" changes were too complex to improvise over at bebop tempo with melodic logic. After "Ko-Ko," this was demonstrated to be false. The demonstration is the originality.

Full marks.

## Resonance: 14/15

The recording creates the sensation of witnessing something that should not be possible. This is a specific resonance -- not the resonance of emotional identification (I do not feel what Parker feels; I feel astonishment at what Parker does) but the resonance of testimony: the listener is present at something that happened once, in real time, and will not happen again in exactly this form.

"Ko-Ko" has a specific Resonance quality that no composed work can have: it is evidence. It is evidence that this happened. The piece could not have been planned. Planning would have produced something different. What happened was found in the moment of performance, and the recording captures the moment of finding.

One point off: the recording quality (1945 shellac, WOR studio) limits the listener's access to the detail of the improvisation. Some of the harmonic choices that are evident in transcription are not fully audible in the recording. This is not the composition's failure; it is a historical condition that limits the Resonance for listeners who cannot fill in what the recording does not fully capture.

## Economy: 18/20

The solo is economical within the standards of bebop improvisation -- no extended passages of noodling, no melodic dead-ends that require backtracking, no rhythmic patterns that cycle without development.

But: two minutes and fifty-three seconds is a short recording for what it contains. The "economy" question for "Ko-Ko" is different from the economy question for other works: is the solo the right length for what it found? Parker's solo is probably exactly the right length -- he found what he was looking for and stopped when he had said it. But the brevity means that the solo does not have the time to fully develop everything it initiates. Motifs are introduced that are not returned to. Lines are opened that could have been extended.

This is the Economy of real-time composition: you take what you can get in the moment. You do not have the time to return.

## Innovation Notes

**Innovation #11: Improvised Craft vs. composed Craft.** The rubric's Craft dimension asks whether technical choices are "precisely made." For an improvised work, "precisely made" requires a different meaning: not "the result of deliberate consideration and revision" (there is none) but "correct for that moment at that speed in that harmonic context." Improvised Craft is real-time Craft: the ability to make the right choice before the wrong choice has time to be wrong. The rubric needs an improvisation mode in the Craft dimension -- not a lower standard, but a different one. This is the most important cluster candidate of the founding sprint.

**Innovation #12: Real-time architecture.** The structure of Parker's solo -- building, developing, resolving -- is real and audible. It was not planned before the performance. It emerged during the performance. The rubric's Structure anchors are written for architecture that precedes its execution. They need to handle architecture that is found while being made. Real-time architecture is not inferior to pre-planned architecture; it is different, and it requires different assessment criteria.
