# Music Theorist on "Ko-Ko"

## Formal Analysis

"Ko-Ko" presents specific technical content that the other panel members have noted but not fully analyzed. I will address the harmonic, rhythmic, and motivic content of Parker's improvisation.

## The "Cherokee" Changes: Technical Summary

"Cherokee" (Ray Noble, 1938) is a 32-bar AABA form. The A sections are in Bb major, standard ii-V-I based. The bridge (B section) is the challenging material:

- 8 bars: B major (ii-V-I in B major)
- 4 bars: Bb major (ii-V-I return)
- 4 bars: Ab major (ii-V-I in Ab major)
- 4 bars: G major (ii-V-I in G major)
- 8 bars: C major (ii-V-I in C, leading back to Bb)

The bridge moves through five key centers in sixteen bars, each approached through its dominant. At 300 BPM, each two-bar ii-V-I occupies approximately 1.6 seconds. The improviser must hear each harmonic change, select appropriate pitches, and execute them in real time.

## Parker's Harmonic Choices: Technical Analysis

Parker's approach to the "Cherokee" changes involves three distinct techniques:

**1. Chord-tone improvisation.** In the A sections, Parker frequently emphasizes the 3rd, 5th, and 7th of each chord. This is the bebop harmonic vocabulary's basic level: identify the chord, play its characteristic tones. Parker deploys this as the foundation of his lines, not as the whole.

**2. Chromatic approach tones.** Parker surrounds chord tones with chromatic neighbors -- a half-step below or above the target tone, approached and left. This is the bebop chromaticism that distinguishes it from swing improvisation: the chromatic notes do not belong to the chord, but they create a sense of direction toward the chord tone they surround. Used correctly, they produce a feeling of harmonic richness without harmonic confusion.

**3. Substitution and reharmonization.** In several passages, Parker implies chord substitutions that are not present in the original changes. Most notably, he uses the tritone substitution (replacing a dominant seventh chord with the dominant seventh a tritone away) to create chromatic melodic motion through otherwise disjunct harmonic territory. In the bridge, where the key centers are moving rapidly, the tritone substitution smooths the transitions by creating linear motion between the keys.

## The Gillespie Error: Technical Analysis

The moment where Gillespie plays the wrong chord can be precisely located. Gillespie plays what appears to be a chord from a different location in the form -- a moment of position confusion. Parker's response is technically remarkable: he plays a melodic figure that functions correctly over BOTH the intended chord AND Gillespie's erroneous chord. The figure uses chromatic approach tones that are harmonically neutral -- they do not strongly imply either harmonic reading, and they resolve to a chord tone that is correct in both contexts.

This is not luck. This is the highest application of the chromatic approach-tone technique: using harmonic neutrality strategically to bridge between intended and actual harmony. Parker understood the technique well enough to deploy it at speed in an emergency.

## Motivic Development in Improvisation

Parker's solo exhibits motivic development that is audible in transcription and was generated in real time. Three specific motivic events are identifiable:

1. An ascending chromatic figure in the second bar of the first chorus that returns (transformed) in the third chorus

3. A specifically "Parker-esque" turn figure (a quick ornament on the 3rd of a chord) that appears at structural moments in the solo

None of these recurrences were pre-planned. The motor system, trained to these patterns, reproduced them in appropriate harmonic contexts at appropriate structural moments. The result, in transcription, looks like composed motivic development.

## Innovation Note

The Craft assessment of this improvisation required applying bebop-tradition criteria, not Classical sonata criteria. This is consistent with the rubric's requirement to assess Craft within the tradition's grammar. But the rubric's Craft anchors are written for composed music -- they describe qualities of notation and conscious compositional choice. An improvisation mode needs to specify: for improvised works, Craft is assessed on (a) the precision of real-time harmonic navigation, (b) the quality of chromatic and rhythmic choices given the tempo and harmonic context, and (c) the degree to which melodic logic is maintained under the pressure of real-time execution. These criteria are the improvised-tradition equivalents of the composed criteria the rubric currently specifies.
