# The Performer on "Ko-Ko"

## Focus

I want to address the question no other reviewer has fully answered yet: what does it mean to "perform" an improvisation?

When a performer plays a composed work, the relationship is clear. The score exists; it specifies the notes, the rhythms, the dynamics, the phrasing. The performer's job is to realize the score -- to bring the notation to sound in a way that serves the music's intentions. The craft of the performer is the craft of interpretation: choosing between the available readings of the notation and finding the one that best serves the work.

When Parker improvised "Ko-Ko," there was no score to realize. The performer IS the composer at every moment. Every choice -- pitch, rhythm, duration, phrasing, dynamics -- is made in real time, without the possibility of reconsideration, in the context of a rhythm section playing simultaneously that requires Parker's contribution to be coordinated with their own.

This is a different act from performance in the traditional sense. It is composition-in-performance. And it requires a different kind of craft assessment.

## Real-Time Craft Assessment

The craft of improvised performance has three components that do not have clear equivalents in composed-performance craft:

**1. Fingering and physical execution at tempo.** Parker's improvisation at 300 BPM requires motor execution that is not consciously controlled in the sense that a pianist practicing a passage consciously controls fingering. The motor system must be trained to execute at this speed automatically. The choices of fingering -- which keys are depressed in what sequence -- are not consciously made during the performance; they are the output of years of training that has built patterns in the motor cortex. Craft in this sense is not the deliberate choice of fingering but the training that makes automatic execution possible.

**2. Harmonic navigation in real time.** The "Cherokee" changes move through multiple key centers in the bridge. A performer improvising over this must hear the harmonic movement and select pitches consistent with it, in real time, before the chord has fully sounded. This requires internalization of the harmonic vocabulary at a level that makes the selection automatic -- the performer does not consciously think "this is a Bb7 chord; I should emphasize the b7 and #9"; the motor system produces that emphasis without conscious direction.

**3. Melodic logic under pressure.** Parker's solo has melodic logic -- phrases set up and resolve, motifs are introduced and returned to, the arc builds over multiple choruses. This logic was not pre-planned. It was found in real time, while executing the first two components. The craft of maintaining melodic logic under the pressure of harmonic navigation at motor-execution speed is the highest component of improvised craft. This is what separates Parker from technically proficient but musically empty improvisers.

## The Gillespie Error as Craft Evidence

The moment Gillespie plays the wrong chord is the best available evidence of Parker's third-component craft. A musician with excellent motor execution and harmonic navigation but limited melodic logic would have stumbled -- the wrong chord would have disrupted the motor pattern and produced a visible glitch. Parker's melodic logic was strong enough to absorb the disruption and redirect it. He did not stumble; he pivoted.

This is what improvised Craft looks like at its highest: not the absence of unexpected inputs, but the capacity to incorporate unexpected inputs into the musical argument without disruption. The rubric's Craft anchors (25: "the technical choices are exactly right -- voice leading is smooth and purposeful, harmonic language is consistent and expressive") describe composed-Craft. They do not describe this.

## Performability Assessment

The head of "Ko-Ko" is performable as notated -- it is demanding but achievable by a bebop-trained musician with sufficient preparation. The improvised section is, by definition, not "notated" and therefore not assessable for performability in the traditional sense.

The transcription of Parker's solo is performable by an advanced musician -- the harmonic choices are correct, the rhythmic patterns can be reproduced with sufficient practice. But reproducing the transcription is a different act from the original improvisation. The original was found in real time; the reproduction is memorized. Both are performable. They are not the same act.

## Innovation Note

The Craft dimension needs an explicit improvisation mode. The current anchors assess composed Craft -- they describe the qualities of notation and conscious compositional choices. An improvisation mode would assess real-time Craft: motor execution at tempo, harmonic navigation without deliberation, and melodic logic under pressure. Beethoven has correctly identified the gap; this note provides the specific technical criteria that the mode needs. The founding cluster of improvised music assessment requires this as its first element.
