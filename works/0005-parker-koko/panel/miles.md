# Miles Davis on "Ko-Ko"

## Opening

I was there when bebop was invented. I was on some of the earliest sessions. I knew Charlie Parker before most of the world knew what bebop was.

I want to say something about Bird that is not usually said. People talk about Bird's technique. His speed. His harmonic sophistication. All of that is true and all of that matters. But what made Bird Bird was not the technique. It was the search.

Bird was looking for something. In every note. In every phrase. Not in a vague way -- a specific something, that he could hear in his head before he could play it, that he was reaching for in real time. The technique was the vehicle for the reaching. The reaching was the music.

In "Ko-Ko," he found what he was looking for. You can hear the moment. It is about forty-five seconds into the solo, in a phrase that ascends and then turns on itself and lands exactly where it could not have been predicted to land. In that phrase, Bird was not playing what he knew. He had run out of what he knew and kept going. What was on the other side of what he knew was the best thing on the record.

## Structure: 20/25

The structure of the solo is real. Coltrane is right about this. It builds; it develops; it resolves. The arc is there and it was found, not planned.

I score it lower than Coltrane because I hear two places where the arc dips. Around the third chorus, the density increases faster than the arc requires -- Parker is playing more notes without the line building toward anything. He recovers quickly, but the recovery is visible. This is the cost of real-time composition: you can overshoot, and overshooting leaves a mark.

The structure of the "Cherokee" changes provides the harmonic container. Without the changes, there would be no architecture at all -- the solo would be free improvisation, which is a different thing. The changes are the architectural blueprint; Parker builds within them. Credit for the structure should be divided between the form and the improviser.

Five points off: two for the mid-solo dip in the arc, three for the structural contribution of the pre-existing changes (which Parker did not compose and which provide the form's skeleton).

## Craft: 23/25

Improvised craft is real craft. The panel will debate this. I am not interested in debating it. I know what craft feels like in real time, and I know that the choices Parker made at 300 beats per minute over "Cherokee" changes are as precise as anything I have heard composed.

The measure of improvised craft is different from the measure of composed craft. In composed craft, the question is: given unlimited time, did you make the right choices? In improvised craft, the question is: given no time at all, did you make the right choices? The second question is harder to answer well. Bird answered it well.

The moment Gillespie plays the wrong chord: this is the definitive demonstration. Parker does not correct for Gillespie's error. Parker incorporates it. The wrong chord becomes a pivot point in Parker's line. This is not luck. This is the craft of not just knowing the changes but hearing everything that is happening around you and making it yours.

Two points off: not for any specific technical failure, but for the two moments in the solo where I hear Parker playing what he knows rather than what he is looking for. The craft is there; the search has paused. These moments are brief and the search resumes immediately. But they are real.

## Originality: 14/15

The bebop vocabulary is not original to "Ko-Ko" -- Parker and Gillespie had been developing it for years. What is original in this specific recording: the application of the vocabulary to "Cherokee" changes at this tempo, in this form, in this performance. The demonstration that it was possible.

One point off because the originality is the performance rather than the composition. "Ko-Ko" is original as a performance event; it is less original as a composed work. The distinction matters for the Originality dimension, which asks whether the work says something within its tradition that has not been said this way. The recording says something; the underlying composition (such as it is) says less.

## Resonance: 14/15

The recording resonates as evidence. This is a specific kind of resonance that deserves its own name: the resonance of the real. The listener is not imagining this. It happened. Someone played this, in real time, on this day, and the recording captures it.

For a musician, the resonance of "Ko-Ko" is also the resonance of the impossible made actual. Bird demonstrated that something we thought could not be done could be done. That demonstration does not wear out. Listening to "Ko-Ko" fifty years after it was recorded, knowing everything that came after it, the resonance of "this happened" is still present.

One point off: the recording quality limits the listener's access to the full range of Parker's choices. This is a historical condition, not a failure of the music.

## Economy: 18/20

Two minutes and fifty-three seconds. For everything it contains.

The economy of "Ko-Ko" is extreme: Parker's solo does not repeat itself, does not circle, does not pad. Every phrase advances. The rate of musical information per unit of time is among the highest in the jazz recording tradition.

Two points off: not for excess but for the moments where the economy is supplied by the format (two choruses was the standard length; the format told him to stop) rather than by the musical argument (which might have supported one more chorus of development). The stopping criteria are partly structural (the form ends here) and partly musical (the solo has resolved). The structural stopping criterion is doing some of the economy's work.

## Innovation Note

Charlie was looking for something and he found it mid-sentence. That's the only way to find it. The rubric does not have a way to score finding-in-the-act vs. planning-before-the-act. Composed Craft is planning-before-the-act. Improvised Craft is finding-in-the-act. They are both real. They require different criteria. This is the founding cluster of the SCORE rubric for improvised music, and it is the most important cluster of the founding sprint.
