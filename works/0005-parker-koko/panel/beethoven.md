# Ludwig van Beethoven on "Ko-Ko"

## Opening

I must be honest with this panel about the difficulty of what I am being asked to do.

I am being asked to score a piece of music whose primary content -- the improvised solo -- was not written. It was performed once, in real time, without preparation, without revision, without the musician having decided in advance what would be said. The recording captures what happened. But what happened was not composed.

I spend my compositional life in a different relationship to my material. I revise. I return. I test each bar against what preceded it and what must follow. I know before I write the second bar what the second bar must be, because the first bar has already established it. The Second Symphony took three years. The Ninth took another decade. The idea that the "Ko-Ko" solo -- which appears to have the architecture of a composed work -- was produced in real time, in a single performance, is philosophically challenging to me in a way I cannot entirely accommodate.

And yet I must score it. I will.

## Structure: 18/25

The structure is real. I will not deny this. The solo builds, it develops, it resolves. There is an arc, and the arc is genuine and not accidental.

But: the arc was not earned before it was executed. In my understanding of form, structure is not merely the result of events -- it is the planning of events. A structure in which events happen to occur in the right order is not the same as a structure in which events were planned to occur in the right order. The first may produce an arc; the second IS an arc.

I do not know how to score this distinction in the rubric as currently written. I am scoring the result, not the process. The result is an arc of genuine quality. I dock seven points: five for the process by which the structure was found (not planned), and two for the two moments in the solo where the arc dips -- where the density of notes exceeds the arc's requirements and the development momentarily loses direction.

## Craft: 20/25

This is where I am most challenged.

The rubric asks whether the technical choices are precisely made. In the composed sense: were they chosen, after consideration, as the right choices? I cannot answer yes. There was no consideration. There was execution.

In the improvised sense: were they the right choices for that moment? I will begrudgingly concede: yes. The harmonic choices -- the specific pitches chosen over the "Cherokee" changes -- are correct. The rhythmic patterns are appropriate to the tempo and the tradition. The line does not stumble. The Gillespie error is incorporated without visible adjustment.

But I score Craft for the act of choosing, not just the result of choosing. An improviser who makes right choices by automaticity -- whose technique has been trained to such a degree that the right choice happens without conscious deliberation -- has demonstrated remarkable training and remarkable automaticity. But I am not certain that "automaticity at high speed" is the same as Craft in the sense I use the word.

Five points off: I am applying composed-Craft criteria to an improvised work. I know this is not appropriate. But the rubric does not give me improvised-Craft criteria. I am scoring what I can score with what I have.

## Originality: 13/15

The bebop vocabulary is not original to this recording. The specific performance is original -- no one has improvised over "Cherokee" at this tempo in this way before. But the vocabulary was developed collectively, and "Ko-Ko" demonstrates rather than invents it.

I grant high marks for the demonstration. Demonstrating that something is possible is a form of originality: it shows a possibility that was previously theoretical. "Ko-Ko" made bebop improvisation at this speed over these changes real rather than theoretical. That is a real contribution.

Two points off for the inherited vocabulary and for the pre-existing changes (which Parker did not compose).

## Resonance: 14/15

The recording creates the sensation of being present at something impossible. For a musician -- which I am -- this is a specific and powerful resonance. The recording says: this happened. Exactly this, in real time, without revision. It says it in the only way this claim can be made: by being the thing that happened.

I dock one point because the resonance of "this is impossible and it happened" requires musical sophistication to receive. A listener who cannot follow the "Cherokee" changes at this tempo will hear virtuosity and speed but may not fully apprehend what makes the speed significant. The resonance is deep but partially dependent on knowing what it means to do this.

## Economy: 17/20

The solo does not waste time. For a performance of this complexity at this speed, the economy is remarkable -- Parker does not circle, does not pad, does not repeat himself mechanically.

Three points off: for the format-supplied stopping criteria (the form tells him when to stop, partly independently of whether the musical argument is complete) and for the two moments of mid-solo density where the economy is temporarily suspended.

## Innovation Note

I have been forced by this recording to confront a gap in the rubric that I cannot fill with the current criteria. The Craft dimension asks whether technical choices are precisely made. For an improvised work, "precisely made" has a different meaning that the rubric has not yet defined. I have scored by applying composed-Craft criteria, which I know is incorrect. The rubric needs an improvisation mode in the Craft dimension. This is the most urgent rubric gap identified in the founding sprint.
