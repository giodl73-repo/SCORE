# Work Description: "Ko-Ko"

## What the Recording Does

"Ko-Ko" begins with a trumpet-and-alto-saxophone theme -- a composed head written specifically for this recording rather than borrowed from "Cherokee." The head is rapid (approximately 300 beats per minute), angular, chromatic, and establishes the bebop aesthetic immediately: this is not swing, not Tin Pan Alley, not the blues as it has been played before. This is a new grammar.

Then Parker plays.

What follows in the main solo is two minutes and change of improvisation at a speed that most musicians cannot follow in real time, over harmonic changes of extreme complexity, producing a melodic line of such logic and inevitability that when it was transcribed and analyzed by musicians in the years following the recording, it appeared to have been composed -- not improvised. Lines that set up and pay off motifs across dozens of bars. Harmonic substitutions that imply the underlying changes at the highest level of sophistication. Melodic shapes that resolve into each other with the logic of counterpoint.

It was not composed. It was found.

## The "Cherokee" Changes

The "Cherokee" chord changes are built on a 32-bar AABA form with a bridge (B section) that moves through a series of remote key relationships: B major, Bb major, Ab major, G major, each approached and departed through a standard ii-V-I progression. In thirty-two bars, the harmony passes through more keys than most popular songs visit in an entire performance.

To improvise over "Cherokee" at bebop tempo, a musician must navigate these changes in real time -- must hear the harmonic movement, select pitches that function within it, and place those pitches in rhythmic patterns that create melodic logic, all simultaneously, at approximately five notes per second.

Parker does not navigate the changes. He transforms them. His improvised lines imply the changes through melodic shapes that suggest the correct pitches without stating the harmonic root -- a technique later theorized as "playing on the changes" rather than "playing the changes." The distinction matters: playing the changes means demonstrating that you know where you are harmonically; playing on the changes means making the harmonic movement itself a melodic resource, treating each chord's characteristic pitches as colors in a painting rather than points on a map.

## The Error at the Start

At the very beginning of the recording -- in the introduction, before Parker's solo begins -- Gillespie plays a chord that is incorrect for the moment. This is audible to any musician who knows "Ko-Ko." Parker, already playing his improvised line, incorporates the wrong chord without missing a beat. He sidesteps Gillespie's error so cleanly that a casual listener cannot identify it as an error at all. What the musician hears as Gillespie's mistake, Parker treats as information, and the information becomes part of the line.

This is the highest demonstration of improvised Craft: not the ability to play what you planned, but the ability to make what happened sound like what you planned. Parker was not planning; he was responding. His response was more precise than most composers' plans.

## The Speed Question

"Ko-Ko" is played at approximately 300 beats per minute. At this tempo, a sixteenth-note lasts approximately 50 milliseconds -- shorter than the shortest musical event the human motor system can consciously execute. Parker is not consciously selecting pitches at this speed. He is executing patterns that have been internalized at the deepest level of motor memory, in combinations that have been trained to respond to harmonic contexts, in a real-time stream that produces the illusion (or reality) of composed logic.

This is what Coltrane meant by technique clearing the way for the search: the technique (the scales, the arpeggios, the chromatic patterns, the ii-V-I responses) is so automatic that Parker does not think about it. What Parker is thinking about -- what he is searching for -- is the melodic and emotional logic of the line. The technique executes the search; the search is the music.

## Why This Recording

November 26, 1945 was the first recording session organized specifically for bebop as a new music. The Savoy session produced "Ko-Ko," "Billie's Bounce," "Now's the Time," and "Thriving on a Riff" -- four recordings that define the genre. "Ko-Ko" is the most extreme of the four: the fastest tempo, the most complex changes, the most technically demanding improvisation.

It is also the most perfectly executed. There are no stumbles in Parker's solo. There are no moments where the line loses its internal logic. For a piece improvised at this speed, in this harmonic terrain, in a first (or at most second) take, this is a performance that exceeds what should be statistically possible.

The recording defines bebop the way "So What" defines modal jazz: not by describing the aesthetic but by demonstrating it, completely, in a single performance.
