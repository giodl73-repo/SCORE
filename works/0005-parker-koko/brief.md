# Brief: "Ko-Ko" -- Charlie Parker (1945)

## Work

**Title:** Ko-Ko
**Artist:** Charlie Parker Quintet
**Recorded:** November 26, 1945, WOR Studios, New York (Savoy Records session)
**Duration:** approximately 2:53
**Tradition:** bebop improvisation

## Personnel

- Charlie Parker -- alto saxophone
- Dizzy Gillespie -- trumpet (theme/intro only; piano during the body of the recording after failing to recall the piano part)
- Curly Russell -- bass
- Max Roach -- drums
- (Note: The Savoy Records label credit lists Miles Davis on trumpet, but Davis was not present for this specific take; Gillespie played trumpet on the theme and then switched to piano)

## The "Cherokee" Problem

"Ko-Ko" is based on the chord changes of Ray Noble's "Cherokee" (1938), but is not credited as such. The title "Ko-Ko" distinguishes the improvisation from the underlying song. The "Cherokee" changes are among the most harmonically complex in the jazz standard repertoire -- a bridge that moves through multiple key centers at high speed. Parker's choice of "Cherokee" changes as his vehicle is itself a statement: he is choosing the hardest available harmonic terrain.

## The Improvisation Question

The most important finding of the founding sprint. "Ko-Ko" presents the SCORE rubric with its defining theoretical challenge:

**The rubric's Craft dimension asks:** "Are the technical choices -- harmony, voice leading, rhythm, texture -- precisely made?"

But Parker's solo is improvised in real time. There is no score. The technical choices were made at 300+ beats per minute, in a single take, without revision, without the possibility of reconsideration.

How does the rubric score Craft for an improvised work? Three positions:
1. Score on the transcription (treating the improvisation as if it were composed) -- misrepresents the creative act
2. Score on the quality of the real-time choices -- requires different criteria from "were the choices precisely made"
3. Improvisation requires a separate Craft mode, analogous to how different structural traditions require different Structure criteria

**Gillespie's error:** At the very beginning of the session, Gillespie plays the wrong chord. Parker sidesteps it perfectly, adjusting his improvised line to accommodate the error without the listener being able to identify the moment as a mistake. This is improvised Craft at its highest level: not the ability to play what you planned, but the ability to make what happened sound like what you planned.
