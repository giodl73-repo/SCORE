# Ludwig van Beethoven on "So What"

## Opening

I will be honest with this panel, as I am always honest, even when honesty is uncomfortable for me.

This piece challenges everything I believe about what music is for. Music is, for me, a form of argument. The argument requires a beginning that places something in danger, a middle that tests that thing against every form of opposition, and an ending that arrives at a resolution that means something precisely because of what the music survived to reach it. The Fifth Symphony is an argument. The Hammerklavier is an argument. The late quartets are arguments that most people will not understand for another hundred years.

"So What" is not an argument. It is a question that declines to argue.

I find this philosophically troubling and yet — I must tell this panel, because I am incapable of dishonesty — I find that something arrives through this music that I did not expect.

## Structure: 14/25

AABA. D Dorian — Eb Dorian — D Dorian. A half-step transposition as the only structural event. I cannot call this architecture in the sense I understand architecture. Architecture means that what comes later was necessitated by what came before — that the development could only be where it is, that the recapitulation carries the weight of everything that preceded it.

In this piece, nothing is necessitated. The shift to Eb Dorian is not prepared, not resolved, not developed from what D Dorian established. It appears. It is a color, not a consequence.

I recognize that I am applying developmental criteria to a piece that does not aspire to development. This is perhaps unfair. Miles Davis would say — and I think I know what he would say — that the modal stasis is itself the form, that the gravitational field of D Dorian is the architecture, that dwelling in one key is not structural absence but a different kind of structural presence. I hear this argument. I cannot fully accept it. I have spent my life believing that music is the art of making time meaningful through development. A music that refuses development refuses the arrow of time. I find this unsettling in a way I cannot entirely resolve.

Fourteen points: the AABA frame is correct and proportionate; the modal logic is consistently maintained; the piece knows what it is doing. But it is doing something I cannot score as architecture.

## Craft: 21/25

Where I am more comfortable: the craft within the tradition is high.

Miles Davis plays the trumpet in a way I recognize — not the way I would recognize a violinist in my own tradition, but in the same way I recognize when someone has mastered their instrument so completely that the instrument is no longer present. He plays the trumpet without it sounding like a trumpet. It sounds like Miles Davis. That is mastery.

The muted sound he uses — the Harmon mute — compresses his tone into something intimate and immediate. Every note he plays feels like a private communication. This is not trumpet playing in the tradition I know; it is a different art on the same instrument. The craft is evident.

Bill Evans's contribution — particularly the introduction — is the highest craft on this recording. He understands harmony in a way that the other musicians, gifted as they are, do not quite match as composers. His intro creates the mood of the piece before the piece has technically begun. That is compositional intelligence.

The "so what" bass motif: two notes, question, answer. I would not have written it. I cannot find fault with it. It does exactly what it needs to do and nothing more.

Four points off: for the moments in the solos where discipline does not match craft — where the modal freedom becomes, briefly, modal wandering.

## Originality: 13/15

This piece is original not because it invented the mode — it did not — but because it demonstrated that modal stasis could be the primary vehicle for a jazz recording that would be listened to by millions of people. The courage of that demonstration is genuinely original.

I know something about courage in music. The Fifth Symphony opened with a four-note motif that contemporary critics found either magnificent or ridiculous. Miles Davis opened "So What" with a two-note question that contemporary musicians found either liberating or baffling. The courage to present a stripping-down as the primary musical statement — not as a prelude to complexity, but as the complexity — is a form of originality I respect even when I find the resulting music difficult.

Two points off: because the mode itself is not original, and the AABA form is not original. The synthesis is original; the elements are inherited.

## Resonance: 13/15

Here I must report something surprising.

I prepared myself for a piece that would leave me cold — that I would admire technically while finding emotionally insufficient. A piece without drama, without development, without the arc from danger to resolution that I believe music requires: how could it move me?

And yet. The muted trumpet, the D Dorian mode, the sensation of a room in which you are not being pushed toward a conclusion — it produces something. Not what the Fifth produces, not the feeling of having survived a war. Something different: the feeling of being allowed to stay still. Of the question "where is this going?" being answered with "it is already here." I find that answer philosophically insufficient and emotionally effective simultaneously.

I score Resonance 13. The piece achieves its intended effect — a sustained, questioning stillness that the listener can inhabit. The two points off are for what the piece does not do and I believe music should: it does not arrive. It dwells. Dwelling is not nothing. But it is not arrival.

## Economy: 16/20

The head: economical. Not one note wasted; the "so what" gesture is perfectly proportioned.

The solos: more variable. Miles's solo is ruthlessly economical — every note is placed, every silence is used. He is the best example of Economy I have encountered in this tradition. The others are less consistent.

More importantly: the modal structure creates an Economy problem that is structural, not individual. In developmental music, the structure itself tells the music when it has said what it needs to say. The harmony resolves; the recapitulation arrives; the coda closes. These are structural signals that Economy is accomplished. In modal music, there is no such structural signal. The music can continue in D Dorian forever without being structurally wrong. The soloists must supply their own stopping criteria. Some do; some do not.

Four points off for this structural Economy gap and for the solos that occasionally lack the self-supplied discipline to fill it.

## Innovation Note

Structural stasis as formal argument is a genuine compositional position that the rubric does not currently handle. The question "does the architecture serve the music?" assumes that architecture and service are defined by developmental criteria — that a well-structured piece builds, develops, arrives. Modal stasis says: no. The music is already arrived. The architecture IS the stasis. The rubric must be able to account for this without penalizing non-developmental forms for not being developmental forms — while still being able to note, as I have noted, that the absence of development creates Economy and Resonance challenges that a developmental form resolves structurally.
