# Miles Davis on "So What"

## Opening

You want to know what I was looking for. I'll tell you what I was looking for. I was looking for the thing that was in the note. Not the thing the next chord told me to play, not the thing the changes demanded, not the navigation. The thing that was already there, waiting, in D.

The bebop cats — Bird, Dizzy — they were the fastest navigators in the world. I wasn't going to out-navigate Bird. Nobody was. What I was looking for was something different. I was looking for what was underneath the navigation. What you found when you stopped moving.

This piece is the answer to the question I'd been asking for ten years.

## Structure: 22/25

The form is AABA. The A is D Dorian. The B is Eb Dorian up a half-step. You come back to D. That's the whole thing.

You're going to ask me: is that a structure? Yes. It is a structure. It is a structure that was arrived at after a long period of looking for it. George Russell had shown me the modal logic — the way a mode creates a gravitational field instead of a chord progression. I knew what I wanted. The AABA frame is the container I found for it.

Is it architecture in the way a fugue is architecture? No. It doesn't need to be. It is architecture in the way a room is architecture. A room doesn't need to change shape to be a room. A mode doesn't need to develop to be a structure. It creates a space. The space is the structure. What you do in the space is the music.

The one thing the structure doesn't do: it doesn't earn its half-step shift to Eb. That shift exists. It works. But I couldn't tell you why it's a half-step and not a whole step except that it sounded right. That's not the same as architecture earning its transitions. Bach would dock me for that. He's right to dock me. Two points off.

## Craft: 22/25

Bill Evans understood this music before anyone else in the room. His introduction — the rubato opening — is the thesis statement of the whole piece before the bass enters. He knew what we were looking for. What he played in that introduction is some of the most intelligent music-making I've heard.

Paul Chambers's bass line — the "so what" motif — is perfect. Two notes. Question, answer. Nothing wasted. If Chambers had played three notes, the whole thing would have been wrong. He played two.

My solo: I'll tell you what was in it and what wasn't. What was in it: space. More space than most people knew what to do with. Every note I placed, I placed where the silence before it had prepared it. What wasn't in it: a lot of things I could have played but didn't. That's not absence. That's the craft.

Coltrane's solo is technically extraordinary. He and I were thinking about this material differently — he was searching the mode exhaustively, I was searching it selectively. Both are valid. His craft is beyond question. But his solo is longer than the music needs in places, which is a craft question. Three points off overall for moments where the soloists' individual excess works against the piece's economy.

## Originality: 14/15

Is this original? Yes. I didn't invent modes. George Russell had theorized this. But I was the first to make modal music that anyone outside a music school would listen to and not be able to stop listening to.

The originality is not in the concept. The originality is in what you do with the concept. You take away the chord changes, which is the entire structural logic of bebop, and you put something else in its place: the mode as tonal field, the improviser's relationship to that field as the drama, the space between notes as a musical event. That hadn't been done this way. In this room. With this instrumentation. On this record.

One point off because I know I was standing on Russell's shoulders. Original does not mean uninfluenced.

## Resonance: 14/15

The piece produces the sensation it set out to produce. It produces the sensation of being inside a room where you are not required to move. Where you can look at one thing and find that it contains enough. Where the question "where is this going?" is answered with: it's going here. It's already here.

The fact that this is the best-selling jazz record ever made suggests that other people had this experience. A lot of them. Most of them without knowing anything about modes. One point off because there are listeners who find the stasis uncomfortable — who need forward motion to feel that music is happening. That is not a failure of the piece. But it is a real response, and the piece doesn't offer anything to those listeners except the invitation to stay still, which some people cannot accept.

## Economy: 20/20

Nothing wasted. This is the piece I've been looking for. Two modes, two chords, one question, one answer, and then you let the musicians find what's there. The intro by Evans is exactly as long as it needs to be. The solos are as long as they are and not longer than the space requires — mostly.

The word that matters is restraint. Restraint is not absence. Restraint is knowing what to remove and having the discipline to remove it. This piece was made by removing almost everything bebop thought was necessary and finding out what was left. What was left was enough.

## Innovation Note

The structural stasis in this piece is not a failure of structure. It is a different kind of structure. The mode creates a gravitational field that does what chord progressions do in tonal music — establishes home, defines tension and release, gives the improviser somewhere to be and somewhere to return to. The rubric's Structure anchors are written for developmental forms. They need to handle modal forms. The mode IS the structure. Not the absence of structure — a different kind of structure, operating on different principles, asking different things of the listener and the improviser. This is something the rubric should name.
