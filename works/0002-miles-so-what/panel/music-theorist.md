# Music Theorist on "So What"

## Formal Analysis

"So What" operates within the tradition of modal jazz, a formal system that is distinct from tonal jazz (based on functional harmony) and from free jazz (which may abandon harmonic structure entirely). The modal tradition has its own grammar, and assessment of this work must apply that grammar.

## Structure Analysis

**Modal center vs. functional harmony.** D Dorian is not "D minor with a raised sixth" — this is the most common mischaracterization of the piece. D Dorian is an Aeolian mode transposed to D, but more importantly, it is a modal center: a gravitational field in which the D is home, certain scale degrees create instability (the seventh, the leading tone tendencies suppressed because the Dorian mode lacks a leading tone), and the raised sixth (B natural) provides a characteristic brightening that distinguishes Dorian from natural minor.

The structural consequence of this is significant: modal music does not move through functional progressions (I–IV–V–I or ii–V–I), so there is no structural tension-and-release in the tonal sense. Instead, structural tension comes from the improviser's relationship to the modal center — how far they range from the tonic, which chromatic inflections they introduce, how they approach and leave the tonic.

**The half-step modulation.** The shift to Eb Dorian is not a functional modulation (it does not establish a new tonal relationship based on harmonic logic). It is a direct transposition — modal shift by minor second. In the context of this piece, its function is coloristic: the same gesture, the same gravitational logic, at a higher pitch level. The effect is one of brightening (higher register) and subtle intensification (the half-step rise is the smallest possible harmonic movement, creating a sense of adjacency rather than contrast).

Theoretically, this is interesting: the B section does not provide contrast through a new formal argument (as a B section in sonata form would), but through a chromatic shift that leaves the formal logic identical. This is a characteristic feature of modal AABA form that distinguishes it from tonal AABA form.

**The "so what" motif.** The bass call (E–D) and horn response (voiced as a chord voicing on the same pitches) is a miniature example of call-and-response form. The two-note gesture is not a melody in the thematic sense; it is a harmonic indicator — it names the mode and establishes the tonal center. Every subsequent note in the piece is heard in relation to this establishing gesture.

## Craft Analysis

The voice leading in the horns' chordal response is of interest. The voicing is quartal — built on fourths rather than thirds — which produces the specific ambiguity that Evans cultivated in his approach to modal harmony. Quartal voicings do not imply a functional harmonic direction; they create a chord that "leans" in multiple directions simultaneously without strongly implying resolution. This is precisely correct for the modal aesthetic: the chord does not want to go anywhere; it inhabits its position.

The bass line during the solos (walking bass) is harmonically simpler than in bebop, which makes sense: the modal center does not require the complex harmonic navigation that bebop changes demand. Chambers plays mostly root-motion and modal notes, filling the rhythm section's role without the harmonic complexity of bebop bass.

The drum writing (Jimmy Cobb) is similarly modal in approach: the time is stated clearly but the accents are less angular than bebop drumming, creating a sense of flow rather than punctuation. This is a crafted choice: the rhythm section is not simply reduced in complexity but reconfigured to serve the modal aesthetic.

## Harmonic Content

The soloists' use of the mode deserves specific attention:

**Miles Davis** operates primarily within the core Dorian pitches, using the upper extensions (7th, 9th) sparingly and for color rather than harmonic direction. His approach is scale-selective rather than scale-exhaustive.

**John Coltrane** treats the mode more comprehensively, exploring its upper reaches and occasionally reaching beyond the strict Dorian pitches into chromatic territory. This produces moments of modal "outside" playing — brief excursions that create tension against the modal center. His use of the mode is harmonic in a way Miles's is not; he is navigating, even without changes to navigate.

**Bill Evans** in his solo demonstrates quartal harmonic thinking applied to improvisation — building melodic lines from fourths and seconds rather than thirds, creating a voice that is harmonically integrated with the chord voicings he established in the introduction.

## Innovation Note

The rubric's Structure anchors assume developmental form as the standard against which all structures are measured. Modal form creates structural coherence through a different mechanism: the tonal center establishes a gravitational field that replaces functional harmonic progression as the architectural principle. D Dorian is the architecture of "So What" in the same sense that sonata form is the architecture of the Fifth Symphony — they are just different kinds of architecture. The rubric should be amended to recognize this distinction, scoring modal form against modal criteria (internal consistency of the modal center, quality of the establishing gesture, coherence of the improvisers' relationship to the tonal field) rather than against developmental criteria it was not designed to meet.
