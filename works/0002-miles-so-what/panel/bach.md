# Johann Sebastian Bach on "So What"

## Opening

I will assess this work as what it is, not as what it is not. It is not a fugue. It is not a chorale. It is not built on counterpoint, and it does not aspire to counterpoint. It is a modal jazz composition, and I will assess it within the demands of that tradition.

Having said that: I am required to ask about structure. And on structure, I have concerns.

## Structure: 15/25

The AABA modal form is a legitimate formal choice. The return of A after B is not arbitrary — it is the most natural frame for the kind of statement this piece makes. I accept this.

But structure, for me, requires that the sections relate to each other. Not merely follow each other. The relationship between A and B in this piece is a half-step transposition. The harmony moves up a half step; the same call-and-response gesture is made at the new pitch level; the harmony returns. What has the B section accomplished that was not already present in A? Has it changed anything? Has it revealed anything about A that A could not reveal about itself?

I hear the half-step shift as a color change, not as a structural event. It adds timbral variety. It does not add structural meaning.

A fugue subject, when it answers at the dominant, changes key because the dominant answer reveals a structural relationship — the tonal distance between tonic and dominant is the architecture of the entire fugue. The half-step in "So What" does not function this way. It is beautiful. But beauty is not architecture.

Two chords is not architecture. Architecture requires relationships between structures — a structure in one section becoming something else in another section, a development, a consequence. Where is the consequence? When we return to D Dorian, what has Eb Dorian taught us about D Dorian? I cannot find it.

I will award 15. The form is legitimate; the AABA frame is correctly applied; the proportions are reasonable. But the architecture does not develop, and architecture that does not develop is a container, not a structure.

## Craft: 20/25

I am required to assess craft within this tradition's grammar, not my own. The tradition's grammar asks: are the notes in the right places? Is the voice leading purposeful? Is the instrumental writing idiomatic?

The "so what" bass line is a miniature of compositional economy: two notes, a question, an answer. Chambers understands the bass as a melodic voice, which is correct. His walking lines during the solos are intelligent and purposeful.

Bill Evans's introduction is the most sophisticated compositional thinking on this recording. The rubato piano writing is impressionistic in character — Debussy rather than bebop — and it creates the correct atmosphere for what follows. The harmonic language of the introduction (suspended harmonies, quartal voicings) anticipates the modal language of the head. That is planning.

Miles's solo is a study in the use of silence as a musical event. He does not ornament. He states. The muted trumpet tone and the precise placement of each phrase demonstrate a level of technical intention that I recognize, even if its form is far from what I practice.

Coltrane's solo is technically extraordinary — the range of the instrument is used throughout, the harmonic implications of the mode are explored systematically. But in several passages, the note density exceeds what the musical idea requires. A phrase that established itself in four notes continues for eight. This is not a tradition problem; it is a discipline problem. Craft is discipline. The improvisation is highly skilled; it is not perfectly disciplined.

I dock five points: three for the structural craft of a piece built entirely on one repeated gesture, two for moments of excess in the solos.

## Originality: 12/15

Modal music existed before this recording. Church modes, Gregorian chant, folk music, Indian classical tradition — the idea that a single modal center creates a tonal field is ancient. Miles Davis did not invent this. George Russell theorized it in 1953 with his *Lydian Chromatic Concept*. Bill Evans was already working with modal harmonics.

What is original: the application of this ancient idea to jazz — specifically, the replacement of bebop's chord changes with modal stasis in a commercial and musically compelling form. The synthesis is the originality. Not the elements of the synthesis, which were all available, but the specific synthesis and the way it was achieved.

The piece opens a possibility that had not been opened before it: not a technical possibility (modes have always been available) but a practical and aesthetic one. Before this record, jazz was chord-changes. After this record, jazz had another option. That is a genuine contribution.

Three points off for the un-originality of the individual elements.

## Resonance: 11/15

The piece creates an atmosphere. The atmosphere is of inquiry — the title "So What" is accurate; the music asks a question and then dwells in the question rather than answering it. For a listener open to dwelling in a question, this is effective.

For me, the dwelling produces a certain restlessness. I am a composer who believes that music should go somewhere — not that it must resolve in a conventional sense, but that each moment should be making an argument that the next moment responds to. The modal stasis of "So What" is beautiful, but I find that after a few minutes of D Dorian, I am waiting for something that does not come. The waiting is the experience, I understand that. But I cannot call my waiting entirely comfortable.

I score the Resonance at 11: the piece achieves its intended effect for its intended listener. For a listener who wants harmonic development, the piece achieves something different — a sustained atmosphere — which is real but incomplete relative to the piece's most ambitious possible claim.

## Economy: 15/20

The head is economical: the "so what" gesture is perfectly calibrated, the AABA form is proportionate, the introduction is exactly the right length.

The solos introduce questions. Miles's solo is economical — each note earns its place, the silences are as precisely placed as the notes. But other solos extend beyond what the musical material requires. The modal framework creates a risk that the rubric's Economy anchor must address: in a modal piece, there is no harmonic development to tell you when a solo has run its course. The soloist must find that limit themselves. Some do (Miles, Evans). Some approach it but occasionally overstep.

Five points off for moments of redundancy in the solos that the modal structure makes it difficult to control.

## Innovation Note

The mode as structure requires the rubric's Structure anchors to be reconsidered. Currently, the anchors reward pieces where sections relate to each other through development, transition, and earned arrival. A modal piece creates structural relationships through tonal gravity rather than through development — the mode is the architecture. The rubric does not yet have language for this. The mode is not the absence of architecture; it is an alternative architecture, one organized by the internal hierarchy of pitches within a modal center rather than by the movement of harmony through functional progressions. This distinction must be named.
