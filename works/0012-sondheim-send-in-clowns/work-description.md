# Work Description: 0012 — "Send in the Clowns"
# Stephen Sondheim, A Little Night Music (1973)

---

## The Dramatic Situation

Desirée Armfeldt is an actress in her mid-thirties, touring the provinces in a theatrical company. She was once Fredrik Egerman's lover. Years later, she encounters him again: he has married Anne, a girl half his age, and the marriage has not been consummated. Desirée and Fredrik spend a night together; Anne discovers this. Fredrik, confronted with his own foolishness, resolves to stay with Anne.

Desirée is left alone. She has loved Fredrik for years, finally had the opportunity to reclaim him, and watched him choose the wrong thing. The song she sings is not a conventional lament. It is something more precise: self-aware sorrow, the grief of a woman smart enough to see exactly how she and Fredrik have made fools of themselves, and compassionate enough to include him in the joke.

"Isn't it rich? Are we a pair?" She knows what they are. She doesn't hate him for it. She doesn't even hate herself. She is wry and heartbroken simultaneously — a combination that is technically very difficult to write and nearly impossible to sentimentalize.

---

## Musical Analysis

### Form
Modified strophic form with developmental harmonic trajectory. The song has three primary strophes, each beginning with "Isn't it —" or "Isn't it rich?" and each describing a moment of missed timing. A fourth section ("Just when I stopped opening doors...") introduces the ironic reversal: now that she has stopped trying, Fredrik is arriving. The final strophe is contracted — the last question, "Send in the clowns / Don't bother, they're here," arrives as the compressed, devastating endpoint.

### Meter and Phrase Structure
The waltz (3/4) is continuously disrupted. Sondheim drops beats throughout — phrases that begin with a three-count pulse suddenly lose a beat or add one. A typical phrase might be:

- Bars 1-2: 3/4 as stated
- Bar 3: phrase ends before the third beat — the rest IS the beat
- Bar 4: the harmonic resolution that should arrive with the bar line comes a beat late, or not at all

The effect: the music itself cannot get its timing right. The meter is Desirée's situation given rhythmic form.

### Harmonic Language
The harmonic language uses suspension and delayed resolution as its primary expressive device. The dominant seventh arrives; the resolution is withheld. This is not a voice-leading error — it is a voice-leading decision. The harmonic irresolution mirrors the dramatic irresolution: Desirée cannot resolve her situation; the harmony cannot resolve its tension.

The word "clowns" — at "Send in the clowns" — arrives on a half-cadence or an interrupted cadence rather than a full authentic cadence. Sondheim does not give the title word a satisfying harmonic landing. The irony is musical as well as textual: the word that should be the conclusion arrives without conclusion.

### The Rests
The rests throughout this song are not absences of music. They are the character not speaking — the pause before she finds the next self-aware phrase, the hesitation before the ironic mask settles back into place. In the context of the drama, these rests are more expressive than any note could be. This is Innovation #26 terrain: the rest as dramatic event.

### Prosody
The alignment of lyric stress with musical stress is Sondheim's primary Craft marker. Every syllable lands on the beat that its emotional weight demands. "Isn't it rich?" — the stress on "rich" falls on the strong beat, but the question mark lifts the phrase upward rather than settling it. "Are we a pair?" — the stress on "pair" falls correctly, but "pair" rhymes with "there" in a way that suggests incompleteness rather than completion.

The lyric and the musical phrase are not parallel — they are the same argument expressed in different media simultaneously (Innovation #16, Lyric-Note Unity from work 0007, confirmed here).

## Historical Context

*A Little Night Music* opened on Broadway in February 1973. The decision to set the entire score in waltz time (3/4 or its derivatives) was Sondheim's structural commitment to the operetta tradition — the Viennese waltz as the metric emblem of a particular kind of romantic comedy, the kind that is doomed and beautiful simultaneously.

"Send in the Clowns" became the most commercially successful song Sondheim ever wrote — the one song from his catalog that achieved popular-song status outside the theater. He found this somewhat ironic: he wrote songs to serve dramatic moments, not to produce hits. "Send in the Clowns" succeeded commercially partly because it can be extracted from its dramatic context and still communicate a general feeling of bittersweet regret. But the song's full achievement is inseparable from the dramatic situation that produced it.

The song received a Tony Award and a Grammy Award. It has been recorded by more performers than any other Sondheim song. Barbra Streisand, Frank Sinatra, and Judy Collins each recorded definitive versions. The song is Sondheim's most frequently cited work in discussions of the musical theater as an art form.
