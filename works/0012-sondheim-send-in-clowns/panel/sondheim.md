## Step 0 — Structural Approach

**DEVELOPMENTAL (modified waltz-song form)**

Before scoring, the structural approach must be named. This is a modified strophic form with a developmental harmonic and dramatic trajectory — not a simple AABA standard, not a through-composed aria, but something in between. Each strophe develops the dramatic situation: the first establishes the irony (they missed each other again), the second extends it (now that he's arrived she's about to leave), the third inverts it (now that she's given up, he's finally ready), and the final compression ("Don't bother, they're here") completes the dramatic arc. The form is driven by dramatic necessity, not by musical convention.

Is this Cluster A (structural self-proof)? No. The song does not prove a formal claim by being the demonstration of it. It illustrates its emotional character through its structural hesitations — it shows rather than proves. The Cluster A test requires that the work be the proof; this work is the demonstration. Cluster A does not apply.

## Review

I am reviewing my own work, which places me in a particular position. I know what I was trying to do. The question is whether I did it.

What I was trying to do: write a song in which Desirée's intelligence and her grief occupy exactly the same note simultaneously. Not intelligence then grief, not intelligence covering grief — both at once, in every phrase. The irony is not a defense mechanism. It is not Desirée protecting herself from the pain. It is the form her pain takes. She is that kind of person: she sees the joke even when she is the joke.

The musical problem this creates: conventional lament melody is expansive, sustained, reaching upward. That is the vocabulary for grief openly expressed. But Desirée's grief is not openly expressed — it is expressed through irony, which means through compression, through understatement, through the phrase that ends a beat early rather than completing its arc.

Every melodic hesitation in this song — every phrase that pulls back before its cadential landing — is a Craft decision in service of dramatic function. The metric disruptions (the dropped beats, the added rests) are not metric variety for variety's sake. They enact what Desirée cannot say: "We were never on the same beat." The harmony's refusal to resolve when the text says "clowns" is not a voice-leading choice; it is a dramatic statement. The word that should land definitively does not land.

This is Dramatic-Functional Craft. Every technical choice in this song has to be assessed first by the question: does it serve what Desirée needs to express at this moment? The hesitations serve her. The irresolutions serve her. The compressed final strophe — the contraction from full strophe to two lines — serves her in the most precise way: she has said what she needed to say and stops. The economy of the ending is the economy of a woman who has run out of ways to be wry about her own heartbreak.

What I would change: very little. The second strophe ("Isn't it bliss?") sustains slightly longer than the dramatic moment requires; the melody continues half a phrase after the lyric's meaning has been delivered. This is the one moment where the music outruns the dramatic necessity. It is not a failure — it gives the performer breathing room — but it is the one place where musical logic slightly outweighed dramatic logic.

The rest is correct. The rests are music. The irresolutions are argument. The metric hesitations are character. Every technical choice serves Desirée in this moment.

**Craft assessment note: Dramatic-Functional Craft criteria applied throughout. The primary Craft question is whether each technical choice serves Desirée's emotional state at this moment. It does, with one minor exception (second strophe extension). This constitutes an independent naming of the Dramatic-Functional Craft criterion — Cluster D confirmation contribution #1.**
