# Historian — "Erbarme Dich, mein Gott" (BWV 244)
*Weight: 10%*

---

**Context and Position in Tradition**

"Erbarme Dich" was composed for the premiere of *St. Matthew Passion*, BWV 244, performed Good Friday, April 11, 1727, at the Thomaskirche in Leipzig. Bach was 42 years old, in his fourth year as Thomaskantor. The *St. Matthew Passion* is one of the largest-scale works in the Baroque repertoire — double choir, double orchestra, soloists — and "Erbarme Dich" comes at a climactic moment of Part I: Peter's denial and its aftermath.

**The Lamento Tradition**

The aria draws on the *lamento* tradition — a genre of grief aria with a long history in Baroque vocal music, often characterized by descending chromatic basses, minor mode, and specific intervallic gestures associated with grief (falling semitones, descending lines). The *lamento* was a well-established Baroque form by 1727; Purcell's "When I am laid in earth" from *Dido and Aeneas* (1689) is the canonical English-language instance.

What Bach does that is not entirely conventional: he moves the primary expressive argument from the bass voice (the conventional lamento bass) to the upper voices. The continuo bass is not the lamento bass; it is a straightforward harmonic support. The lament lives in the conversation between the violin and the alto — in the upper register, where grief speaks in a higher voice than the traditional convention places it. This is a refinement of the tradition rather than a departure from it.

**Position in Bach's Sacred Output**

By 1727, Bach had composed approximately 200 cantatas (of the roughly 300 surviving today), as well as the *St. John Passion* (1724). He was at the height of his mastery of the sacred dramatic form. The *St. Matthew Passion* is generally considered his crowning achievement in this tradition, and "Erbarme Dich" is among the most celebrated arias within it.

The aria was essentially unknown until Mendelssohn's revival of the *St. Matthew Passion* in 1829 — 79 years after its composition and 102 years after its premiere. The modern understanding of Bach's sacred music, including the historical significance of this aria, is largely a retrospective construction that began in the nineteenth century.

**The Devotional-Dramatic Problem**

The *St. Matthew Passion* is a work that sits at an unusual intersection in the history of music: it is simultaneously a liturgical work (composed for church performance on Good Friday, serving a specifically devotional function within the Lutheran liturgy), a dramatic work (the Passion narrative has characters, a plot, and a specific dramatic arc), and a concert work (it was, from Mendelssohn's revival onward, performed in concert halls rather than churches, by audiences who were not primarily attending a liturgical service).

This three-way status has been a persistent question in musicological discussion. Is the *Matthew Passion* primarily a church work or a concert work? Is its correct context the liturgy or the concert hall? The question has never been definitively answered, because the work inhabits all three contexts simultaneously and does not simply belong to any one of them.

"Erbarme Dich" crystallizes this question: it is a prayer (devotional direction upward), it is a dramatic response to a specific narrative moment (dramatic direction into the story), and it is an aesthetic object of extraordinary beauty that functions in concert performance without any liturgical or dramatic context at all (concert-hall direction toward the listener). All three directions are active.

**Tradition Contribution**

This aria established a model for the devotional-dramatic aria that influenced sacred vocal music for generations. The two-voice argument (obligato instrument as co-equal expressive voice with the singer, not as virtuosic display) is the direct ancestor of, among others, Handel's late oratorio arias, C.P.E. Bach's expressive instrumental writing, and even — by the long road of the nineteenth century revival — certain conceptions of the song accompaniment in Schubert.
