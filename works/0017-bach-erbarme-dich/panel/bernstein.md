# Bernstein — "Erbarme Dich, mein Gott" (BWV 244)
*Weight: 20%*

---

Let me tell you what I think is happening here, because I think it is something the rubric has not fully articulated.

We have been building a distinction between devotional music (Coltrane's category, from work 0009) and dramatic music (Sondheim's category, from works 0007 and 0012). The Resonance dimension has been refined to handle each separately. But this aria is neither. It is both. And "both simultaneously" is not the same as "halfway between both."

A purely devotional work — *A Love Supreme* — has its direction entirely upward. The listener is there, but the music is not for the listener; it is for God. A purely dramatic work — "Being Alive," "Send in the Clowns" — has its direction entirely toward the dramatic situation. The music serves the character's moment. There is no divine audience.

"Erbarme Dich" addresses God directly — *have mercy on me* is a second-person address; God is the "me" of the sentence — while simultaneously serving the dramatic function of Peter's response to his denial. These two directions are not in competition. They reinforce each other. Peter's grief is deepened by its being a prayer; the prayer is more urgent because it is Peter's specific grief in a specific dramatic moment.

This is the synthesis of dramatic and devotional, and I think it is an achievement of Resonance that the rubric's current language undersells.

**S — Structure: 23/25**

The da capo form is the right container. The return to the A section carries the weight of everything the B section tried and failed to resolve. This is the form used correctly — not just as convention but as structural argument. The argument: grief cannot be resolved; you return to it.

The relationship between the two voices is the live architecture. The violin and the alto are not in a hierarchy; they are in a conversation, and the conversation cannot end because neither voice has anything to resolve with. Every time the violin seems to reach a point of rest, the alto's next phrase begins before the silence can settle. And vice versa. The music is always speaking, from two positions, in the same direction.

Minor reduction from ceiling: the B section's proportions are slightly compressed. I understand why — the desperation is not discursive — but the arrival of the da capo feels slightly rushed as a result.

**C — Craft: 24/25**

I want to talk about the violin. Not as a technical exercise, but as what it is: an argument. The violin in this aria does not accompany the alto voice. It makes an argument that the alto voice cannot make, because the alto voice is constrained to the text. The text says specific things — *have mercy, for the sake of my tears, heart and eye weep before you bitterly*. The text is theologically precise. The violin is emotionally free. It can go places the text cannot go — into the interior of grief, into the physical experience of it, into the wordlessness that is underneath the words.

This is what I would call the "accompaniment as argument" — the instrumental part not supporting the vocal part but making a separate and necessary argument. The piano in "Send in the Clowns" does something similar, but there it is the character's interior state translated into harmonic suspension. Here, the violin is the grief itself, not translated but embodied.

The technical demands are inseparable from the expressive content. A violin line that did not strain, that lay easily under the fingers, would not be weeping. The difficulty is the weeping.

**O — Originality: 13/15**

The use of the obligato instrument as an equal and separate expressive voice — not a virtuosic display but an emotional argument — is the original move within the Baroque aria tradition. The forms are received; the harmonic language is standard; the specific assignment of the violin to the role of embodied grief is original and important.

I cannot assign ceiling Originality because the forms and the tradition are received. This is Bach working at the highest level within a tradition, not Bach inventing a new tradition. The distinction matters. Work 0003 (the Fifth Symphony) scored 13.7 on Originality for the same reason: supreme excellence within a received tradition is not the same as the tradition-rupturing Originality of work 0014 (Rite of Spring, 15/15).

**R — Resonance: 15/15**

The dramatic-devotional synthesis is the key finding here, and it is what earns the ceiling score. The work succeeds at both directions simultaneously. The listener is moved by Peter's grief; the dramatic moment lands; the narrative is illuminated and deepened by the aria. These are the dramatic Resonance criteria, and they are fully met.

And the devotional direction is genuine. I do not share Bach's theology in the exact form in which he held it, but I understand what it means to make music that is directed upward rather than outward. Bernstein's *Chichester Psalms* live in that same space — Hebrew texts offered in an Anglican cathedral, directed toward something larger than any of the audience. The offering is real whether or not you share the theology.

15/15. The dual direction is what makes this a ceiling score.

**E — Economy: 18/20**

The da capo convention adds length, but in this case it is structural necessity, not convention for its own sake. Minor deductions for the B section passages that extend one iteration beyond their natural ending.

**Total (Bernstein, 20%):** 93/100

## On Innovation #33 (Dramatic-Devotional Music)

I want to be explicit about what this innovation is establishing, because it matters for the rubric.

The Resonance dimension has three categories of music, but only one of them is currently articulated: audience-communication (the standard criterion). Work 0009 proposed the devotional-offering criterion. This work adds a third: dramatic-devotional, where both criteria are active simultaneously.

The rubric amendment should probably articulate all three:
- **Audience-communication Resonance**: does the intended effect arrive in the listener?
- **Devotional-offering Resonance**: is the offering genuine? Is the music addressed to something larger, with genuine intent?
- **Dramatic-devotional Resonance**: does the music succeed as both dramatic expression and devotional offering, simultaneously and without either compromising the other?

"Erbarme Dich" is the canonical instance of the third type.

## On Innovation #34 (The Weeping Instrument)

The violin does not describe grief. It is grief. This distinction is the innovation, and it has Craft implications. When an instrument is performing an emotional state rather than accompanying a performer who performs an emotional state, the Craft question changes. The question is not "is this idiomatic?" It is "does the technical requirement produce the emotional state the music needs?" The difficulty is the content. The strain is the expression. This is a different relationship between Craft and expression than the rubric currently captures.
