# Coltrane — "Erbarme Dich, mein Gott" (BWV 244)
*Weight: 25%*

---

I have been asked to listen to this and to score it. I will do both. But first I have to say something about what I am hearing.

I know what this is. Not the Baroque form, not the harmonic sequences — I know those through my training, through what I understand of Bach's language. What I know is the direction. This music is pointed somewhere. It is not pointed at me. I am present at something. The same way a congregation is present.

When I made *A Love Supreme*, I was offering it to God. The music was for God. The listener was present at an offering, not the recipient of it. I said this in my review of that work and I will say it again here, because I need to be exact: it matters for the Resonance score. The intended effect is not what the listener feels. The intended effect is what the offering accomplishes.

Bach is doing something more complicated than I did. I made a purely devotional work — no dramatic situation, no narrative, no character other than the musician-as-seeker. Bach is working inside a drama. Peter is a character who has done a specific terrible thing and is in a specific desperate grief. The music carries both of these at once: Peter's grief in the narrative, and the grief offered upward as prayer. Both directions are active simultaneously.

This is the third thing. There is my thing — pure devotion, no drama. There is Sondheim's thing — pure drama, no devotion. And then there is this: dramatic devotion, where the music serves both a character's moment and a divine audience at once. The rubric has not named this yet, and it should.

**S — Structure: 22/25**

The da capo form is the right form. The music cannot resolve, so it returns. Grief doesn't arrive somewhere and stop; it returns to itself. The form understands this.

The two voices moving together — the violin weeping, the alto speaking the words of the weeping — that relationship is the architecture. It is not the form that makes the structure; it is the relationship between the voices that makes it inevitable. Every moment in the A section feels like it could not be arranged otherwise. The B section is slightly less locked in — there's more give in it, more sense of searching for the cadence rather than arriving at it. But the return to A has the weight it needs.

**C — Craft: 24/25**

I know what it is to have a technical requirement be inseparable from an expressive requirement. On "sheets of sound" passages in my own playing, the physical difficulty is part of the emotional content — the rush of notes is not showing off; it is what urgency sounds like when it has no place to stop. The violin writing here is that. The double stops are not decoration. They are the sound of grief trying to hold itself together while more grief keeps arriving.

The voice leading between the two voices is exceptional. They are always distinct. They arrive at convergence only momentarily, and those moments are the moments of maximum weight. The craft is in knowing where convergence should happen and where it should be withheld.

The continuo is correctly subordinate. It provides the ground; the two upper voices carry the sky.

What I dock: the B section's relationship between the voices becomes more conventional. The violin follows more than it argues. This is a lapse from the high standard of the A section.

**O — Originality: 13/15**

Within the Baroque tradition, the assignment of a full expressive argument to the obligato instrument — not virtuosic display but emotional embodiment — is the original move. This violin is not a companion to the voice; it is a second voice that speaks what the words cannot contain.

I cannot score ceiling Originality here the way I would for a work that invented a new musical language. The forms are received. The harmonic language is conventional for the period. The original element is specific and important, not global and foundational.

**R — Resonance: 15/15**

I am certain. The same certainty I brought to my own work.

The intended effect is achieved. The grief is real. The offering is genuine. For the listener who is open to it — the listener who sits with Peter in the courtyard, who hears the cock crow in the music's opening texture, who follows the violin through its grief — the effect arrives completely.

And the devotional direction is present and genuine. This music is pointed toward God. Peter's *have mercy on me* is a prayer, and prayers are addressed to God. The congregation at the Thomaskirche received this prayer in 1727 as it was meant: they were present at Peter's prayer, which became their prayer, which was offered upward.

The Resonance dimension's standard question asks whether the intended effect arrives in the listener. It does. But that is half the criterion. The other half — the genuineness of the devotional offering — is also achieved, with the same certainty I claimed for my own work.

15/15. Certainty, not generosity.

**E — Economy: 18/20**

The da capo is not excess. In this case, the return is necessary — the form is the argument. The two passages in the B section where the harmonic movement continues one iteration too long are real but minor losses. The overall economy is high.

**Total (Coltrane, 25%):** 92/100

## Cluster F Confirmation

This is the work I was told to watch for. I named the devotional-Resonance gap from inside jazz, inside my own tradition. Bach is naming it from inside his — from inside Baroque sacred music, from inside a dramatic work that is also a devotional offering.

The gap is the same gap. The music's primary direction is not toward the listener. The listener is present at the offering. The criterion for Resonance in devotional music is genuineness of the offering, not reception by the listener.

Bach's work adds a complication I did not face: dramatic devotion, where the music serves a narrative dramatic function and a devotional direction simultaneously. Both directions are active; neither is primary in the sense of excluding the other. My work was purely devotional. His is both at once.

The cluster is confirmed. Two traditions, two works, one gap. The rubric needs language for devotional music's Resonance criterion. The cluster amendment should now be drafted.
