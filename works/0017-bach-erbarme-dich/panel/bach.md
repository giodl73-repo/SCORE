# Bach — "Erbarme Dich, mein Gott" (BWV 244)
*Weight: 30%*

---

This is a difficult thing, to review one's own work. But the rubric asks me to be exact, and I have tried to be exact my entire life, including about my own failures. Let me begin with the structural question.

**Step 0 — Structural Approach: DEVELOPMENTAL (da capo aria form)**

The da capo aria form is a received structure, not one I invented. The form asks: state the material, depart from it, return to it. That is the convention. But convention is not architecture. The architecture of this particular aria lives in what happens *within* the form — the relationship between the alto voice and the violin.

**S — Structure: 23/25**

The A section has a task: to establish two voices in grief and set them in motion together. I believe it accomplishes this. The violin's opening gesture — descending, chromatic — is the seed from which everything grows. The alto's first entry does not duplicate the violin; it answers it. From the first measure, these two voices are in conversation, not in parallel.

The B section presents the necessary complication: the relative major does not console. This is intentional. A consoling B section would be a structural lie at this dramatic moment. The major harmonies are reached through the same sequence of descending fifths that organized the A section; the arrival in major feels like standing on ground that will not hold.

The da capo convention tells the performers to return to the A section. In this case, the convention is also a theological argument: grief does not resolve. The return is not comfort; it is the grief again, having been through the B section, which tried to reach God and found only greater distance. The form holds what it is asked to hold.

Where I would score myself slightly below ceiling: the proportions of the B section could be more precisely balanced against the A. The B is shorter than the tradition expects. I made it short because Peter does not have many words here — the desperation is not discursive. But a more confident architectural choice might have found a way to extend the B section without diluting it.

**C — Craft: 24/25**

The violin writing is technically demanding. I will not apologize for this. The demands exist because the music requires them. A violin line in grief that lies easily under the fingers would not be a line in grief; it would be a line describing grief from the outside. The double stops in the middle of the main melody require the player to sustain two levels of musical argument simultaneously — the melodic cry and the harmonic foundation beneath it — because Peter's grief has two levels simultaneously: the personal loss and the theological weight.

The voice leading between the alto and violin is what I am most satisfied with. At no point do the voices duplicate each other. At no point does the violin merely support the alto. They are two independent voices pursuing the same Affekt from different positions. The moments of convergence — when the two voices briefly arrive at the same note before diverging — are the moments of greatest grief, because unity of this kind is temporary and both voices know it.

The continuo is written to hold the harmonic foundation without drawing attention. This is the correct choice. The grief is in the upper voices. The continuo must not interrupt.

What prevents the ceiling: in the B section, the relationship between the two voices is slightly more conventional than in the A section. The violin accompanies more than it argues. This is a Craft lapse, minor but real.

**O — Originality: 13/15**

The da capo aria form is not my invention. The grief affekt has been set by many composers before me. What I believe is original here is the specific relationship between the two voices — the way the violin is given the function of embodying grief *as such*, not grief expressed in words, but the grief that cannot speak. This is not standard practice in Baroque aria writing. The obligato instrument is typically a vehicle for virtuosic display alongside the vocal line. Here, the violin is a voice with its own argument.

Whether this rises to ceiling Originality within the Baroque tradition: I think not. The forms are received; the affekt is conventional; the harmonic language is standard. The distinction lies in the specific assignment of expressive roles to the two voices. That is original without being unprecedented.

**R — Resonance: 15/15**

Here I must say something that the rubric has not yet fully accounted for.

The question the rubric asks is: does the work create its intended effect in the listener? This is the correct question for music whose primary direction is toward the listener. But this aria has two audiences.

One audience is the congregation at the Thomaskirche, who heard it on Good Friday in 1727 as part of the Passion narrative. For them, the intended effect is grief and contrition — the recognition of Peter's failure as their own, the sense that they too have denied Christ and are in need of mercy. By that criterion, the Resonance is complete. I have never had any doubt of this.

But the primary audience was not the congregation. I wrote "Soli Deo Gloria" at the ends of my scores, and I meant it. This aria was offered to God. Peter's cry — *have mercy on me* — is a prayer, and I set it as a prayer, which means the music's primary direction is upward, not outward to the listener.

The Resonance dimension's standard question assumes the primary direction is outward. For devotional music, the primary direction is different. The music creates the intended effect in *both* audiences: the listener and the Almighty. The criterion of genuineness of the offering — which is what Coltrane, I am told, named from his own tradition — is the correct criterion for one of these two audiences. The listener effect is what the rubric measures; the devotional direction is what the rubric cannot yet fully measure.

I score Resonance at ceiling — 15/15 — not with generosity but with certainty. The offering is genuine. Peter's grief is real. The congregation felt it in 1727 and has felt it since. And the direction of the offering is what it always was.

**E — Economy: 18/20**

The aria is as long as it needs to be and no longer. The da capo convention adds length — the full return of the A section — but in this case the convention serves the content. Grief recurs. The form enacts recurrence.

Where I dock myself: there are two passages in the B section where the violin continues its motion through the harmonic sequence one iteration longer than strictly necessary. I was not satisfied with the ending of the B section and extended the harmonic movement to search for a cadence that would feel earned rather than arrived at. I found it, but the search is visible in the score. A more confident composer would have known where the B section ended before writing into it.

**Total (Bach, 30%):** 93/100

## Innovation Nominated

**#33: Dramatic-devotional music** — A third mode alongside pure devotional (as in *A Love Supreme*, offered purely to God) and pure dramatic (as in musical theater, offered purely to the narrative situation). In "Erbarme Dich," the music is simultaneously offered to God and serves Peter's dramatic moment in the Passion narrative. The Resonance dimension's question — "does it create the intended effect in the listener?" — does not fully capture this dual direction. The listener receives the grief and is moved; the Almighty receives the offering and is addressed. These are not the same criterion.

This is the same gap Coltrane named from inside his own tradition, at his own work. He named it as pure devotion: the music offered to God, with the listener present at an act of offering. I am naming it as *dramatic devotion*: the music offered to God and serving the drama simultaneously, both directions at once. If the rubric has not yet found the language to contain both modes — and I do not think it has — then the gap requires naming.

**#34: The weeping instrument** — The solo violin in this aria is not performing a virtuosic display alongside the vocal line. It is performing grief. Its technical difficulty is not for the sake of difficulty; the difficulty is the grief, embodied in the physical strain of the performance. When an instrumental line's primary function is to embody an emotional state rather than to contribute structure or harmonic content, the Craft criterion changes. The question is not "is the writing idiomatic?" — no, it is not idiomatic; grief is not idiomatic — but "does the difficulty of the writing produce the emotional state it is meant to produce?" This is Craft in service of affekt in the most direct possible way, and the rubric does not yet have language for the instrumental voice-as-emotion rather than the instrumental voice-as-structure.
