# Work Description: "Erbarme Dich, mein Gott" — Bach, BWV 244

## Narrative Context

*St. Matthew Passion* BWV 244 was first performed on Good Friday, April 11, 1727, at the Thomaskirche in Leipzig. The text is the Passion narrative from the Gospel of Matthew, supplemented by chorale settings and lyric poetry — the latter mostly by Picander (Christian Friedrich Henrici), a Leipzig postal official and poet who collaborated with Bach on many cantata texts.

"Erbarme Dich" comes near the end of Part I. Peter has followed Christ to the High Priest's courtyard. Three times he is recognized; three times he denies any knowledge of Jesus. The third denial has just occurred when the cock crows. Peter remembers Christ's prophecy ("Before the cock crows, you will deny me three times"). The text says: "And Peter went out and wept bitterly."

The recitative directly preceding the aria — "Erbarme dich" — is short and sets the scene. Then the aria begins.

## Musical Description

### Forces
- Alto voice (Peter)
- Solo violin obligato (the weeping)
- Continuo (cello, double bass, harpsichord/organ)

### Form
Da capo aria in binary form. Key: B minor. Duration: approximately 6-7 minutes.

- **A section:** "Erbarme dich, mein Gott, um meiner Zähren willen" — Have mercy on me, O God, for the sake of my tears. The alto voice and solo violin alternate and intertwine phrases. The violin does not merely accompany; it weeps. The violin's opening gesture — a falling phrase with chromatic neighbors — is itself a musical figure of grief that persists throughout.

- **B section:** "Schaue hier, Herz und Auge weint vor dir bitterlich" — Look here: heart and eye weep before you bitterly. Relative major, but the major does not console; it intensifies the appeal by moving toward something lighter that the grief immediately pulls back down.

- **Da capo:** Return to A section as written, with the performance convention that the singer adds ornaments reflecting what has been experienced in the B section.

### The Violin Writing

The solo violin part is among the most technically demanding in the Baroque repertoire:
- Extended double-stop passages requiring simultaneous melody and harmonic support
- Large position shifts in the middle of lyrical phrases
- Sustained intensity throughout: no rest passages, no neutral measures
- The difficulty is not for virtuosity's sake — every technical demand produces a specific expressive result; the strain of the playing embeds itself in the sound

The violin's role is not accompaniment. It is the voice of grief that cannot speak. The alto voice speaks the words; the violin speaks what the words cannot contain.

### Harmonic Language

The harmonic movement is organized through chains of descending fifths — B minor → E minor → A minor → D major → G major — each new key a new level of grief, each landing slightly further from home than expected. The sequences do not arrive at comfort; they arrive at new positions of supplication. This is not development in the classical sense; it is the music discovering, through harmonic movement, that there is no resolution available for this kind of grief.

### The Affekt

The single affect — grief, pleading, the quality of a human being exposed before God — is held without deviation for the entire duration. This is the Baroque doctrine of the Affekt in its purest application: one emotion, sustained to exhaustion, offered completely. Nothing is withheld. Nothing is moderated.

## The Devotional Dimension

This aria is simultaneously:

1. **Dramatically functional** — it follows Peter's denial and is his musical response; it serves the narrative of the Passion
2. **Devotionally directed** — it is offered as a prayer; it is not primarily addressed to the audience but upward; Peter's grief becomes the congregation's grief which becomes a collective offering

Bach described his purpose in composing the Passions as service to God and to the congregation's edification. The aria is not entertainment. It is not even primarily drama. It is offered.

This is the devotional-Resonance question that Cluster F names: when the music's primary direction is toward something larger than the listener — God, the transcendent, the sacred — the Resonance dimension's standard criterion ("intended effect on the listener") captures only part of what's happening.
