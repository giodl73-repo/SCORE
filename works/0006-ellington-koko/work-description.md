# Work Description: "Ko-Ko" (1940) — Duke Ellington

## The Opening: Brass Declaration and Blanton's Bass

The piece opens without introduction. Ellington is not interested in easing the listener in. The brass section attacks a unison figure in E-flat minor — a tight, staccato call-and-response between sections, two bars of brass and two bars of brass, each time answered by Blanton's walking bass in the spaces between. The minor mode is established immediately and without ambiguity: this is not the familiar major-key blues of the swing era. This is blues as darkness, as weight.

Jimmy Blanton's bass in these opening bars is already doing something no jazz bassist of 1940 was expected to do: it is melodically active, not merely timekeeping. The notes are chosen for their harmonic implication, their rhythmic phrasing against the brass figures. Blanton is in conversation with the brass section, not merely supporting it. Ellington knew this when he wrote the arrangement — or rather, when he heard Blanton and understood what Blanton made possible. The bass becomes a voice.

## Harry Carney's Baritone: Architecture as Sound

The most architecturally significant moment in "Ko-Ko" is Harry Carney's baritone saxophone entrance. This is not, in conventional jazz terms, a "solo" — the word suggests improvisation, individual expression emerging from a composed context. Carney's passage is composed, placed with exact knowledge of what Carney can do with a sustained low note, what weight he gives it, what vibrato, what control across the registers. Ellington wrote for a sound he knew as well as he knew his own voice.

The baritone, in this register, creates the gravitational center of the piece. Everything else in the Ellington orchestra — the trumpets, the trombones, the clarinets, the rhythm section — can move freely because Carney anchors them. He is the ground against which everything else is figure. This is an architectural function, not a featured-soloist function. Carney is not the star; Carney is the foundation. The distinction matters enormously for understanding what Ellington is doing.

The orchestration around Carney reinforces this: while he plays, the other voices reduce in texture and volume, creating space for his register to fill. This is not simply "give the soloist room to play." It is the calibration of the entire orchestral texture around one specific sound that Ellington knew precisely.

## The Through-Composed Architecture: What Sections Do

"Ko-Ko" proceeds through a series of orchestral sections that are not merely variations on the blues changes. Each section has a distinct texture:

**Section 1 (opening brass declaration):** Tutti brass against Blanton's bass. E-flat minor established as the tonal home. Dynamics: loud. Articulation: staccato. Character: authoritative.

**Section 2 (Carney baritone feature):** The low anchor. Sustained, resonant, orchestrally supported. The first tonal departure from the tight brass texture.

**Section 3 (muted brass development):** The brass sections take the material through muted voicings — Joe Nanton's plunger mute, Cootie Williams's mute, the sound transformed from the opening declaration into something more interior. The blues changes continue underneath.

**Section 4 (woodwind and saxophone trading):** The reed sections take over from the brass. The harmonic language becomes denser, the voicings more chromatic. Ben Webster's tenor is audible in the section but not as a featured soloist — he is part of the harmonic texture.

**Coda:** Brief, decisive, tonally affirmative. The piece does not resolve the minor blues into a major conclusion; it ends where it began, in E-flat minor, with the same assertion that opened it. The coda does not conclude. It affirms.

## Voice-Specific Composition: What It Means in Practice

To understand "Ko-Ko" technically, one must understand that Ellington composed the way a playwright writes for specific actors rather than for character types. He did not write "trombone" and mean any trombone; he wrote "Joe Nanton" and meant the specific sound that Nanton's plunger mute technique produced, a sound that was Nanton's and no one else's. He did not write "baritone saxophone" and mean any baritone; he wrote for Harry Carney's particular combination of physical size, breath capacity, and musical intelligence.

This compositional method has a specific technical consequence: the parts are not interchangeable. A modern jazz orchestra performing "Ko-Ko" can play the notes, but the notes were written to work with specific players' particular approaches — their articulation, their tone, their timing within a phrase. The notes without the players they were written for produce something correct but incomplete. Not wrong, exactly, but missing something that cannot be recovered.

This is unusual even among great orchestrators. Brahms wrote for the orchestra, and his parts can be played by any competent symphony orchestra with full realization. Ellington wrote for the Ellington orchestra, and his parts require the Ellington orchestra to achieve what he heard when he wrote them. The specificity is not a limitation of his compositional method; it is the compositional method.

## The Blues as Structure, Not Convention

"Ko-Ko" is a 12-bar minor blues. This means that every 12 bars, the harmonic pattern — i, iv, i, V7, i in E-flat minor, with variations — repeats. This is the scaffolding.

But the scaffolding is almost invisible under the orchestral architecture that Ellington has built on it. A listener who came to "Ko-Ko" expecting a blues — the directness, the vocal quality, the emotional legibility of the blues form in its natural environment — would encounter something strange and demanding. The blues here has been transmuted. The form is present; the spirit of the form is present in a different sense than it is present in a Muddy Waters recording or even a Count Basie blues performance. Ellington is using the blues as a formal container for an orchestral argument.

This creates what the panel will need to name: the blues as both anchor and departure point, as both honored tradition and compositionally exceeded form. The piece respects the blues by working within its changes; it transcends the blues by what it builds on those changes. Neither "respects" nor "transcends" fully captures what happens when the two are simultaneous.

## Economy: Three Minutes and Fifteen Seconds

"Ko-Ko" is three minutes and fifteen seconds long. This is not the length of a piece that is filling time. It is the length of a piece that has exactly as much to say as it can say in this formation, at this tempo, in this studio, on this day.

The economy is the economy of a working jazz orchestra: the budget of a single recording side (approximately three minutes of wax), the attention economy of a 1940 dance-hall audience (even one this piece was never designed for dancing), and the compositional economy of Ellington himself — a composer who worked fast, thought in specific sounds, and knew before he arrived at the studio exactly how many bars he needed.

The question for the panel is whether this economy is chosen or imposed. A five-minute "Ko-Ko" — one with more space for the orchestral sections to develop, with a longer Carney baritone passage, with a more extended development of the muted brass material — might have been possible. Whether it would have been better is a different question. The economy of the existing piece is complete, in the specific sense that the piece says what it came to say and stops.
