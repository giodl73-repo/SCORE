# Brief: "Ko-Ko" (1940) — Duke Ellington and His Orchestra

## Subject

"Ko-Ko" was recorded on March 6, 1940, at the RCA Victor studios in Chicago. Duration: approximately three minutes and fifteen seconds. Personnel: Duke Ellington (piano), Wallace Jones, Cootie Williams (trumpets), Rex Stewart (cornet), Lawrence Brown, Joe Nanton, Juan Tizol (trombones), Barney Bigard (clarinet), Johnny Hodges, Otto Hardwick (alto saxophones), Ben Webster (tenor saxophone), Harry Carney (baritone saxophone), Jimmy Blanton (bass), Sonny Greer (drums). Arranged and composed by Ellington.

The recording is a 12-bar minor blues in E-flat minor, expanded through orchestration into a through-composed suite of distinct textural sections. Not a blues with solos layered over changes, but a blues reimagined as orchestral architecture. The structure proceeds through a series of orchestral sections, each with its own voicing, texture, and rhythmic feel, unified by the underlying harmonic pattern and the organizational intelligence that comes from Ellington's specific knowledge of each player.

Harry Carney's baritone saxophone provides the anchoring low-register feature that gives the piece its gravitational center. Jimmy Blanton's bass — Blanton being the most technically advanced jazz bassist alive in 1940 — moves with the melodic independence of a horn rather than the timekeeping function of a rhythm section instrument. The piece does not feature a conventional improvised solo section: it is through-composed throughout, every note in every part placed by Ellington with specific knowledge of who would play it.

"Ko-Ko" is one of the earliest recordings by what jazz historians call the Ellington "Blanton-Webster band" — the configuration generally regarded as the finest orchestra Ellington led. The piece opens with a staccato, call-and-response brass tutti against Blanton's walking bass, proceeds through Harry Carney's baritone feature, develops the orchestral fabric through mute changes and section trading, and arrives at a brief coda that affirms rather than resolves.

## Audience

**Original audience:** 1940 swing-era listeners who had followed Ellington through his Cotton Club broadcasts. "Ko-Ko" registered as a departure — darker in mode, denser in texture, less accessible to dance-floor use than the popular Ellington. Record buyers who placed it on their turntables encountered something significantly more demanding than the popular image of the swing orchestra.

**Contemporary audience:** Jazz musicians, historians, and listeners who know "Ko-Ko" as a canonical statement of orchestral jazz at its most organized. It is cited as proof that jazz orchestral writing can achieve the formal discipline of the European orchestral tradition without surrendering its rhythmic and tonal identity. It is required listening in jazz education precisely because it demonstrates what separates arranged jazz from merely harmonized jazz.

## Tradition

The jazz orchestra tradition of 1940 was dominated by the swing band — Fletcher Henderson's arrangements, Benny Goodman's orchestra, Count Basie's rhythm-section-forward approach. Ellington occupied a strange position: commercially successful enough to tour continuously, but compositionally ambitious enough that critics debated whether his work was "real jazz" or something closer to composed concert music.

"Ko-Ko" sits at this intersection. It is built on the blues, but the blues here is almost unrecognizable in its original popular sense. The minor mode, the orchestral density, the through-composed character, the absence of conventional improvised solo choruses — all push toward the concert hall tradition Ellington was courting in 1940, while refusing to abandon the jazz foundation that made his music itself.

What the jazz-orchestra tradition demands: swing and groove; section writing in which brass, woodwind, and rhythm sections trade material with clarity; individual voice architecture that uses the specific capabilities of specific players; formal coherence that takes the music somewhere and brings it to arrival. Ellington meets all four demands and raises them. His section writing deploys specific players — not "the trombones" but Joe Nanton's growl-and-plunger mute contrasted with Lawrence Brown's legato. His sense of individual voice goes further: Harry Carney's baritone is not the lowest saxophone; it is a specific sound that Ellington spent years calibrating against all the other sounds in his orchestra.

## Core Question

**Is writing for irreplaceable specific players the highest form of orchestral Craft, or does it make the piece less universal?**

On one reading, Ellington's voice-specific method is the most sophisticated orchestrational strategy possible: rather than writing for "the trumpet section" and trusting that any competent trumpet section can realize the intention, you write for Cootie Williams's specific capability, knowing that what you write will be realized by precisely the voice you heard when you wrote it. The specificity is total.

On another reading, this specificity represents a failure of universality that distinguishes enduring compositions from historically interesting recordings. A Beethoven symphony survives because its architecture does not depend on any specific player. "Ko-Ko" cannot be played without loss by any ensemble other than the Ellington orchestra of March 1940. The piece is unrepeatable. Is that a feature or a limitation?

## Innovation Targets

**#13 — Craft-as-casting**: The compositional strategy of writing for specific named players rather than instrumental sections — treating the irreplaceable human voice as the primary unit of orchestral composition. Does the Craft dimension adequately account for a method whose excellence cannot be assessed without knowing both the written part and the specific player for whom it was written?

**#14 — Blues-as-simultaneously-honored-and-transcended**: The structural use of the blues form in a context that both depends on and departs from the blues tradition — where the 12-bar harmonic pattern is present but submerged under orchestral complexity that would be unrecognizable to a Delta blues audience. How does the rubric assess a work that uses a traditional form as scaffolding while converting that form into something the tradition itself would not recognize?

## Open Questions

1. Can the recording be the work? In jazz, the recording often is the primary text. "Ko-Ko" survives as a recording; the score is a reconstruction. If we accept the recording as the work, then the work is inextricably tied to these players, this studio, this March day in 1940. What does it mean to score a work whose primary text is also its definitive and unrepeatable performance?

2. What is the blues here? Ellington deploys the minor blues form, but the blues' traditional function — as a vessel for emotional directness — is transmuted by orchestral sophistication. The orchestra distances the listener from the blues' directness even as it deploys the blues' harmonic language. Is this transmutation a gain or a loss?

3. Harry Carney's baritone as architecture. Carney's sound is not merely a featured instrument; it is the gravitational anchor of the entire orchestral texture. Without Carney — or more precisely, without Carney's specific sound — the balance of the piece shifts in ways that cannot be compensated. Is this the composition's highest achievement or its vulnerability?

4. The absence of conventional improvisation. "Ko-Ko" is through-composed. In 1940 jazz, through-composition in a commercial recording is unusual. What is gained and what is lost by this choice? Does it place "Ko-Ko" more in the concert music tradition — and if so, should it be scored against jazz criteria or concert music criteria?
