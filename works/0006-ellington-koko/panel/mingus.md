# Mingus reviews "Ko-Ko" (1940)

**Step 0 — Structural approach: DEVELOPMENTAL (blues-based jazz orchestra)**
Blues changes as developmental scaffold. The harmonic pattern returns every twelve bars; the orchestration develops around it. Not development in the European symphonic sense, but development in the jazz-compositional sense: each return of the changes finds the orchestra in a different textural relationship to the same harmonic foundation.

Let me say first what this piece is not. It is not a jazz orchestra arrangement in the usual sense of the word. Most jazz orchestra arrangements are delivery systems for improvised solos — the head tells you the tune, the solos tell you what the players have to say about the tune, the head comes back so you know it's over. There is nothing wrong with this structure. I have used it myself. But Ellington is doing something different with "Ko-Ko," and if you hear it as a conventional head-solos-head you will miss what is actually happening.

What is actually happening is that Ellington is building an orchestral argument about what the blues can carry. Not proving it in the way a mathematician proves a theorem. Demonstrating it in the way a man demonstrates that he can carry something heavy — by picking it up and carrying it.

**Structure:** The architecture develops through textural contrast, and the architecture is correct in its proportions. The opening brass declaration establishes the authority of the piece before the first solo moment arrives. Carney's baritone provides the gravitational center. The muted brass development transforms the opening declaration into interior reflection. The piece knows where it is going.

My concern — and it earns a three-point dock — is that the structure is shaped more by the available forces and the available time than by what the blues content requires. The 78-rpm constraint was not Duke's fault, but it was a constraint, and I can hear the places where the music needed more room and didn't have it. The muted brass section, in particular, could have gone deeper. It arrived at something and then had to stop because the side was full. A structural dock is appropriate even when the cause was external, because the result on the recording is real.

**Craft:** I want to return to the question of what Craft means when you're writing for specific people. The standard Craft assessment asks: are the voice leading choices correct? Are the harmonic choices precise? Are the textures balanced? All of these apply here, and Ellington passes all of them with distinction. The voice leading in the ensemble passages is immaculate. The harmonic language of E-flat minor is deployed with complete control — the borrowed chords, the chromatic passing tones, the moment where the harmony lifts before returning to tonic — all of this is precise.

But there is an additional dimension that the standard Craft assessment does not capture, and I want to name it directly because I think it matters for the rubric: Ellington's craft includes the casting. He chose to put Harry Carney on the baritone anchor because he knew what Carney would do with it. This is compositional craft, not just orchestrational craft. It is the same thing a playwright does when they write a role for a specific actor — writing to the actor's strengths, their particular way of being present, rather than writing for a type. Whether the rubric's Craft dimension can assess this kind of casting knowledge is a question I will leave for the SUMMARY to address. I score Craft 24 because the standard criteria are met at a very high level, and because the casting is right even if I am not sure the rubric has a category for it.

**Originality:** Duke had absorbed the European orchestral tradition, the blues tradition, the swing tradition, and his own decade of experimentation with through-composed jazz, and "Ko-Ko" is the synthesis of all four at a moment when he happened to have the best orchestra he ever led. That is not small. But what I want to know — what I always want to know — is what this music is telling a truth about. And the answer is: it is telling a truth about the blues as a container that can hold more than people thought it could. That is honest and important and real. I dock two points because the truth the music tells is about form and possibility rather than about something harder and more specific. The blues can carry this much weight. Yes. Now tell me what the weight is. Duke almost gets there. "Ko-Ko" almost gets there.

**Resonance:** The piece hits. The opening brass declaration is an event. Carney's entrance is an event. The closing affirmation is an event. The emotional register — dark, authoritative, serious — is achieved and sustained throughout the three minutes and fifteen seconds. I score 14. The near-miss of the top score is that the piece is so architecturally controlled, so precisely orchestrated, that it sometimes gets in its own way emotionally. The architecture is the communication, but at moments the architecture becomes slightly more visible than the communication. This is a small complaint about a large achievement.

**Economy:** The through-composition means every moment is placed. Nothing happens by accident. But three minutes and fifteen seconds for what this piece is trying to do is not enough, and I'm going to dock three points to say so directly. The brevity is not economy — it is constraint. Economy would mean the piece is exactly as long as it needs to be. "Ko-Ko" needs to be longer. It is short because of the recording medium, not because the music is done. The difference matters.

Duke Ellington is the only person who could have made this record on this day. That is not a sentimental statement about the specialness of historical moments. It is a factual statement about what was in the room: Blanton, Carney, Nanton, and Ellington's knowledge of all of them. Everything else is commentary.
