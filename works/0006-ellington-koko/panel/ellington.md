# Ellington reviews "Ko-Ko" (1940)

**Step 0 — Structural approach: DEVELOPMENTAL (blues-based jazz orchestra)**
The 12-bar minor blues functions as the repeating harmonic scaffold on which a series of distinct orchestral sections develop. Each section represents a textural transformation of the same underlying harmonic material. The structure develops through orchestration rather than thematic development in the European sense — the development IS the voicing, the muting, the registration, the specific sound of each player as deployed in each section. Structure and orchestration are the same thing here.

I will speak about this piece as carefully as I can, because it is mine and I am not a detached observer. But the SCORE framework requires honesty, and the honest account of "Ko-Ko" begins here: this is the piece where I found out what my orchestra could do at its best.

**Structure:** The architecture is the orchestration. This needs to be said clearly, because someone trained in the European tradition might look at "Ko-Ko" and say: where is the development? Where is the recapitulation? Where is the structural argument that one section leads necessarily to the next? These questions are not irrelevant, but they are asking for something that the piece is not doing. The piece is doing something else: it is using orchestral texture as the structural argument. Each section is a different way of listening to the same blues. The structure is a tour of the orchestral possibilities of this particular piece, with these particular players, at this particular moment. Every section leads necessarily to the next because I heard it that way, with these people, and could not have heard it any other way.

I dock two points for the same reason I always dock points when I review my own work: not everything that is in the piece needed to be there. The muted brass development could have gone one chorus further. The coda could have built more. The three-minute constraint of the 78 rpm side was not a creative choice; it was a commercial constraint, and it closed some doors that might have been opened. Within what it is, the architecture is right. As a complete statement of what the piece could have been, it is very good but not complete.

**Craft:** I want to name the innovation directly. Craft in orchestral writing is normally assessed against how well the composer has deployed the available instrumental forces. This is not wrong. But for the music I compose — for the music I have spent fifty years composing — the forces are not "available" in the generic sense. The forces are Harry Carney and Jimmy Blanton and Joe Nanton and Cootie Williams. They are not interchangeable with "baritone saxophone," "bass," "trombone," "trumpet." They are specific people with specific sounds, and I composed to their sounds.

This is a different kind of Craft. It is harder, in one sense, because you cannot simply write the correct voice leading and trust that it will work. You have to write the correct voice leading for this specific voice, knowing that the voice has properties — a personal tone, a particular attack, a characteristic phrase-ending — that are not in any textbook. I know Harry Carney's breathing capacity, his vibrato, his particular weight in the low register. I wrote to that. No one else can play what I wrote for Harry and produce what I heard when I wrote it.

I dock one point because there are moments where I hear, in retrospect, that I could have been more specific. Not wrong — not generic — but less precisely calibrated to the person than I knew I could be. The Craft is very high. It is not everything it could have been.

**Originality:** The blues in E-flat minor, through-composed, for a named orchestra of specific players, with no conventional improvised solo section, opening with a brass declaration that is as far from a dance-hall swing tune as anything I recorded in 1940. This is not the work of a man who was working within jazz conventions. This is the work of a man who had absorbed jazz conventions completely enough to build something from them that those conventions did not anticipate. I will score Originality 14 because the form — the blues, the orchestra — is inherited. What I did with it is not. The highest Originality score would require that I had found a way to say something the tradition had no words for at all. This piece says something the tradition had not found yet. That is close to the top but not quite there.

**Resonance:** The piece achieves what it set out to achieve. The darkness of the minor mode, the weight of Carney's baritone, the authority of the opening brass declaration, the density of the through-composed architecture — all of this creates a specific emotional effect: seriousness, weight, the feeling of an orchestra that knows exactly what it is doing and has something to say. This is not music for dancing. It is music for listening. The listener who opens to it will find it opening to them. I score 14.

**Economy:** Three minutes and fifteen seconds. Within the commercial constraint, everything present earns its place. Nothing in the arrangement is filling time. But the commercial constraint was real, and I dock two points to acknowledge what is not there. A longer "Ko-Ko" — one not bound by the 78 rpm side — might have been more than very good. Might have been complete. Within what it is, the economy is high. Against what it could have been, there is absence.

One final note, for the panel. People sometimes ask me whether it troubles me that no other orchestra can play "Ko-Ko" the way my orchestra played it. I find the question confused. A piece of music is not more valuable because anyone can play it. A piece of music that is precisely what it is — precisely these sounds, this organization, these people — is doing something that a piece written for "an orchestra" cannot do. Universality is overrated. Specificity is everything.
