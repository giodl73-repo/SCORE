# Music Theorist reviews "Ko-Ko" (1940)

**Step 0 — Structural approach: DEVELOPMENTAL (blues-based jazz orchestra)**
Twelve-bar minor blues progression as repeating harmonic framework; each repetition deploys a distinct orchestral texture. The development is textural-orchestrational rather than motivic-thematic. Formal analysis must account for this tradition's grammar, which does not require motivic development in the European sense.

**Structure:** The 12-bar E-flat minor blues changes form a repeating harmonic period — I iv i V7 i, with chromatic alterations characteristic of the Ellington harmonic vocabulary. Each repetition of the period receives a distinct orchestral treatment. This is a standard jazz-orchestra formal approach, and Ellington executes it with precision.

What distinguishes "Ko-Ko" structurally from other blues arrangements is the absence of a conventional solo section. The standard jazz-orchestra arrangement of this period allocates several chorus repetitions for improvised solos over the changes; "Ko-Ko" substitutes through-composed orchestral textures in those positions. The structural implication is significant: the piece's architecture is determined entirely by the composer's arrangement decisions rather than by the interplay of composed and improvised materials. Every structural decision was made before the recording session. This produces a formal integrity and predictability that differs from jazz forms with improvised components.

The structural weaknesses, such as they are, involve proportion. The through-composed sections are unequal in their degree of development. The Carney baritone feature, which functions as the piece's structural center of gravity, is shorter than its architectural function warrants. The muted brass development section is longer than its motivic content requires. These proportional imbalances are real, though not severe. I dock three points.

**Craft:** I will focus on the specific technical choices.

The E-flat minor tonality is maintained with rigor throughout. The harmonic language is consistent with the tradition's grammar: the minor tonic, the subdominant (A-flat minor), the dominant seventh (B-flat7), and characteristic Ellington chromatic alterations — raised elevenths, flat ninths, major sevenths — are deployed with consistent intentionality. There are no harmonic contradictions, no moments where the voicing produces an unintended dissonance.

The voice leading in the ensemble passages deserves specific attention. In the brass section voicings, Ellington consistently maintains contrary motion between the outer voices — the bass voice moving one direction while the soprano brass voice moves the other — producing smooth harmonic motion even through the chromatic alterations. This is correct by the tradition's voice-leading grammar and is achieved without the stiffness that characterizes less sophisticated arrangers who apply contrary-motion rules mechanically.

Blanton's bass line merits specific analysis. In the opening brass declaration, Blanton's line is not a conventional jazz walking bass — quarter notes on the chord tones in rotation. It is a composed melodic line that moves by both step and leap, creating a counterpoint to the brass that is melodically independent rather than merely supportive. The bass and the brass are in genuine melodic dialogue. This is a technically advanced orchestrational choice, and it works.

One technical observation that edges toward the innovation the brief identifies: the parts are calibrated to specific players in ways that require knowledge of individual player characteristics to assess fully. Carney's part, for example, exploits the lowest register of the baritone saxophone — notes that require Carney's specific breath support and embouchure control to produce cleanly at the intended dynamic. A baritone saxophone part written in this register by a less attentive arranger would produce muddy, uncontrolled tone; Ellington's knowledge of Carney's capacity makes the part idiomatic in a hyperspecific sense. This is technically interesting and points toward the Craft-as-casting innovation the panel has been asked to explore.

I score Craft 24. One point docked for the minor proportion issue in the Section 3 muted brass passage, where the harmonic rhythm slows in a way that creates a slight staleness before the next textural change arrives.
