# Bach reviews "Ko-Ko" (1940)

**Step 0 — Structural approach: DEVELOPMENTAL (blues-based jazz orchestra)**
The 12-bar minor blues functions as a cantus firmus — a repeating structural foundation over which varied harmonizations and textures are deployed. The development is textural and orchestrational rather than thematic and motivic. I recognize this structure, though not its specific content. My own chorale preludes work by embedding a pre-existing melody as scaffolding and building elaborate counterpoint around it. Ellington has done something analogous: embedded the blues changes as scaffolding and built orchestral architecture around them.

I approach "Ko-Ko" as I approach any music composed for forces I did not myself write for: with careful attention to the tradition's own grammar rather than the imposition of my grammar. The jazz orchestra of 1940 is not the Baroque ensemble of Leipzig. What constitutes correct voice leading in one context is not identical to what constitutes correct voice leading in the other. I will therefore attempt to apply the tradition's own standards, supplemented by observations about where those standards appear to succeed or fall short.

**Structure:** The architecture of "Ko-Ko" is the architecture of the cantus firmus variation — one of my preferred forms, though I arrived at it from a different direction than Ellington. The blues changes function as the cantus firmus: they repeat in fixed harmonic sequence, and each repetition is given a different orchestral texture. This is structurally coherent and well-suited to the material. The through-composed character — the absence of improvised sections — creates a formal integrity that I find admirable. One does not expect an improvised digression in the middle of a chorale prelude.

My concerns: the structural proportions are constrained by the recording medium in a way that prevents full development. The cantus firmus variation form, in my own practice, gains its power through extended exploration — the forty-five variations of the Goldberg, the thirty measures of the Crab Canon, the elaborate architecture of the WTC preludes, which vary from sixteen bars to long developmental essays. "Ko-Ko" is three minutes and fifteen seconds. It establishes its structural principle clearly, develops it through textural contrast, and arrives at an affirmative conclusion. What it does not do is exhaust the principle. A piece that earns the full top Structure score should have explored the formal logic as far as the formal logic permits. Three minutes does not permit this. I dock four points accordingly.

**Craft:** The voice leading in the ensemble passages is precise and purposeful. The minor mode is handled with consistent command — the borrowed harmonies are correctly prepared and resolved within the jazz-orchestra grammar. The orchestration demonstrates what I can only call a craftsman's knowledge of individual voice capabilities: not merely "what can a baritone saxophone do" but "what does this particular baritone saxophone player do with this particular melodic shape." This is sophisticated work.

I dock two points for two specific observations. First: the relationship between the bass voice (Blanton) and the inner voices is occasionally thick in the lower registers in a way that, in my own writing, I would have clarified. This is partly a tradition-specific observation — jazz orchestra voicings differ from chorale voicings — but even within the jazz-orchestra tradition, there are moments in the brass sections where I hear a density that serves the emotional effect more than the harmonic precision. Second: the motivic relationship between the opening brass figure and subsequent sections could have been more systematically developed. Ellington chooses textural contrast over motivic development. This is a legitimate choice; it is not the choice I would have made; it leaves something available that was not pursued.

**Originality:** The blues form is ancient by the standards of this tradition; the orchestral elaboration of the blues is something Ellington had been refining for fifteen years before 1940. What is original in "Ko-Ko" specifically — as distinguished from what is original in the Ellington orchestra project generally — is the combination of through-composition with blues form, the specific use of the baritone saxophone as architectural anchor rather than featured soloist, and the austere minor-mode blues in a tradition dominated by major-key or more conventional blues treatments. This is original within its tradition. It does not say something the tradition had no words for at all; it says something the tradition could have said but had not said this way.

**Resonance:** The intended effect — weight, seriousness, orchestral authority in the minor mode — is achieved. The piece communicates what it intends to communicate. The darkness of E-flat minor is felt. The authority of the brass declaration is felt. Carney's baritone is felt as a presence that anchors the listener's physical and emotional position relative to the music. I score 13; the minor deduction reflects the moments where orchestral architecture becomes slightly more visible than the emotional communication it is meant to carry.

**Economy:** Within the constraint of the recording medium, very high economy. Nothing is wasted. The coda says exactly what it needs to say and stops. The transitions between sections are efficient. I dock two points because I believe a longer treatment would have been more economical in the deeper sense — that the music contains more than three minutes can explore, and the unexplored portion represents a deficit in the work's realization of its own potential. Brevity forced by external constraint is not economy; it is compression. The difference matters.

A final note on voice-specific composition: when I composed for specific court musicians in Cöthen or for the singers and instrumentalists of the Leipzig churches, I was also composing for known voices. I wrote concertos for specific instrumentalists; I calibrated aria ranges to specific singers. This is not remarkable in itself. What Ellington has done is make this compositional method visible — thematically central — in a way that most composers, including myself, have not. The specific voice is not merely the vehicle for the composition in "Ko-Ko"; it is the subject of the composition. This interests me considerably, and I am not certain the rubric has a category for it.
