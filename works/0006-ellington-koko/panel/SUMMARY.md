## 2. Gate Status

**Gate: PASS**

Score 90.3 is within the expected range (86-91) and well above the advisory threshold (60). The panel affirms "Ko-Ko" as a canonical-import work of high distinction: a jazz orchestra composition that achieves its structural goals with exceptional craft, at the cost of brevity imposed by the recording medium and the unresolvable tension between voice-specificity and universality.

---

## 3. Key Agreements

**High Craft consensus (23–24).** All four reviewers who assessed Craft placed it in the 22–25 anchor band, with three at 24 and Bach at 23. The technical execution — voice leading, harmonic control, orchestral balance, the specific use of Blanton's melodic bass — is excellent by the tradition's own standards.

**Structural brevity concern (unanimous).** All five reviewers docked Structure points for the same underlying reason: the 78-rpm recording side (approximately 3:15) imposed a time constraint that prevented full development of the piece's architectural potential. This is the panel's single most consistent observation. The structure is well-organized within what it is; what it is is shorter than the formal logic warrants.

**Resonance high but architecturally mediated.** Both Ellington (14) and Mingus (14) score Resonance near the top, while Bach (13) docks one point for the moments where orchestral architecture becomes more visible than the emotional communication it carries. The minor deduction is small but reflects a genuine observation: the piece is so carefully organized that it occasionally reads as organized rather than as felt.

**Economy docked for external constraint.** Ellington (18) and Bach (18) both score Economy high but below maximum, while Mingus (17) docks more heavily. The shared reasoning: within the available time, nothing is wasted; the available time was not chosen by the composer but imposed by the commercial medium; the distinction between economy and compression matters for how we assess the piece's relationship to its own potential.

## 4. Key Tensions

**Tension 1: Ellington + Mingus vs. Bach on voice-specific Craft.**

Ellington and Mingus both name voice-specific composition as the highest possible Craft achievement in jazz orchestra writing — Ellington as a practitioner defending his method, Mingus as a practitioner who absorbed and extended the method. Both score Craft at 24, acknowledging the near-perfection of the technical execution while withholding the top point for minor proportion issues.

Bach scores Craft 23 — one point lower — and his reasoning illuminates the gap. He recognizes voice-specific composition as a legitimate and admirable craft strategy; he notes that he himself composed for known instrumentalists and singers. But he argues that the standard Craft assessment cannot fully evaluate voice-specific composition without information it does not have: the specific capabilities of the specific players for whom the parts were written. He scores what he can assess (voice leading, harmonic control, orchestration within the tradition's grammar) while noting the gap.

The Music Theorist scores Craft 24 and observes the same gap as Bach: the parts are calibrated to specific player characteristics that require biographical and acoustic knowledge to assess fully. This is the Craft-as-casting innovation (Innovation #13) — named by the brief and confirmed by the panel.

**Resolution:** The panel agrees that Craft-as-casting is real, consequential, and not currently captured by the rubric's Craft dimension. The Craft anchors assess technical execution against the tradition's grammar; they do not assess the compositional strategy of calibrating parts to irreplaceable individual players. This is a rubric gap. Candidate for innovation logging.

**Tension 2: Is the brevity a structural failure or an external imposition?**

Ellington acknowledges that the 78-rpm constraint closed compositional doors; he docks Structure two points. Mingus docks three, arguing that the piece "needs to be longer" in a way that is independent of the medium constraint — that the through-composed form required more development to make its argument fully. Bach docks four, applying the cantus firmus variation standard that expects systematic exploration of the formal logic's full possibilities.

The Historian notes the historical context: the 78-rpm side was the commercial reality of 1940 recording, not a creative choice. This does not make the brevity disappear from the recording that exists, but it contextualizes the dock appropriately. A dock for brevity imposed by commercial constraint is different from a dock for brevity chosen by the composer.

**Resolution:** The panel accepts that the structural constraint was externally imposed and notes it. The dock remains because the SCORE framework assesses the work that exists, not the work that might have existed under different conditions. The dock is held at appropriate levels (2-4 points, not higher) in recognition of the external cause.

**Tension 3: Blues honored or blues transcended?**

Ellington (14) and Mingus (13) diverge slightly on Originality, with the gap reflecting their different views on the blues-as-simultaneously-honored-and-transcended dynamic.

Ellington's position: the piece honors the blues by working within its changes and transcends it by what it builds on those changes. Both are genuine. The originality is in the synthesis.

Mingus's position: the piece tells a truth about what the blues form can carry — orchestral argument, textural density, harmonic sophistication — but does not yet tell us what the weight is. The form proves it can hold more; the piece does not fully fill it with something specific enough to qualify for the highest Originality anchor.

The Historian scores Originality 13, noting that the originations are real and consequential for jazz history but that no single element of "Ko-Ko" is wholly without precedent; the originality is in the specific combination and realization.

**Resolution:** Panel converges at 12–14 for Originality, with Ellington at the high end (14) and Bach at the low end (12). The 12–14 range reflects a genuine achievement in saying something within the blues-orchestra tradition that had not been said this way, without reaching the highest anchor of saying something the tradition had no words for at all. Blues-as-simultaneously-honored-and-transcended (Innovation #14) is logged.

## 6. Cluster Analysis

**No new cluster triggered by "Ko-Ko."**

Innovation #13 (Craft-as-casting) is a strong candidate for future cluster formation if a second work in the corpus demonstrates voice-specific composition as the primary compositional method. Candidates: any work that Ellington is known to have composed specifically for named players (Concerto for Cootie, 1940); any work where the gap between the written part and the specific player's realization is demonstrably the central artistic question.

Innovation #14 (Blues-as-simultaneously-honored-and-transcended) is a category observation rather than a cluster seed. It names a structural phenomenon — the use of traditional form as scaffolding for a compositional argument the tradition would not recognize — that may recur in other blues-based or tradition-based compositions. Whether this warrants a cluster depends on future panel sessions.

Cluster C note: "Ko-Ko" is through-composed rather than improvised. It does not advance Cluster C (Improvised Music, seeded at work 0005). The panel notes this and observes that the corpus now contains one through-composed jazz orchestra work (0006) and one bebop improvisation (0005), which together bracket the composed-improvised spectrum in jazz.

## 7. Design Recommendations

**Recommendation 1: Annotate the Craft dimension for voice-specific composition.**
Suggested annotation: "In works where the compositional method is calibrated to specific named players rather than instrumental sections — where the irreplaceable individual voice is the primary compositional unit — Craft should be assessed both against the tradition's general grammar and against the composer's demonstrated knowledge of the specific player's capabilities. The latter cannot be assessed from the score alone; it requires knowledge of the specific performance context."

**Recommendation 2: Acknowledge medium-imposed brevity in Economy assessment.**
The 78-rpm recording side was a commercial constraint in 1940 jazz. Future assessments of recordings from this period should note when brevity is medium-imposed rather than compositionally chosen, and calibrate Economy scores accordingly — docking for the constraint while acknowledging it as external.

**Recommendation 3: Blues-as-scaffolding as a distinct formal category.**
The use of traditional forms as structural scaffolding for compositional arguments the originating tradition would not recognize is a recurring phenomenon in 20th-century music. The rubric's Originality dimension does not currently distinguish between conventional use of a traditional form and use of a traditional form as raw structural material for a different argument. A note in the Originality anchors may be appropriate.
