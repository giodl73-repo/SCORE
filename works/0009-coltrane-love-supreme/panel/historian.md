# Historian on "A Love Supreme, Part I: Acknowledgement"

## Historical Placement

December 9, 1964. The location is specific: Van Gelder Studio, Englewood Cliffs, New Jersey — Rudy Van Gelder's studio, where the most important jazz recordings of the post-war period were made. Van Gelder engineered the Blue Note catalog, the Prestige catalog, and beginning in 1961, the Impulse! catalog. He understood how to capture a jazz quartet in a room. The sonic environment of *A Love Supreme* is Van Gelder's environment, and it is the right environment for this music.

The historical moment is equally specific. In December 1964, the jazz avant-garde is fracturing. The year before, Ornette Coleman had released *The Shape of Jazz to Come* (1959) and *Free Jazz* (1960), establishing the free-improvisation position. Albert Ayler was moving further into collective abstraction. The question dividing the jazz world is: is technical mastery a precondition for transcendence, or is it an obstacle? Coleman's position (and increasingly Ayler's) is that the vocabulary of bebop and post-bop harmony constrains the music and must be abandoned. The position Coltrane represents — and *A Love Supreme* is its clearest statement — is that technique must be mastered before it can be transcended.

*A Love Supreme* is simultaneously a document of mastery (the harmonic sophistication of the quartet, Coltrane's complete command of the tenor, the formal architecture of the suite) and a document of transcendence (the devotional purpose, the simplicity of the four-note motif, the vocal quality Coltrane reaches in the improvised passages). It argues, through its existence, that the mastery-versus-transcendence debate is a false choice.

## Tradition Placement

*A Love Supreme* is not in a tradition when it is recorded; it is in the process of creating one. The tradition it creates — spiritual jazz — does not have a name in December 1964. What it has is predecessors: Ellington's sacred concerts (the first was 1965, but Ellington's sacred intentions were already embedded in his compositional practice throughout the 1950s), the influence of John Coltrane's engagement with Ravi Shankar's raga tradition (beginning in earnest around 1961-62), and the broader post-bop search for musical form adequate to spiritual experience.

The four-part suite format has precedents. Bach's keyboard suites, Ellington's *Black, Brown and Beige* (1943) as the grandest jazz precedent for multi-movement structure with a programmatic purpose. But the devotional suite in jazz — not a suite that performs spirituality for an audience, but a suite that IS an act of devotion — does not have a clear predecessor. Ellington's sacred concerts are closer, but they are explicitly performance events, designed for a concert audience in a sacred space. *A Love Supreme* is a recording-studio document: four musicians making an offering, and the recording is the offering.

## Contribution to Tradition

*A Love Supreme* did three things to jazz tradition that matter historically:

First, it established the formal viability of the devotional suite. After *A Love Supreme*, it is possible to make music in jazz whose primary purpose is spiritual offering rather than artistic demonstration. The form exists. Musicians who came after — Keith Jarrett, Pharoah Sanders, Alice Coltrane — all worked in a formal space that Part I of *A Love Supreme* opened.

Second, it demonstrated the expressive range of the post-bop quartet. The quartet of Coltrane, Tyner, Garrison, and Jones is the most coherent ensemble unit of the early 1960s jazz period. *A Love Supreme* is their summative statement, and it demonstrates what a small ensemble with complete collective understanding can achieve. The subsequent history of jazz quartet music — through Keith Jarrett's Standards Trio, through the Marsalis brothers' bands — is inflected by this demonstration.

Third, it provided a formal argument against the abandonment of technique in the service of freedom. By showing that mastery and transcendence are not in conflict — that the four-note motif is the simplest possible expression achievable because the technique is so fully mastered that it can be set aside when simplicity is required — *A Love Supreme* made a historical argument that influenced how the technical-vs-freedom debate was resolved.

## Originality Assessment: 14/15

By historical standards: what was available to Coltrane in 1964 that he did not use? The bebop harmonic vocabulary he had developed in the previous decade is present but subordinated to the modal framework. The Ellington precedent for multi-movement jazz is acknowledged but not followed formally. The raga tradition's devotional frame is integrated but not borrowed from directly.

What Coltrane did that had not been done in this combination: the twelve-key transposition as a structural universality argument; the recording-studio offering (not a live sacred concert but a studio act of creation offered upward rather than outward); the integration of the simplest possible motif (four notes, four syllables) with the most sophisticated improvisational tradition available in 1964.

One point off: the devotional-jazz precedent (Ellington) is closer than Coltrane's position implies. Ellington had been making sacred music for twenty years. The form was available. What Coltrane added was the studio-recording-as-offering framing and the twelve-key structural argument, both of which are genuinely new. The combination is original; the concept of devotional jazz composition is not originated by Coltrane.

## Historical Note: The Legacy

*A Love Supreme* was received with near-universal critical recognition at the time of its release in January 1965. *Down Beat* gave it five stars. It sold widely by jazz standards. It was performed in European cathedrals. A religious community in San Francisco — the Saint John Will-I-Am Coltrane African Orthodox Church, founded in 1969 — uses Coltrane's music as liturgy and treats him as a saint.

This reception history is evidence for the Resonance dimension: the music has functioned as a devotional object for communities of listeners who receive it as an offering. The intended effect — the experience of being present at an act of devotion — has been received across sixty years by audiences who did not know Coltrane personally and were not present at the December 1964 recording. That the offering has been received across this distance and duration is historical confirmation of its Resonance.
