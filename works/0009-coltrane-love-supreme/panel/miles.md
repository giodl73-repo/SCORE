# Miles Davis on "A Love Supreme, Part I: Acknowledgement"

## Opening

I have complicated feelings about this music.

Not about the music itself — the music is right. I have complicated feelings about being asked to review it. Coltrane worked with me. He found his voice in my bands. When he left to form the quartet, I watched what happened with *A Love Supreme* and I knew something had been completed that does not have a sequel. Not for him. For the music that we were all trying to make.

I will be fair and I will be specific. That is the only way to review music you care about.

## Structure: 22/25

The structure is built around three decisions: the four-note motif, the twelve-key transposition, and the spoken word at the end. These three decisions are the architecture. Everything else is execution within an architecture that has been correctly set.

The twelve-key transposition is where I want to spend some time. I know what the conventional analysis would say: this is not development; it is repetition of a motif at different pitch levels; it goes on for five minutes; that is too long. The conventional analysis is wrong.

What Coltrane is doing with the twelve-key transposition is a structural argument, not a structural process. The argument: this motif belongs to all keys. To make the argument, you visit all twelve. There is no shortcut. There is no way to demonstrate that a motif is universal by playing it in three keys. You play it in twelve or you do not make the claim.

The structure is correct. Three points off, not for the transposition, but for the closing section after the spoken words. Once the words have been spoken, the structural argument is complete. The closing collective improvisation is excellent music but it is structurally past the end of what needed to be said. The argument concluded; the music continued.

## Craft: 22/25

I want to say something about improvised Craft that needs to be said.

The rubric's Craft dimension was written for composed music. I know this because I have watched it struggle with improvised music. Ko-Ko was the first case. This is the second. Each time an improvised work comes before a panel, someone has to say: the criteria for composed Craft are the wrong criteria for improvised Craft. The criteria for improvised Craft are: can you navigate the harmonic terrain at tempo without hesitation? Can you maintain your physical control of the instrument throughout? Does your improvised line have shape and logic that you found in the moment rather than planned in advance?

These are different from the composed-Craft questions. They are not inferior questions. They are different questions. The rubric needs to say this, and it needs to say it permanently, not through the side door of panel notes.

Coltrane's improvised sections in Part I: motor execution across five minutes — controlled, precise, in full command of the instrument at every dynamic and in every register. Harmonic navigation across twelve transpositions in real time — correct, responsive, finding the right notes for each new key without hesitation. Melodic logic — the arc of the improvisation builds and releases in a shape that was found in performance, not written in advance.

Three points off: there are moments where the improvisation settles into familiar patterns — the "sheets of sound" approach that Coltrane had developed by 1964 — rather than searching for the new phrase. I have seen Coltrane when he is fully inside the search, when every phrase is genuinely found. This recording has that quality most of the time. Not all of the time.

## Originality: 13/15

The twelve-key transposition is original. The devotional purpose integrated structurally at this level is original. The combination of these two is original.

What is not fully original: the modal framework underneath everything is Miles Davis's modal framework. *Kind of Blue* is five years earlier. Coltrane was on that record. He knows where the modal language came from.

This is not a diminishment. Coltrane took what I gave him and did something with it that I could not have done and did not do. The twelve-key transposition is not a Miles Davis compositional idea. The spiritual integration at this depth is not a Miles Davis compositional idea. He went further than I showed him how to go, and the place he went is his alone.

Two points off for the inheritance.

## Resonance: 13/15

The music creates the specific experience of being present at something directed elsewhere. This is the exact effect it intends — to make the listener feel that they are witnessing an offering rather than receiving a performance. The music achieves this. For a listener who is open to it, the experience is of witnessing rather than receiving. That is the correct intended effect, and it arrives.

Two points off: I am not a religious person in the way Coltrane was a religious person. I find the devotional frame limiting in a way I cannot fully set aside. The music is extraordinary. The devotional purpose is, for me, not a necessary component of what makes it extraordinary. I am scoring Resonance on the music's success at its own stated intention, not on whether that intention aligns with my aesthetic preferences. Two points off for the passages where the devotional intensity closes off rather than opens up — where the spiritual urgency narrows the music rather than widening it.

## Economy: 17/20

Most of Part I is exactly as long as it needs to be. The transposition sequence: exactly right. The vamp: exactly right. The motif introduction: exactly right. The spoken words: the most economical possible statement.

Three points off: the closing section is too long. Once the words are spoken, the structural argument is complete. What follows the words is music in search of an ending it cannot find, because the ending already happened. I dock three points for music that continues past the end of its argument.
