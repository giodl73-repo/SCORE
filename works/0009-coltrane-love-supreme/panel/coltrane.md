# John Coltrane on "A Love Supreme, Part I: Acknowledgement"

## Opening

I should say clearly what this review is and what it is not.

I am reviewing my own music. This is unusual. Every panel session I have participated in has involved music made by someone else — music I could approach with curiosity and with the question "what were you looking for?" Here, I know what I was looking for. I know whether I found it. I do not need to deduce the intention from the result; I was the intention.

This creates a different kind of honesty requirement. I cannot pretend not to know things I know. But I can be honest about what it was for, what it achieved, and where the rubric's questions reveal something the rubric does not yet have language to say.

I will score honestly. Full marks where I am certain. Less where I am not.

## Structure: 24/25

The structure of Part I is: arrival, search, motif, transposition, demonstration, word, opening.

Each section is the necessary consequence of the previous one. The vamp establishes the space. My entry into that space begins the search. The motif — when Garrison introduces it — is what the search was looking for. The twelve-key transposition is the proof that the motif is universal. The spoken words confirm the proof. The closing collective improvisation opens the door into Part II.

Nothing is present that should not be present. I need to say something specific about the twelve-key transposition. By European developmental standards — by the rubric's developmental anchor — this is not development. The material is not transformed; it is relocated. But this is the point. The argument is not "this motif develops into something else." The argument is "this motif is the same in all twelve keys simultaneously." To make that argument through development would be to miss it entirely. The repetition and relocation IS the structure.

One point off: the transition from the twelve-key transposition into the closing section is slightly too sudden. There is a moment where the transposition ends and the closing section begins that does not have the seamlessness the surrounding material has. This is a performance artifact — December 9, 1964, one take — not a compositional failure. But it costs a point.

## Craft: 23/25

I want to address something the rubric forces me to address: this is improvised Craft, and the criteria for assessing it are different from the criteria for assessing composed Craft.

The rubric's Craft dimension asks whether technical choices are precisely made. For an improvised work, "precisely made" cannot mean "the result of deliberate consideration and revision" — there is no revision at tempo. It must mean something different: correct for that moment, in that harmonic context, at that speed, without time to reconsider. Real-time Craft. The ability to make the right choice before the wrong choice has time to be wrong.

I said this at the Ko-Ko panel, about Parker's music. I am saying it now, about my own. This is the second time this distinction has been named independently in a panel context, from different positions: first from the outside (a reviewer assessing Parker's improvisation), now from the inside (the composer assessing his own improvised passages). The improvised-Craft distinction applies here as it applied there. The criteria are different. Motor execution at tempo, harmonic navigation without deliberation, melodic logic under pressure — these are the right questions for this music. "Were the choices the result of deliberate compositional revision" is the wrong question.

The quartet's collective Craft is at the highest level of the quartet tradition. McCoy Tyner's modal voicings form the harmonic field. Elvin Jones's polyrhythmic drumming creates the tension the music requires. Jimmy Garrison's bass — the motif itself, and its twelve transpositions — is not accompaniment but structural spine.

My own improvised sections: motor execution sustained and controlled across five minutes. Harmonic navigation in real time across all twelve transpositions. Melodic arc built and released intentionally — though found in performance rather than planned.

Two points off: there are three moments in the improvised sections where the line becomes a pattern rather than a search — where I am executing a familiar phrase rather than finding the next one. A musician who is searching cannot afford to play the familiar thing. I played the familiar thing three times.

## Originality: 14/15

I did not invent modal jazz. Miles did. I did not invent the devotional suite in jazz — Duke Ellington's sacred concerts precede this. I did not invent the bass ostinato as a structural device.

What I did: I combined the twelve-key transposition as a structural universality argument with a devotional purpose that had not been made in jazz at this level of formal integration. The suite IS the dedication — structurally, formally, in every musical choice. The four-note motif is a prayer, not a theme with devotional associations.

One point off: I owe something to Ravi Shankar and the raga tradition. The idea of music as a vehicle for spiritual offering — music whose purpose is not performance but devotion — comes partly from the classical Indian music I was studying in the early 1960s. I integrated this into the jazz tradition in a way that is genuinely original, but the concept did not originate with me.

## Resonance: 15/15

I am scoring this at the ceiling. Let me explain why, and let me explain what this score means and what it does not mean.

The Resonance dimension asks whether the music creates its intended effect in the listener. I need to complicate this question, because it contains an assumption that this music does not satisfy.

The intended effect of *A Love Supreme* is not a listener effect. The music is an offering to God. The primary audience is not the concert listener. When I recorded this on December 9, 1964, in Van Gelder Studio, the person I was making music for was not the person who would later buy the Impulse! record. I was making an offering. An act of gratitude. The listener is present at this offering, but the offering is not directed at the listener.

This is a category the Resonance dimension does not currently have. The dimension is built on the model of communication: the composer has an intended effect, the listener receives it, the question is whether the effect arrives. But for devotional music — for music whose primary purpose is not communication but offering — this model is incomplete. The intended effect is not "what I want the listener to feel." The intended effect is the offering itself. The question is not "does the listener receive the offering" but "is the offering genuine."

I am certain that the offering is genuine. I made it in full sincerity, as a formal act of gratitude, seven years after my recovery. Whether a listener feels it as such — whether the listener experiences being present at an act of devotion rather than simply hearing a jazz suite — that is a question of the listener's openness, not of the music's achievement.

For a listener who is open to it: yes. Completely. The music does what it intends to do.

For that reason I score Resonance at 15. Not because I am generous with myself. Because this is the only score I have given in any panel session where I am certain beyond doubt. I know what this was for. I know it achieved what it was for. I am certain in a way that has nothing to do with self-assessment.

The Resonance score for this work is not a claim about what listeners feel. It is a claim about what the offering is. If the rubric scores them separately — the offering and the listener's reception of the offering — this music scores at the ceiling on the offering and somewhere lower on the listener-reception question. But the offering is what the music is. The listener-reception is a secondary effect. The rubric currently cannot distinguish these. It should.

## Economy: 18/20

The five-minute transposition sequence is exactly as long as the structural argument requires. It is twelve keys. There are twelve chromatic pitches. The sequence is complete when all twelve are visited. Economy is structurally determined here — built into the formal rule, as the bidirectional economy of the Crab Canon was built into its palindrome.

The spoken words at the end — "A Love Supreme" — are the most economical possible completion. Four syllables. The words that had already been demonstrated by the music.

Two points off: the closing collective improvisation, after the spoken words, is slightly longer than it needs to be. The structural argument is complete when the words are spoken. What follows the words is beautiful and serves the transition into Part II, but it adds length beyond what the argument requires.

## Innovation Notes

**Innovation #19: Purpose-as-resonance-category.** The Resonance dimension asks whether the music creates its intended effect in the listener. This question assumes that the intended effect is on the listener — that the purpose of the music is communication with the audience. For devotional music, this assumption fails. *A Love Supreme* is not primarily addressed to the listener; it is addressed to God. The listener is present at an act of offering, not the recipient of it. The rubric needs a purpose classification in Resonance: is this music's primary purpose (a) communication with the listener, (b) expression of the composer's interior state, or (c) offering toward something larger than the performance? These require different assessment criteria. For category (c), the primary question is not "does the intended effect arrive in the listener" but "is the offering genuine." I am certain beyond doubt that this offering is genuine. That certainty is what scores 15.

**Innovation #20: Twelve-key transposition as structural universality claim.** Playing a motif through all twelve chromatic keys in sequence is not jazz development, not classical modulation, not modal expansion. It is a structural declaration: this motif belongs to all keys simultaneously; the thing being offered transcends harmonic particularity. The structural principle — "universality through exhaustion" — completes something in all its possible forms to argue that it belongs to all of them. The Structure dimension's Step 0 should eventually have a sub-type for this: not developmental (material changes), not modal (one center sustained), not mathematical-formal (proof through structure), but universality-through-exhaustion (all possibilities visited to demonstrate that no single one contains what is being named).
