# Charles Mingus on "A Love Supreme, Part I: Acknowledgement"

## Opening

Let me tell you what this music is before I score it.

This is a man standing up in front of his God and saying thank you. Not saying thank you in words — words are easy and cheap. Saying thank you in the only way that costs something: by making music. By using everything he knows, everything he has built, every hour of practice and every moment of search, and offering it. Not performing it. Offering it.

I have strong opinions about what music is for. I believe music that tells a truth is worth more than music that demonstrates technique. I believe music that demands something of the listener is worth more than music that flatters the listener. I believe music that costs the maker something is worth more than music that comes easily. By all three of these standards, *A Love Supreme, Part I* is among the most serious things I have heard in a panel room.

I will score fairly. I will also say the things I need to say.

## Structure: 23/25

The structure argument is the twelve-key transposition, and the twelve-key transposition is the most original structural gesture I have encountered in this panel series.

Here is what it is: a claim. Garrison plays the four-note motif — "A Love Supreme" — in one key. Then in the next. Then the next. All twelve. Not because the music requires harmonic contrast — it doesn't. Not because the listener needs variety — variety is not the point. Because the music is making a claim: this offering is not in any one key. It is in all keys. It belongs to all of them simultaneously. Love is not tonally specific.

That is a structural argument in the same register as Bach's palindrome or Beethoven's four-note development. It is not making music; it is making a claim through music. The claim is: love is universal. The demonstration is: play it in every key.

Two points off: the opening vamp, while atmospherically correct, takes slightly longer than it needs to take. I understand why — the space needs to be established before Coltrane enters — but the space could be established in slightly less time. The structural argument begins with the motif; everything before the motif is setup, and the setup is slightly long.

## Craft: 21/25

I am going to say something about improvised Craft.

The question of whether the rubric can assess improvised Craft against composed-Craft criteria has come up before — I have heard it referenced in connection with the Parker panel. I am naming it here, independently, because I think the panel needs to confirm this from multiple angles: Coltrane's improvised passages in Part I cannot be assessed against the same criteria as a composed string quartet. The questions are different. Motor execution at tempo. Harmonic navigation in real time. Melodic logic found under pressure, not planned before performance.

These are the right questions for this music. The standard jazz-composition questions — is the voice leading smooth, are the harmonic progressions logically derived, is the motivic development consistent — are the wrong questions. The music is not composed in that sense. It is improvised, and improvisation has its own criteria.

Coltrane's improvised Craft here: I am scoring it against improvised-Craft criteria. Motor execution: controlled. Harmonic navigation: precise across all twelve transpositions. Melodic logic: present; the arc builds and releases in a shape that was found in performance.

Four points off: there are passages — particularly in the middle of the transposition sequence — where the improvisation becomes illustrative rather than searching. Coltrane is playing "spiritual jazz phrases" instead of searching for the phrase the music requires at that moment. This is a Craft failure by improvised-Craft criteria: the search stops, the familiar vocabulary takes over. The familiar vocabulary is beautiful. But it is familiar.

Also: McCoy Tyner's pianism is extraordinary, and Jimmy Garrison's bass work — sustaining the twelve-key transposition over five minutes with rhythmic and harmonic accuracy — is underrated in most discussions of this recording. The Craft of the full quartet deserves acknowledgment.

## Originality: 14/15

What is original here:

The structural use of twelve-key transposition as a universality argument. This has not been done in jazz — or, as far as I know, in Western music — as a deliberate structural claim about the nature of the material being transposed. It is compositionally original and conceptually original simultaneously.

The devotional purpose integrated at the formal level, not the dedicatory level. Every other "dedicated to God" piece in jazz is a normal jazz piece with a dedication. This suite has God in its structure — in the choice to make the music an offering rather than a performance, in the use of the simplest possible motif (four notes, four syllables, no harmonic complexity), in the spoken words at the end that are not a performance but a completion.

One point off: the spiritual-music-as-jazz-composition idea owes something to Ellington's sacred concerts. Ellington was first. Coltrane took it further, but he did not start it.

## Resonance: 14/15

The music creates the experience of being present at an act of offering. This is what it intends to do. This is what it does.

I am not a conventionally religious man. But I know what honesty sounds like. I know what it sounds like when a musician is doing something that costs them something — when they are not performing but giving. This music sounds like that. The giving is audible. The gratitude is audible. Not as sentiment, not as decoration, but as the reason the music exists.

This is the highest use of Resonance: music that tells the truth about what it cost to make it. Music that does not protect itself. Music that is not calculating what the audience needs to hear but saying what needed to be said regardless of what the audience needs.

One point off for the passages where the music becomes sonically intense in a way that slightly overruns the devotional quality — where the search for intensity becomes performance of intensity. Not often. But it happens.

## Economy: 18/20

The motif is four notes. Four syllables. The most economical possible statement of what is being offered.

The transposition sequence is exactly as long as the structural argument requires. Twelve keys. No more.

The spoken words are the most economical possible confirmation. Coltrane does not sing them; he does not perform them. He says them.

Two points off: the closing section is longer than the structural economy requires. The argument is complete when the words are spoken. What follows is music in search of closure that has already been achieved.
