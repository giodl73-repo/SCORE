# Work Description: 0013 — "Rhapsody in Blue" (Opening)
# George Gershwin (1924)

---

## Historical Context

George Gershwin received a telegram from Paul Whiteman in January 1924 informing him that a concert called "An Experiment in Modern Music" was being planned for Aeolian Hall in New York City, and that Gershwin's new work would be the centerpiece. The premiere was on February 12, 1924. Gershwin had five weeks.

He composed the piano part quickly — he later claimed he heard the structure of the piece in its entirety during a train journey, the rhythm of the wheels giving him the rhythmic framework. He gave the orchestration to Ferde Grofé, who specialized in adapting popular music for Whiteman's large dance orchestra.

The result was premiered with Gershwin at the piano, Whiteman conducting, and a mixed audience of concert-hall regulars and jazz enthusiasts. The reception was strongly positive. The work was immediately recognized as something new — though what exactly it was new within, or what tradition it was attempting to advance, was disputed from the first night.

## Musical Analysis

### The Opening Glissando
The clarinet solo that opens the piece was not composed as a glissando. Ross Gorman, the clarinetist at the premiere, ran the trill up to the high register as a theatrical joke during rehearsal. Gershwin heard it and instructed him to keep it. This is Innovation #27 territory: the improvised score-marking as founding gesture. Gershwin recognized something in the improvised choice that was better than what he had written, and he incorporated it — but the decision was not "compose the glissando" or "improvise freely." It was a third kind of decision: hear the improvised improvement and ratify it.

The glissando rises from a low clarinet trill (around low Bb or C) to a high Bb held at the top of the instrument's range. The rise is through all the chromatic pitches in between — a smear, not a step. The effect is simultaneously comedic (the exaggerated reach) and genuinely moving (the actual achievement of that high note). It is, in miniature, the piece's synthesis argument: the jazz gesture (the glissando, the blue inflection of pitch between positions) achieving classical arrival (the sustained, pure high note).

### Rhapsody Form
The piece is in a rhapsody form: loosely developmental, without the strict exposition-development-recapitulation of the sonata, but with a trajectory that moves through distinct thematic areas and arrives at a recognizable culmination. The sections include:

1. **Opening (clarinet glissando + main theme):** The main theme, brass-heavy, syncopated, in a march-like character
2. **Slower middle section:** The "love theme" — one of the most famous melodies in American concert music, languid, lyrical, major-mode
3. **Development sections:** Bridge material, rhythmic treatment, harmonic departure
4. **Return and culmination:** The love theme returns, orchestrated at full force; the piano cadenza before the final section

The form is classical in its use of developmental structure and thematic return, but the thematic material and harmonic language are jazz-inflected throughout.

### Harmonic Language
The harmonic language operates in the area between blues harmony and classical functional harmony. Blue notes (the flatted third, the flatted seventh) appear throughout, creating a modal ambiguity that is specific to the blues tradition. These notes exist within what is otherwise a conventional classical tonal framework — the piece has a clear key center, uses dominant-tonic progressions at cadential moments, and arrives at an unambiguous tonic major at the end.

The synthesis question is whether these two harmonic systems are genuinely integrated or merely coexisting. This is the Music Theorist's primary analytical question.

### The Jazz Elements: Genuine or Flavored?
Ellington's primary concern will be whether the jazz elements in the Rhapsody are structurally present or decoratively applied. The syncopation is real — the rhythmic writing uses jazz-derived rhythmic patterns throughout. But is the syncopation doing the same structural work that syncopation does in jazz? In jazz, syncopation is not merely rhythmic variety; it is the primary carrier of harmonic and expressive information. The jazz musician plays off the beat because the off-beat is where the feeling lives.

In the Rhapsody, the syncopation is rhythmic variety within a classically organized formal structure. The feeling lives in the melody and the harmony, as in classical music; the syncopation provides rhythmic energy. This may be the key limitation: the jazz technique is present as technique without the jazz expressive philosophy that gives that technique its meaning in the jazz context.

## The Synthesis Completeness Question

A synthesis is complete (Innovation #28) when neither source tradition could recognize itself as primary. 

**Test:** Would a classical musician in 1924 hear the Rhapsody as a jazz piece? No — it uses classical developmental form, classical orchestration, classical harmonic framework with jazz inflections. The classical architecture is primary.

**Test:** Would a jazz musician in 1924 hear the Rhapsody as a classical piece? Also no — the syncopation, the blue notes, the improvisatory character of the piano writing are recognizably jazz-derived.

**Result:** Each tradition can recognize the other's elements, but each tradition also recognizes its own elements as primary. The synthesis may be incomplete — not because the work fails, but because it is a first attempt at a synthesis that would take later composers (Bernstein, among others) further toward completeness.

This finding, if confirmed by the panel, would be an important nuance to Cluster E: synthesis can be original without being complete; incomplete synthesis has its own distinct value as a founding gesture.
