# Reviewer: Duke Ellington (25%)
# Work: 0013 — "Rhapsody in Blue"

---

## Step 0 — Structural Approach

**DEVELOPMENTAL (rhapsody form — free-form developmental)**

The form is loosely developmental — moving through thematic areas rather than strictly developing material. From a jazz perspective, "developmental" means something different than it does in the classical tradition: in jazz, development happens in the moment of improvisation; in the Rhapsody, it happens through the pre-composed movement of thematic areas. The Rhapsody uses the classical meaning. This is itself a synthesis choice.

## Review

I want to tell you what it sounded like in 1924, because I was there for the music of that period even if I was not in Aeolian Hall that night.

In 1924, jazz was not what it later became. It was dance music primarily — ragtime derivatives, blues inflections, syncopated rhythms designed for bodies to move to. The idea that jazz could be taken to Carnegie Hall, to Aeolian Hall, to any concert venue and presented as a subject for serious listening — this was new. Whiteman's "An Experiment in Modern Music" was asking a question that a lot of people thought was either ridiculous or insulting: can jazz be art?

Gershwin answered: it can if it wears a classical suit.

That's the first thing I want to say about the synthesis. Gershwin did not take jazz into the concert hall on jazz's terms. He took jazz materials — the syncopation, the blue notes, the improvisatory feeling — and put them inside a classical container. The container does the work of legitimizing the contents. The Rhapsody says: these jazz sounds are art because they are inside a developmental form with classical orchestration.

Now, is that synthesis? Yes and no.

It is synthesis in the sense that the resulting work contains elements of both traditions simultaneously and could not have been produced by either alone. That is real. What neither jazz nor classical alone could have produced is this specific experience: the jazz glissando opening into orchestral fullness, the love theme arriving as both a pop melody and a classical lyrical release, the piano both improvising and fulfilling a composed developmental function simultaneously. This is something new.

But it is not synthesis in the deeper sense, which would require that neither tradition recognizes itself as primary. I recognize jazz here. I recognize my tradition here. And what I recognize is that my tradition is the guest. The jazz elements are visitors in a classical house. They are welcome visitors; they enrich the house; the house could not have been this house without them. But they do not own the house.

Let me be specific about what I mean. When I write for my orchestra — for Cootie Williams, for Johnny Hodges, for the specific voices of the Cotton Club band — the swing is structural. The rhythm does not decorate the form; it IS the form. The music is organized through rhythmic feeling, through the groove that the rhythm section establishes and that the soloists play against and with. The form is in the rhythm.

In the Rhapsody, the rhythm decorates the form. The syncopation is applied to a melodic and harmonic structure that is organized classically — by tonal arrival and departure, by thematic statement and development. The rhythm creates energy within that structure but does not organize it. A version of the Rhapsody with its syncopations removed would still be recognizably the Rhapsody. A jazz piece with its syncopations removed is no longer a jazz piece; it is a different thing.

This is the test I would apply to synthesis completeness (Innovation #28): can you remove the jazz elements and still have the essential work? In the Rhapsody, yes. Can you remove the classical elements and still have it? No — without the developmental form and the orchestration, there is no piece. The classical tradition is load-bearing; the jazz tradition is decorative.

**I name this as the synthesis asymmetry: the classical architecture is load-bearing and the jazz materials are expressive coloring. The work achieves synthesis originality (something neither tradition alone could produce) without achieving synthesis completeness (where neither tradition recognizes itself as primary). This is my independent naming of the synthesis criterion — I name it as a jazz practitioner who can feel the asymmetry in the body as well as analyze it intellectually. Cluster E confirmation contribution #2.**

What I find most genuinely original: the glissando. Not because of what it is technically, but because of how it came to be. A clarinetist improvised it; a composer recognized it; the improvised gesture became the founding moment of the piece. That is a different kind of creative act than either pure composition or pure improvisation. Gershwin's ear — his ability to hear the improvised glissando as the right thing and ratify it — is itself a synthesis act.
