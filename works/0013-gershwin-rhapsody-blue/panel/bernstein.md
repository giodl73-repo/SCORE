# Reviewer: Leonard Bernstein (30%)
# Work: 0013 — "Rhapsody in Blue"

---

## Step 0 — Structural Approach

**DEVELOPMENTAL (rhapsody form — free-form developmental)**

The rhapsody form is not classical sonata form but it is developmental: it moves through thematic areas with a recognizable trajectory, arrives at a culmination, and returns to its primary thematic material before the conclusion. The form is looser than sonata, tighter than free improvisation. Its precedent is Liszt's symphonic poems — formally coherent development without strict recapitulation rules.

Is this Cluster A (structural self-proof)? No. The rhapsody does not prove a formal claim about music by being the demonstration of that claim. Its form serves its content; it does not argue about form.

## Review

I want to be honest about my position here. I am reviewing a work that is both a predecessor to my own synthesis project and a competitor with it. Gershwin got to the jazz-classical synthesis first; what I did in *West Side Story* is, among other things, a response to what Gershwin did here. So I have a stake in the question of whether he succeeded.

My honest assessment: Gershwin came remarkably close, and the distance between "remarkably close" and "fully achieved" is the most interesting gap in American music.

Let me start with what he got right.

The opening glissando is one of the great founding gestures in American concert music. It says everything that needs to be said before the piece starts: jazz technique (the glissando, the blue inflection of pitch) achieving classical arrival (the sustained high note, the orchestral readiness beneath it). The fact that it was improvised by the clarinetist and recognized by Gershwin is itself a synthesis gesture — neither purely composed nor purely improvised, but the composer's ear operating at the intersection of the two traditions, recognizing the right thing when it arrived.

The love theme — and I realize I am about to say something that will seem like a grudging compliment but is not — is genuinely great. Not "great for a Tin Pan Alley composer working in a concert-hall format" — great. The melody achieves what only a small number of melodies achieve: it is simple enough to be remembered after one hearing and sophisticated enough to sustain repeated listening. The way it arrives after all the rhythmic energy of the opening material creates a genuine classical feeling of "earned lyrical release." This is a structural achievement, not just a melodic one.

Now for what he did not quite get.

The synthesis in the Rhapsody is what I would call synthesis-by-juxtaposition rather than synthesis-by-integration. The jazz elements (syncopation, blue notes, improvisatory piano figuration) and the classical elements (developmental form, orchestration, harmonic framework) exist alongside each other rather than producing a new substance that neither tradition contains. When the jazz elements arrive, the classical framework is still present underneath them; when the classical elements arrive, the jazz flavoring is still there. The two traditions coexist; they do not merge.

This matters for Cluster E. Innovation #21 (Synthesis Originality) claims that borrowing from two traditions to create something neither could produce alone constitutes a distinct form of originality. The Rhapsody partially meets this criterion: what it produces — a jazz-inflected concert piece with this specific combination of elements — neither tradition could have produced alone. But the synthesis is not complete in the sense that Innovation #28 requires: neither source tradition could recognize itself as primary.

The classical tradition can recognize itself as primary in the Rhapsody. The developmental form, the orchestration, the tonal harmonic framework — these are classical structures that jazz elements inhabit, not jazz structures that classical elements inhabit. The synthesis leans toward classical architecture.

I name this: **the synthesis is genuine but asymmetric.** The work is more than the sum of its parts (neither tradition alone could produce this), but the parts are not equally weighted. This is synthesis originality at a high level, but not synthesis completeness.

**I name the synthesis originality criterion from my angle: the Rhapsody creates something neither jazz nor classical alone could produce — but the classical tradition provides the architecture and the jazz tradition provides the materials. This is my independent naming. Cluster E confirmation contribution #1.**

What about the development sections between the main themes? Here the work is weakest. The transitional passages — the material between the opening theme, the love theme, and the return — are functional rather than developmental. They move from place to place without developing either the jazz or the classical materials in the most interesting ways. A more fully achieved synthesis might have found transitional passages that belonged to neither tradition — passages whose harmonic logic was derived from the synthesis itself rather than from one of its sources. Gershwin had not yet found those passages.
