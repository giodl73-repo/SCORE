# Work: 0013 — "Rhapsody in Blue"

## Step 0 — Structural Approach

**DEVELOPMENTAL (rhapsody form — free-form developmental)**

I must identify what kind of development this is. The rhapsody form has a developmental intention — it moves through thematic areas with a trajectory — but the development is not fugal, not sequential in the traditional sense, not organized through the tension-and-resolution logic of functional harmony in the way I would recognize. It is a looser, more associative development. I can assess it on its own terms only.

---

## Review

I must speak plainly. Much of what is in this piece is foreign to me. The blue notes — the flattened thirds and sevenths that give the harmonic language its distinctive character — are not part of the tradition I work in. The syncopation — the deliberate displacement of accent away from the strong beat — is not my expressive vocabulary. The improvisatory character of the piano figuration — the sense that the soloist is discovering the passage as they play it rather than executing a pre-determined plan — is different from the compositional completeness I require.

So I will speak about what I can assess: the developmental logic, the voice leading, and the question of formal coherence.

**On the developmental logic:**

The piece moves from the opening theme (brass, march-like, syncopated) through transitional material to the love theme (lyrical, piano-led, sustained), and then back toward the opening character for the culmination. This is a developmental trajectory. It is not the most sophisticated trajectory I have encountered — the transitions between thematic areas are functional rather than genuinely developmental, and the motivic relationships between the opening theme and the love theme are not tightly argued — but the shape is recognizable and the arrival at the love theme is earned by contrast, if not by development.

**On the voice leading:**

Within the harmonic framework that the piece establishes — a classical tonal framework decorated with blue notes — the voice leading is generally correct. The blue notes create moments of modal ambiguity, but these are resolved consistently and do not produce unresolved dissonances that would be technical errors within the piece's own harmonic language. I must assess voice leading against the tradition being worked in, and within the jazz-inflected classical tradition Gershwin establishes, the voice leading is adequate.

**On formal coherence:**

The rhapsody form is less coherent than sonata form but more coherent than free improvisation. The piece knows where it is going — the love theme is the emotional destination, and the piece moves toward it — and it returns from that destination in a way that creates a sense of completion. The proportions are reasonable: the opening is not too long, the love theme is not too short, the return is adequate.

**On the synthesis question:**

I find myself in agreement with what I suspect Ellington will say, though I arrive at it differently. The classical structure in this piece is the organizing principle; the jazz materials are the expressive content organized by that structure. This is not different in principle from what I did with the Lutheran chorale tradition — I took pre-existing material and organized it through my own structural logic. The question is whether the result is a synthesis or an absorption.

In my chorale arrangements, I would say: the chorale was absorbed into my counterpoint. The chorale became new. Is jazz absorbed into the classical structure here, or does it remain jazz? My assessment: the jazz remains jazz within the classical container. It is not absorbed. The synthesis is real — both traditions are genuinely present — but the container is classical.

**I name the synthesis criterion from my structural angle: the rhapsody form provides classical architecture; the jazz materials are expressive content within that architecture; the synthesis is genuine (producing something neither tradition alone could produce) but architecturally asymmetric (the classical form is the organizing structure). This is my contribution to the Cluster E confirmation. Cluster E confirmation contribution #3.**

One observation on the improvisatory founding gesture: the clarinetist's glissando, retained by Gershwin, is the most interesting creative act in this piece from my perspective. I spend my entire compositional life working toward completeness — toward the fugue subject that contains everything, toward the counterpoint that leaves nothing to chance. The idea of hearing an improvised passage and recognizing it as better than what was composed, and ratifying that improvised passage, is the opposite of my method. But I recognize it as a creative act. The composer's ear did the work. Gershwin heard the glissando and knew. That knowing is composition.
