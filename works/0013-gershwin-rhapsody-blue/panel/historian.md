## Step 0 — Structural Approach

**DEVELOPMENTAL (rhapsody form — free-form developmental)**

Historically, the rhapsody form has nationalist precedents (Liszt's Hungarian Rhapsodies, Dvořák's Slavonic Rhapsodies) in which folk or national musical materials are incorporated into a loose developmental form. Gershwin's use of the rhapsody form for jazz materials is historically consistent with this tradition: jazz functions as the American national/folk music, and the rhapsody is the classical form that absorbs and presents it to a concert audience.

## Review

I want to place this work in its historical moment precisely, because the historical moment is the primary context for understanding both what it achieved and what it failed to achieve.

**1924: The negotiation between jazz and concert music**

In 1924, jazz was not yet twenty years old as a named musical form. Its roots in African American folk music, blues, and ragtime were widely acknowledged but not always respected. The dominant cultural narrative treated jazz as entertainment — dance music, party music, the music of nightclubs and speakeasies — and treated concert music as art. The question of whether jazz could be art was culturally loaded in ways that had as much to do with race, class, and Americanness as with any musical criterion.

Paul Whiteman's "An Experiment in Modern Music" concert was explicitly an attempt to legitimize jazz — to argue that jazz could be art by presenting it in an art-music venue to an art-music audience. Gershwin's Rhapsody was designed to answer the cultural question as well as the musical one.

This context is essential for understanding the synthesis choice Gershwin made. He did not present jazz on jazz's terms. He presented jazz through the legitimizing frame of classical form, orchestration, and the concert-hall setting. This was the strategic choice available to him in 1924: jazz could not yet enter the concert hall as itself; it had to be translated into classical form first.

**What the Rhapsody founded:**

The Rhapsody opened a conversation that would not be fully developed until Bernstein, Miles Davis, and others a generation later. It established that jazz and classical music could coexist in the same work — that blue notes and classical orchestration were not mutually exclusive. This is a founding gesture in the history of American concert music.

The works that followed — Gershwin's own *Concerto in F* (1925) and *An American in Paris* (1928), Copland's jazz-influenced works of the 1920s, eventually Bernstein's *Jeremiah Symphony* and *West Side Story* — all emerged from the possibility the Rhapsody established.

**The synthesis question historically:**

Historically, the jazz-classical synthesis attempted in the Rhapsody was the first of its kind, which means it was working without a model. Gershwin was making decisions about how to combine the two traditions without examples of how to do it. Given this, the synthesis achievement is remarkable; the synthesis limitation (classical architecture primary, jazz materials decorative) is historically expected.

What the Rhapsody demonstrated was that the combination was possible. The question of what a complete synthesis would look like — where neither tradition recognized itself as primary — was left for subsequent composers to answer.

**Innovation #27 (the improvised founding gesture):**

The clarinetist's glissando, improvised and retained, is historically significant as a creative act. In 1924, the boundary between composition and improvisation was more permeable than it later became in the classical tradition. Gershwin's recognition of the improvised glissando as the correct founding gesture for his synthesis piece is itself a synthesis act: the jazz practice of improvisation meeting the classical practice of composition, in the moment of the creative decision. This is a different creative mode than either tradition alone practices.
