## Work Description

The opening: a solo clarinet begins with a low trill and rises — through a glissando that was not notated in the original parts — to an impossibly sustained high note. The clarinetist at the premiere, Ross Gorman, improvised the glissando as a joke; Gershwin heard it and kept it. The glissando became the piece's signature: the sound of jazz straining toward orchestral ambition, or of classical form opening itself to jazz spontaneity. Both readings are correct.

The piece moves through jazz-inflected themes in a loosely developmental rhapsody structure, arriving eventually at the famous "love theme" in the slower middle section. The orchestration by Ferde Grofé brackets Gershwin's piano throughout.

**Jazz/classical synthesis:** Gershwin explicitly designed Rhapsody in Blue as a fusion of jazz rhythms and blue notes within classical orchestration and developmental form. Blue notes (the flattened thirds and sevenths of the blues scale) exist within a classical harmonic framework. Jazz syncopation animates a developmental structure that follows — loosely — classical practice.

**The question:** Does the work create something that transcends both sources, or does it remain a curious hybrid?

## Cluster E Confirmation Target

Innovation #21 (Synthesis Originality, from work 0010) seeded Cluster E: borrowing from two traditions to create something neither could produce alone constitutes a distinct form of originality. "Somewhere" (0010) is the seed.

**Confirmation requires:** Bernstein, Ellington, and Bach each independently name synthesis originality from their angle — that the result is more than the sum of its sources, or conversely that the synthesis is incomplete (which would be an important negative confirmation: what does an incomplete synthesis look like?).

**Innovation #28 target:** Synthesis completeness — a synthesis is complete when neither source tradition could recognize itself as primary. Rhapsody in Blue may fail this test (it may remain fundamentally classical with jazz clothing). If Ellington finds this, it is a crucial finding.
