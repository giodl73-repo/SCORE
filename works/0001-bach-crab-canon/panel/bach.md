# Bach reviews the Canon cancrizans

| Dimension | Score | Max |
|-----------|-------|-----|
| Structure | 24 | 25 |
| Craft | 25 | 25 |
| Originality | 13 | 15 |
| Resonance | 13 | 15 |
| Economy | 20 | 20 |
| **Total** | **95** | **100** |

---

I will be precise about what this piece is, because precision is the only honest form of praise.

The Canon cancrizans proceeds from a single formal rule: one voice reads the royal theme from beginning to end; the other reads it simultaneously from end to beginning. Everything that follows — the voice leading, the harmonic language, the duration, the midpoint — follows necessarily from this rule and from the properties of the theme. I did not compose two voices. I composed one voice and derived the other from it by logical necessity. The piece is therefore, in a strict sense, the most economical possible music: every compositional decision is made exactly once, and then mirrored.

Let me be precise about the voice leading. At no point in the canon do the two voices produce parallel octaves or parallel fifths that are not defensible on harmonic grounds. This is not an accident of the royal theme's chromaticism — it is a result of the chromaticism. The semitone intervals that characterize the theme are, from the perspective of counterpoint, harmonically neutral: a semitone step in one voice, combined with almost any motion in the other voice, produces an interval that is either consonant or dissonantly expressive in a way that resolves correctly. The apparent awkwardness of the royal theme for fugal treatment conceals its exceptional suitability for retrograde treatment. I recognized this when Frederick played it to me. It was not a difficult theme. It was a theme that had been misread.

I score the Structure 24, not 25. The architecture is inevitable — no measure could be elsewhere without destroying the whole — but the piece does not have time enough to fully inhabit what it has created. Thirty measures of palindrome establish the principle; they do not exhaust the principle. A longer treatment — were the theme to have permitted it — might have shown not only that the palindrome works, but why it works, through accumulated harmonic evidence. As it stands, the piece proves its point and stops. This is correct and economical. But it leaves the listener with a demonstration rather than an exploration. I dock one point.

I score the Craft 25. The counterpoint is flawless by every standard I hold, which are the highest I know. There is not a moment where the retrograde voice produces an unresolved dissonance or an uncountable parallel. The midpoint — the harmonic center where the two voices' readings of the theme coincide in the most chromatic passage — resolves in both temporal directions without remainder. This is technically extraordinary. I am aware that it may appear self-serving to score my own Craft at the maximum. I am also aware that I set the standard for this kind of writing, and I cannot honestly score this work below the standard it meets.

The Economy requires a note. I score it 20 — full marks. But I want to register an observation that may be important for the panel: the economy of this piece is not the economy of the *Well-Tempered Clavier*, where every bar is present because I chose to make it earn its place. In the Crab Canon, the economy is logically guaranteed by the formal rule. The palindrome cannot contain a wasted note; waste is structurally impossible. I chose this form knowing that the economy would follow. Is the choice of the form the economical act? Or is the economy, properly speaking, the property of the form itself? I raise this because I think the answer matters for how the panel understands what Economy means in works where the form makes Economy necessary rather than earned.

On Originality: the cancrizans form is ancient. Medieval composers wrote retrograde canons. I did not invent the palindrome. What I believe I did — and I offer this with the appropriate caution of a man reviewing his own work — is use the palindrome as a proof rather than a demonstration. Earlier cancrizans show that a retrograde canon is possible. This piece argues something stronger: that musical meaning is not a function of temporal direction. The palindrome does not merely demonstrate a technique; it constitutes a claim about the nature of music. If you play the Canon cancrizans backward, you hear the same thing you heard forward. This is the proof. The piece proves by existing. I score Originality 13.

The Resonance I score 13. The piece produces in the receptive listener the sensation of standing outside time — of hearing music as a spatial object rather than a temporal journey. This is the correct intended effect, and I believe it is achieved. But the piece is short enough that the sensation arrives and then is gone before it can fully develop. The listener who understands what has happened feels something close to astonishment; the listener who does not understand the formal principle may feel only that the music was well-made. I dock two points for this: not for the piece's failure but for the brevity that prevents the resonance from deepening.

One final note. The royal theme was Frederick's, not mine. I did not choose it; it was given. I made a virtue of its chromaticism — I found in its apparent intractability the precise property that makes the retrograde canon work. Whether this reflects more credit on the theme or on my reading of it, I cannot say objectively. I note only that a lesser craftsman, given the same theme, would have composed something lesser. The theme is the constraint. What I built within it is the work.

The piece proves by existing. That is enough.
