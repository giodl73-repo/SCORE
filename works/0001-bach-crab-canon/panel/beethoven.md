# Beethoven reviews the Canon cancrizans

| Dimension | Score | Max |
|-----------|-------|-----|
| Structure | 24 | 25 |
| Craft | 25 | 25 |
| Originality | 13 | 15 |
| Resonance | 11 | 15 |
| Economy | 19 | 20 |
| **Total** | **92** | **100** |

---

The structure is perfect. I will not argue with this. The architecture of the palindrome is absolutely complete — no measure could be relocated without destroying the entire logical edifice, and the edifice is sound. Bach agrees with himself at 24. I agree with him. The one point neither of us awards is for the same reason: the piece ends before it has finished discovering what it contains. This is a genuine cost, and it comes from the brevity, not from any failure of construction.

The craft I score 25 without hesitation. The counterpoint between the two voices is exact in both temporal directions. I know what it takes to write invertible counterpoint — I have written it. Writing counterpoint that is correct forward and correct backward simultaneously is a different category of difficulty. It is not twice as hard. It is exponentially harder, because every choice constrains every other choice in both directions at once. The fact that this piece contains no detectable contrapuntal failure — no parallel octaves, no unresolved dissonances, no voice crossings that produce confusion — is a technical achievement I can recognize without reservation.

Now. The question I cannot resolve in the same way.

The mathematics are perfect. The mathematics are, in fact, the argument. Bach says the piece proves by existing, and he is correct that the palindrome constitutes a logical proof that musical meaning survives temporal inversion. I accept the logical proof. I accept that the piece IS the demonstration.

What I want to know — what I cannot determine from analysis alone — is whether the listener *feels* the retrograde as necessity, or only as information.

This is not a small distinction. In the Ninth Symphony, the development of the first movement does not merely demonstrate the motif's flexibility; it produces in the listener the sensation of the music straining against itself, of necessity forcing its way through resistance. The recapitulation does not merely restore the tonic; it arrives with the full weight of everything the development has tested and almost destroyed. The listener does not need to know sonata form to feel this. The form produces the feeling whether or not the listener understands the form.

Does the Canon cancrizans do this? Does the listener feel the retrograde voice as something they needed — as the answer to a question the forward voice was asking — or do they feel it as a very sophisticated additional voice? Does the palindromic completion arrive with the weight of inevitability, or with the lightness of elegance?

I think — and I score Resonance 11 on this basis — the piece produces admiration before it produces feeling. For a listener who knows what is happening, there is something close to awe: the sensation of watching a logical proof materialize in sound. For a listener who does not know, there is the pleasure of well-made counterpoint without the deeper experience the form is capable of creating. Eleven out of fifteen: solid resonance, but the emotional register is compressed by the brevity and by the piece's cool, demonstrative character. It is formally moving. It is emotionally compact. These are related but not identical qualities.

I dock one point on Economy, not nineteen. I dock it for a specific reason: the economy of this piece is not freely achieved. The palindrome makes waste structurally impossible. I have thought about this carefully and I believe the credit for Economy belongs primarily to the formal rule, not to Bach's compositional discipline in the usual sense. The discipline was exercised in choosing and then inhabiting the form. But within the form, there was nothing to be economical about — every note was already committed by logical necessity. I score Economy 19 to mark this as a genuine question rather than a settled answer.

One final observation, which is the most important thing I want to say.

I would never write a crab canon. Not because the technique is beneath me — it is not — but because it runs against everything I believe about how music moves. Drama requires irreversibility. The Fifth Symphony works because the development cannot be undone — because the themes that go into the development are different when they come out, because the recapitulation does not restore what was lost but replaces it with something harder-won. The music has direction because time has direction. You cannot un-ring a bell.

The palindrome un-rings the bell. It says: what happened can be un-happened; the journey can be walked in reverse without loss of sense. This is philosophically interesting. It may even be true. But it is the opposite of what I am trying to do when I compose. I am trying to use music's temporal irreversibility as its primary dramatic resource. Bach is demonstrating that the irreversibility is, in some deep sense, optional.

I find this position beautifully argued and fundamentally wrong. Both things are true.
