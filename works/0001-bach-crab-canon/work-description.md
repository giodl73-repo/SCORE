# Work Description: Canon cancrizans, BWV 1079

## The Royal Theme and Its Properties

Frederick the Great handed Bach a theme. This is the starting point for everything.

The royal theme is long — approximately eight measures in common time — and deeply chromatic. It moves through a chain of semitone inflections that push and pull at the tonal center of C minor without ever fully resolving it into stable diatonic ground. The opening gesture descends from the tonic through a chromatic scale fragment; subsequent phrases extend this chromatic logic into inner voices, creating an unsettled, searching quality. The theme is often described as "melancholy" or "searching," but more precisely it is *tonally ambiguous by design* — it contains so many accidentals, so many chromatic neighbor tones and passing tones, that any simple diatonic analysis of its harmonic implications falls apart immediately.

This is important. Frederick's theme was widely considered, at the time, to be nearly unusable as a fugue subject precisely because of this chromaticism. A good fugue subject is one that bears contrapuntal treatment: it must yield tonal answers, produce consonant countersubjects, and survive the harmonic displacements of exposition, episode, and stretto without creating unresolvable harmonic contradictions. Frederick's theme, with its relentless chromaticism, seems to resist all of this. It is a long, ambiguous, seemingly intractable object.

Bach's genius in the *Musical Offering* was to see that the theme's apparent weakness — its chromatic ambiguity — was in fact its contrapuntal power. A theme that is tonally ambiguous is also tonally flexible: it can be harmonized in multiple ways, can support multiple contrapuntal voices without forcing them into a single harmonic reading. And for the retrograde canon specifically, chromatic ambiguity is an asset rather than a liability: the semitone intervals that characterize the theme read almost as well backward as forward, because semitones have no strong directional harmonic implication the way, say, a rising fourth does. The theme that seemed designed to resist canonic treatment turns out to be almost ideally suited for retrograde treatment.

## The Palindromic Structure

The Canon cancrizans is notated — in its original engraving and in modern editions that follow the original — as a single melodic line. Two performers (or two hands at a keyboard) read from opposite ends of this line simultaneously, moving toward each other and then past each other until each performer has traversed the entire line.

Voice 1 (the forward voice) reads the line from left to right: measure 1 through measure 30 (approximately). This is the royal theme in its original temporal order — the theme Frederick gave Bach, as Frederick gave it.

Voice 2 (the retrograde voice) reads the line from right to left: measure 30 back through measure 1. This is the royal theme in strict retrograde — every note reversed, so that what was the final note of the theme becomes the first note of Voice 2's entry, and what was the first note of the theme becomes the last note of Voice 2's part.

The two voices begin simultaneously. They proceed at the same tempo. They are, by the logic of the palindrome, playing the same music — but one is living its life forward and one backward. At the midpoint of the canon (approximately measure 15), the two voices have each played half of the theme, and they are now playing the same passage that each began with — but from the other's perspective. By the final measure, Voice 1 has arrived at the note with which Voice 2 began, and Voice 2 has arrived at the note with which Voice 1 began.

The result is an exact palindrome: the piece, played backward, is identical to the piece played forward. If you record the Canon cancrizans and play the recording in reverse, you hear exactly what you heard in the forward direction. This is not approximately true; it is exactly, mathematically true.

## Harmonic Implications of the Palindrome

The palindromic structure creates a remarkable harmonic phenomenon. Because the two voices are playing the same theme in opposite temporal directions, every harmonic interval between them is simultaneously a harmony "going forward" and a harmony "going backward." Dissonances that arise between the voices resolve — but they resolve in both directions at once.

Consider a suspension: in Baroque counterpoint, a suspension occurs when one voice sustains a note across a bar line while the other voice moves to a note that creates a dissonance with the sustained note, then resolves down by step. In the Crab Canon, a suspension in the forward reading is, from the perspective of the retrograde reading, a resolution followed by a suspension — the same notes, but experienced in reversed order. The dissonance is prepared and resolved in both temporal directions simultaneously.

This has a remarkable consequence: the harmonic language of the piece is self-consistent not just going forward but going backward as well. Every prepared dissonance is prepared in both directions. Every resolution resolves in both directions. The harmonic logic is bidirectional.

The royal theme's chromaticism intensifies this effect. The semitone chromatic passing tones and neighbor tones that characterize the theme create local dissonances that are easily resolved by contrary motion — and contrary motion is exactly what the retrograde voice provides, since it is moving in the time-reversed direction. The theme's apparent harmonic ambiguity, when subjected to retrograde canonic treatment, becomes a series of carefully balanced, mutually resolving tensions.

## The Midpoint: Where Forward and Retrograde Meet

The structural and harmonic center of the canon is its midpoint — the moment where Voice 1 has played half the theme and Voice 2 has played half the theme in retrograde, and both voices are now playing the exact middle section of the theme (Voice 1 going forward through it, Voice 2 going backward through it).

This midpoint is the most harmonically dense passage in the piece. It is where the chromatic properties of the royal theme are most concentrated, because both voices are drawing from the chromatic interior of the theme simultaneously. It is also the moment of maximum harmonic tension — the piece has been building toward this meeting, and the meeting is not a resolution but a crossing. The voices are at their closest point, harmonically speaking, and then they begin to diverge again, moving backward through the harmonic path they traversed in the first half.

The midpoint produces a striking harmonic effect: the dissonances here are not resolvable in the usual forward-looking way. They resolve bidirectionally — the forward voice resolves forward into the second half of the theme, and the retrograde voice resolves backward into what was (from the forward perspective) the first half of the theme. The listener who understands what is happening experiences the midpoint as a kind of harmonic mirror: the tension that was assembled across the first half of the piece is simultaneously present at the midpoint and already beginning to dissolve, because the second half of the piece is the time-reverse of the first, and what was created is now being uncreated by the same process that created it.

Harmonically, the midpoint sits on a diminished seventh chord or its equivalent — one of the most harmonically ambiguous chords in the tonal system, capable of resolving in multiple directions. This is almost certainly deliberate: the most ambiguous chord placed at the most ambiguous moment, where the piece's two temporal directions are simultaneously present.

## Economy: Every Note Serves Both Directions

The economy of the Canon cancrizans is without parallel in the repertoire. Not approximately without parallel — literally without parallel, because the formal rule of the palindrome makes it logically impossible to waste a note. Every note in the piece is doing double duty: it belongs to the forward reading of the theme and the retrograde reading simultaneously. There is no such thing as a note in the Crab Canon that is not doing structural work, because every note is structural work in two directions at once.

This is a different kind of economy from the economy of, say, the *Well-Tempered Clavier*, where Bach's economy is the result of discipline — the result of reviewing each bar and removing every note that is not earning its place. In the Crab Canon, economy is a *logical consequence of the form*. The palindrome structure makes waste impossible. The composer who understands this has created a machine for maximum economy.

But this raises a question: is the economy genuine, or is it merely formal? The SCORE rubric defines Economy as "does every note earn its place?" In the Crab Canon, every note earns its place because the form guarantees it must. This is a different kind of earning than the kind that requires authorial judgment and discipline. The question the panel will need to address is whether Economy can be fully credited when it is structurally mandated rather than achieved through compositional choice.

## The SCORE Encoding: How Each Dimension Applies

**Structure (25 pts):** The architecture of the palindrome is complete and internally consistent. Every structural decision follows necessarily from the formal rule. The opening is the close, and the close is the opening. The form is as inevitable as a form can be. The only question is whether the piece is long enough to fully inhabit its own structural implications — whether 30 measures of this palindrome fully explores what a palindrome of this material can do.

**Craft (25 pts):** The voice leading between the forward and retrograde voices must be correct at every point: no parallel octaves, no parallel fifths (or where they occur, they must be justifiable), no unresolved dissonances, no voice crossings that produce muddiness. This is an extreme technical constraint because the composer has no freedom to adjust the retrograde voice — it is entirely determined by the forward voice. Every craft decision must be made in the forward voice, knowing that the retrograde voice is its mirror. The counterpoint must be correct in both temporal directions simultaneously. This is technically extraordinary by any historical standard.

**Originality (15 pts):** Retrograde canons existed before Bach — medieval music includes examples of cancrizans. The idea is ancient. What is original is the specific use of the palindrome as a proof rather than a demonstration: the piece argues that musical meaning is not destroyed by temporal inversion, and it constitutes this argument by being a piece in which musical meaning demonstrably survives temporal inversion. The idea of the palindrome is not original; the use of it as structural self-proof may be.

**Resonance (15 pts):** The intended effect is a particular kind of cognitive and emotional suspension — the sensation of standing at a place where time flows in both directions at once, where past and future are the same, where the piece is arriving at its destination and has already been there. This is a specific and unusual intended effect. Whether it is achieved depends on whether the listener experiences the palindrome as felt necessity or as information. For the listener who is open to it, the Crab Canon produces something close to a spatial experience of time — time as a landscape that can be traversed in either direction — rather than the usual temporal experience of music as a journey with a direction.

**Economy (20 pts):** As discussed: logically perfect. Every note earns its place twice. The question is what this tells us about what Economy means.
