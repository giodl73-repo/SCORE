# Brief: Canon cancrizans (Crab Canon), BWV 1079

## Subject

The Canon cancrizans is the second canon in Bach's *Musical Offering* (BWV 1079), composed in 1747 and dedicated to Frederick the Great of Prussia. It is a two-voice canon approximately 30 measures in duration. Voice 1 plays the royal theme — Frederick's long, chromatic, tonally ambiguous theme — from beginning to end. Voice 2 plays the same theme simultaneously from end to beginning, in strict retrograde. The two voices begin at opposite ends of the score, converge at the midpoint, and arrive at the opposite end from where they started — which is, by the nature of the palindrome, the same as where they started.

This is the formal definition of a cancrizans (from Latin: *cancer*, crab) — a retrograde canon, so called because the crab walks backward. But the formal definition understates what the piece does. The Canon cancrizans is not merely built on a palindromic technique. It is a piece that proves a proposition by being the proof: that musical meaning survives temporal inversion. The palindrome is not a decorative structure; it is the argument. You cannot paraphrase the Crab Canon and preserve what it demonstrates, because the piece IS the demonstration.

Every note in the piece is doing double duty: it belongs to the forward reading and the retrograde reading simultaneously. The harmonic dissonances that are created by the two voices resolve — forward going one direction, backward going the other — in both temporal directions at once. This is maximum economy of means. This is the most efficient possible music: not a single note that is not earning its place twice.

## Audience

**Original audience:** Frederick the Great of Prussia, one of the most musically sophisticated monarchs in European history. Frederick played flute, composed for it, and maintained one of the finest court orchestras on the continent. Bach's *Musical Offering* was sent to Frederick after their meeting at Potsdam in May 1747, where Bach improvised fugues on Frederick's theme. The piece was an act of musical diplomacy, a demonstration of compositional mastery, and — quietly — a statement that the old Leipzig Kantor could do things the Prussian court composers could not. The intended audience was exactly one person, and that person was not naive.

**Contemporary audience:** Musicians, music theorists, music lovers, anyone who has heard the piece performed or seen the score laid out as a single staff that two performers read from opposite ends toward the middle. The visual layout of the Crab Canon — the single staff, the two performers approaching each other — is itself a famous image in music history. The piece is now canonical in every sense: it is one of the most discussed pieces in the Western repertoire, referenced in Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach* as an example of self-referential structure, performed at the openings of concerts, studied in music theory courses worldwide.

## Tradition

The canon is a sub-type of the fugue tradition. In the broadest sense: a canon is a composition in which a single melody is harmonized entirely by imitations of itself — voices entering in succession (round canon), simultaneously at different pitches (canon by inversion), or — as here — simultaneously in opposite temporal directions (retrograde canon, cancrizans).

What the tradition demands of a canon:
- **Contrapuntal integrity**: the voices must be melodically independent and harmonically consonant with each other, not merely non-colliding
- **The subject must bear the contrapuntal treatment**: a subject that works only in its original form is not a good fugue subject; the material must have the genetic flexibility to be inverted, augmented, retrograded, and still be itself
- **The counterpoint must resolve**: dissonances must prepare and resolve according to the tradition's grammar; the harmonic language must be internally consistent

The cancrizans adds the additional demand that the subject bear retrograde treatment without losing its identity or creating unresolvable dissonances. This is an extreme constraint: most themes, when played backward, produce nonsense. Frederick's royal theme — chosen not by Bach but by Frederick — turns out, in Bach's hands, to be precisely the kind of theme that bears this treatment. Whether this is luck or Bach's insight into what the theme contained remains an open question.

## Core Question

**What does this piece do that the SCORE rubric cannot yet fully name?**

The Canon cancrizans is an instance of what might be called *structural self-proof*: a musical work whose formal structure is itself the argument the work is making. The piece argues that musical meaning survives temporal inversion. It demonstrates this by being a piece in which musical meaning demonstrably survives temporal inversion. The argument and the demonstration are the same object.

This is different from formal complexity. The Art of Fugue is formally complex; it explores the contrapuntal possibilities of a single subject. The Goldberg Variations are formally complex; they generate thirty variations from a single harmonic bass. These works demonstrate craft and ingenuity. The Crab Canon does something else: it uses its formal structure as a logical proof. The piece is what philosophers of language call a *self-referential performative* — a statement that enacts what it states.

The SCORE rubric has dimensions for whether the structure is well-made (Structure), whether the technique is correctly deployed (Craft), whether the idea is original (Originality), whether the effect lands (Resonance), and whether the means are economical (Economy). But it does not yet have a way to capture *whether a piece proves something through its form rather than merely demonstrating it*. The Canon cancrizans is the first work in the SCORE corpus to force this question.

## Open Questions

1. **Is formal perfection the same as musical meaning?** Beethoven will press on this. The mathematics of the palindrome are perfect. But does the listener experience the retrograde as necessity — as something that had to be — or merely as information? Can the listener feel what the analysis proves?

2. **Does the palindrome prove something, or demonstrate technique?** There is a difference between a palindrome that shows off the composer's skill and a palindrome that constitutes a philosophical claim about the nature of musical time. Which is this? The answer may depend on whether you think music can make philosophical claims at all.

3. **Is the economy of double-duty notes an aesthetic achievement or a logical constraint?** The Crab Canon's Economy score will be perfect — every note earns its place twice. But is this genuine economy, or is it economy that follows necessarily from the formal rule? The distinction matters for whether Economy means anything in a piece where Economy cannot be otherwise.

4. **What did Frederick hear?** We know the piece was sent to Frederick. We do not know how Frederick performed it, whether he performed it at all, what he thought of it, or whether Bach's quiet demonstration of mastery registered as Bach intended it. The piece was composed for one listener. The historical record is silent on whether that listener received it.

5. **Is the brevity a choice or a constraint?** The piece is approximately 30 measures. The royal theme is long for a canon subject. A longer palindrome would require a longer subject or a more elaborate treatment. Is the piece short because the form demands it, because Bach chose it, or because Frederick's theme had a natural stopping point that Bach recognized? And does the brevity limit the piece's ability to fully explore what it contains?
