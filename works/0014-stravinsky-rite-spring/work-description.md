# Work Description: 0014 — The Rite of Spring (Introduction + Augurs of Spring)
# Igor Stravinsky (1913)

---

## Historical Context

Igor Stravinsky composed The Rite of Spring between 1911 and 1913 for Sergei Diaghilev's Ballets Russes, with choreography by Vaslav Nijinsky. The work depicts a pagan ritual: the sacrifice of a chosen maiden who dances herself to death to propitiate the god of spring. Stravinsky later claimed he had a vision of a young girl dancing in a circle of old men who watched until she died.

The first performance on May 29, 1913 at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées in Paris was one of the most famous premieres in music history. The audience began making noise almost immediately — booing, shouting, laughing — and the disturbance escalated until members of the audience were fighting each other. The police were called. Stravinsky watched from backstage and later said he was so angry he left before the piece ended. The conductor Pierre Monteux kept the orchestra playing through the riot. The dancers could not hear the music and relied on Nijinsky, standing on a chair backstage, shouting the counts.

The piece was performed again the following month, without incident, and received a standing ovation.

## Musical Analysis: Introduction

The Introduction opens with a solo bassoon — an unusual choice in itself, since the standard orchestral opening of this period involved full ensemble or a featured melody instrument (violin, flute, oboe). But more unusual than the choice of instrument is the register: Stravinsky places the opening melody at the very top of the bassoon's range, where the instrument sounds strained, reedy, almost plaintive. The melody is derived from a Lithuanian folk song.

The phrase lengths are irregular: the melody does not divide into even four-bar phrases. It extends and contracts according to its own logic, which is the folk-melody logic of speech rhythm rather than the dance-phrase logic of classical meter. The melody is alone — there is no harmonic support, no rhythm section, nothing but the folk melody in an uncomfortable register.

As the Introduction continues, other instruments enter — a horn in a different key, woodwinds adding sustained tones — but they do not harmonize the bassoon melody in the classical sense. They add layers that exist simultaneously with the bassoon melody without supporting it. The texture builds to a kind of polyphonic complexity before the Augurs of Spring crash in.

## Musical Analysis: Augurs of Spring

The chord is the signature of the movement and possibly of the century: an E major triad in the lower strings and an Eb dominant seventh in the upper strings, played simultaneously. The two harmonies clash maximally: the E and Eb are a semitone apart; the chord contains the maximum density of dissonance within the twelve-tone chromatic system.

This chord is not prepared by voice leading from what preceded it. It simply arrives, as if the earth has opened. And then it is repeated — in irregular accents. Not a regular pattern (all quarter-note beats accented, or all beats two and four) but a changing pattern: the accent appears at irregular intervals, different in each bar. The effect is that the listener cannot predict when the next accent will fall — the rhythm is aggressive and unpredictable simultaneously.

The chord itself does not develop or change for the first section of the Augurs. It is hammered. The repetition of a harmonically static element in irregular rhythms is not a technique available in the classical tradition before Stravinsky. The classical tradition requires harmonic motion; static harmony in classical music is a rhetorical device (a sustained dominant before the final tonic), not a structural principle.

## What Was Broken

Stravinsky broke multiple conventions simultaneously in these two sections:

1. **Tonal harmony:** The polytonal Augurs chord is not in any key. It is the superimposition of two keys simultaneously.
2. **Regular meter:** The accents in the Augurs do not follow regular meter. The listener cannot feel a regular pulse.
3. **Voice leading:** The Augurs chord arrives without preparation and is repeated without resolution.
4. **Orchestral register convention:** The bassoon is placed in its highest, most uncomfortable register.
5. **Developmental expectation:** The Augurs chord does not develop; it insists. Static repetition replaces developmental motion.
6. **The expressive tradition:** The entire 19th-century expressive tradition — music as the language of human feeling, organized by singing melody and flowing harmony — is abandoned simultaneously.

Breaking one convention at a time produces a new idiom within the existing tradition. Breaking six conventions simultaneously produces a new space outside the existing tradition. This is Innovation #29: originality through simultaneous rupture.
