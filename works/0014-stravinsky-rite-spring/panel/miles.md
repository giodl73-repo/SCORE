# Reviewer: Miles Davis (20%)
# Work: 0014 — The Rite of Spring (Introduction + Augurs of Spring)

---

## Step 0 — Structural Approach

**DEVELOPMENTAL (new — self-creating structural logic)**

This isn't developmental in the way I think about development. Development for me means: you play what you know, then play what you don't know. The piece doesn't do that; the piece is entirely what you don't know. But it has an arc. It goes somewhere. It starts with a single voice in discomfort and arrives at a chord that shouldn't exist, repeated until it fills the world. That's an arc. I'll score it as developmental in the sense that the structure builds toward something.

---

## Review

I want to talk about economy.

The bassoon opening. Single voice. Uncomfortable register. No accompaniment. The melody goes where it needs to go without decoration. Six notes in. You already know you're in a different place. That is economy of means producing maximum displacement.

The thing I know about economy is this: it's not about notes. It's about what the notes are doing. I can play one note and waste it. I can play twenty notes and none of them are wasted. The question is whether each choice is necessary. The bassoon note in that register — is it necessary? Yes. Nothing else produces that specific quality of vulnerability-before-rupture. The composer tried it in the comfortable register and chose the uncomfortable one. That's a Craft decision.

The Augurs chord. I want to think about the economy of that chord. It's two things at once — E and Eb, maximum possible dissonance in a twelve-tone system. Could it be one thing? Yes. Could it be a conventional dissonance — a tritone, a minor second? Yes. But neither of those produces what this produces: the feeling of the ground itself being two things at once, the primal before harmony. The polytonal chord is economical because no other choice produces exactly this effect. That's the economy test.

The irregular accents. This is where economy gets interesting. You could write regular accents — put the emphasis on the downbeat every bar. That's the conventional choice, and it would create energy. But Stravinsky's irregular accents create something different: unease. You can't predict when the next accent lands. You're always a beat behind. Economy question: is the irregularity necessary? Is there a version with regular accents that does what the irregular version does? No. The regular version produces rhythmic energy. The irregular version produces existential unease. Those are different effects. The irregular accents are economical.

One thing I'll say about this music compared to what I do: I use space. This piece doesn't use space the way I use space — it fills the texture. But the filling is necessary. The chord has to hammer. The insistence is the point. If there were rests, the chord would be commentary; without rests, it is the world. Economy in this piece means maximum density in service of maximum insistence. That's different from my economy but it's economy.

On Originality: this is 15. It has to be. Nothing before it could have produced this. Nothing after it could have produced it for the first time. The specific configuration — bassoon in that register, that chord, those irregular accents, all simultaneous — is unmistakably this composer's choice at this moment. I would know this was Stravinsky if I heard it with no context. That's originality.
