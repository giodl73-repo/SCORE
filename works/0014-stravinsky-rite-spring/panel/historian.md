# Work: 0014 — The Rite of Spring (Introduction + Augurs of Spring)

## Step 0 — Structural Approach

**DEVELOPMENTAL (new — self-creating structural logic)**

Historically, the Rite of Spring created a new structural category in Western music. The claim that it establishes its own structural logic rather than following an existing one is confirmed by its historical reception: composers who followed Stravinsky had to decide whether to accept, reject, or find an alternative to the structural logic the Rite created. This was not true of developmental works that extended the existing tradition; those works provided models to be followed. The Rite provided a rupture point to be reckoned with.

---

## Review

The premiere riot of May 29, 1913 is the most famous event in modern music history. I want to use it carefully as evidence, because it is easy to misuse.

**What the riot does and does not prove:**

The riot proves that the audience at the premiere was not prepared for what they heard. This is evidence that the work was genuinely new — that the audience had no existing framework for receiving it. A work that shocked an educated Parisian concert audience in 1913 was not shocking because of its subject matter (pagan ritual had been set to music before) but because of its musical language. The audience was rioting because the music was doing something the music was not supposed to do.

This is not evidence of failure. It is evidence of a gap between the work's originality and the audience's preparedness. The gap closed quickly — the piece was received enthusiastically a month later, and within a decade it was one of the most performed works in the orchestral repertoire. What the riot documents is not that the work failed but that it was so new that an educated audience needed a month to understand it.

**Historical rupture as Originality evidence (Innovation #30):**

When a work causes a paradigm shift — when composers either embrace or react against it — this is evidence of ceiling Originality. The Rite of Spring caused exactly this paradigm shift. Every significant composer of the 20th century had to take a position on it: Schoenberg's serialism is in part a reaction against Stravinsky's approach; neoclassicism (which Stravinsky himself embraced in the 1920s) is partly a response to the Rite's rupture; minimalism in the 1960s-80s engaged with the Rite's use of static repetition. No work in the Western classical tradition of the 20th century can be fully understood without reference to what the Rite did in 1913.

**The rubric's temporal question:**

The rubric's Originality dimension asks about the work at the moment of creation, not in retrospect. This is the correct scoring principle — the Rite should be scored against the musical world of 1913, not against a world that has absorbed its innovations. Against the world of 1913: the polytonal Augurs chord, the irregular accents, the bassoon register — none of these existed as established techniques. The work was genuinely new at the moment of creation, without the retrospective evidence of influence.

But historical rupture as evidence of Originality (Innovation #30) raises the question: does retrospective evidence improve the Originality score? My position: it does not change the score (which is assessed at the moment of creation) but it confirms it. The historical evidence that the Rite caused a paradigm shift is evidence that the Originality assessment of 14-15 was correct — that the work genuinely was ceiling-level original in 1913. The historical evidence confirms the scoring rather than producing it.

**Historical context: 1913**

In 1913, the Western classical tradition was in a period of maximum pressure. Wagner's chromaticism had expanded functional harmony to its limits; Debussy's impressionism had loosened the tonal center; Schoenberg's atonal works of 1908-1913 were abandoning tonality entirely. The Rite arrives in the same year as Schoenberg's Pierrot Lunaire (1912 premiere) and the same year as the publication of Schoenberg's theoretical work on atonal composition. It was a moment when multiple composers were independently recognizing that the existing tradition had reached its limits. The Rite is not an accident of history; it is the inevitable product of a tradition under maximum pressure.
