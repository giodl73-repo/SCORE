# Reviewer: Johann Sebastian Bach (25%)
# Work: 0014 — The Rite of Spring (Introduction + Augurs of Spring)

---

## Step 0 — Structural Approach

**DEVELOPMENTAL (new — self-creating structural logic)**

I must be honest: I cannot find the fugue here. I cannot find the tonic-dominant structural relationship. I cannot find the voice leading I would recognize as voice leading. I find something else — something that is not disorder, because disorder does not feel like this, but that I do not have the vocabulary to describe from within my tradition.

I will proceed by examining what I can examine: the logic of the piece within its own premises, whatever those premises are.

---

## Review

Let me tell you what I hear first, before any analysis.

I hear a bassoon at the top of its range, alone. The melody is irregular, folk-derived, without the regular phrase structure I would impose on such material. Then I hear accumulation — other voices adding to the bassoon, but not in harmony in my sense. Then I hear the chord.

The chord is two keys simultaneously. I understand this theoretically; I can see that it is an E major triad combined with an Eb dominant seventh. In my harmonic language, this is not a chord — it is a collision. Two harmonic objects occupying the same sonic space simultaneously. And then it is repeated, in accents that do not follow the regular pattern of any meter I recognize.

What I must determine is whether this is incompetence or intention. If a student brought me this chord with these irregular accents, I would ask him: "What happened to the harmony? Where did you lose the voice leading?" He would not be able to answer, because he would have made these choices out of ignorance. Stravinsky is not ignorant. The Firebird and Petrushka, which precede this work, demonstrate complete command of classical harmonic technique. The choices in the Rite are not errors; they are departures. A departure from knowledge is a different thing from an error made in ignorance.

I must score this by its own logic. And within its own logic:

**On Structure:** The Introduction accumulates consistently. The Augurs hammers consistently. These are structural principles — not my structural principles, but coherent ones. The transition between Introduction and Augurs is abrupt, but the abruptness is deliberate. The formal logic is: accumulation produces rupture; rupture produces insistence; insistence produces world. I can follow this logic even if I would not compose this way.

**On Craft:** Within the harmonic language this piece establishes — polytonal, without functional resolution — the voice leading is internally consistent. There is no "correct" voice leading for two simultaneous key centers; the piece establishes its own standards and meets them. The irregular accents are precisely placed — I can see on the score that the accent marks are not arbitrary but follow a changing pattern that is internally organized. This is difficult to compose. It requires knowing what the pattern is and placing the accents accordingly. This is craft even if it is craft in service of something I find foreign.

**On Originality:** I will say something that may surprise: I recognize this piece's formal logic as related to my own. In the Well-Tempered Clavier, I visit all twenty-four keys to argue that the tonal system is universal — that no key is privileged, that the system is complete. Stravinsky's polytonal chord — E and Eb simultaneously — is a different kind of completeness argument: the argument that the tonal system as previously constituted is insufficient, that both its adjacent pitches must be present to say what needs to be said. He is using two keys simultaneously where I use them sequentially. The logic is related even if the result is entirely different.

**On Resonance:** I must be honest. I do not feel this music in the way I feel the St. Matthew Passion or the B minor Mass. The primordial violence, the pre-civilizational energy, the earth before harmony — these are not experiences I recognize as the purposes of music. Music, in my understanding, serves God or serves the listener; it organizes sound to glorify something. What this music glorifies — if "glorify" is even the right word — is rupture itself. I cannot give full marks for Resonance from my position, but I can acknowledge that for a listener whose understanding of music's purposes is broader than mine, this Resonance may be complete.
