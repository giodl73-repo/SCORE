## Work Description

The Introduction: a solo bassoon in its highest, most uncomfortable register. The sound is not "the bassoon" as the classical tradition uses it — warm, lower-register, reliably supportive. It is the bassoon at the top of its range, where the instrument sounds strained, almost vocal in its complaint. The melody is folk-derived, irregular in phrase length. There is no orchestral support; the melody is alone in a register the instrument was not expected to occupy.

The Augurs of Spring: a single chord — Eb dominant seventh superimposed on E major (both sounding simultaneously, a polytonal clash producing maximum dissonance) — hammered in irregular accents. Not 2+3, not 3+2, but irregular groupings that change with each bar. The accent marks appear in unexpected positions. The effect: the Earth itself speaking, without the permission of the ear.

The premiere on May 29, 1913 caused a riot. Audience members fought each other in the aisles. Nijinsky stood on a chair backstage shouting counts to the dancers, who could not hear the music over the audience noise. The conductor, Pierre Monteux, kept playing. These events are documented.
