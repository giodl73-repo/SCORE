# Miles Davis on "Spiegel im Spiegel"

## Opening

I invented the stasis approach in jazz. I know what stasis is. I know what it costs the composer to refuse to develop. And I know what it gives back.

This piece does what I was looking for in "So What." It does it differently. Part was looking for something I wasn't looking for. But we were looking in the same direction.

Let me tell you what I hear in "Spiegel im Spiegel."

I hear someone playing one note for four beats. Or thirty beats. Or however long the note lasts. And in that note, I hear the person who played it deciding not to play the next note yet. Not because they don't know the next note. Because the note they are playing has not finished yet.

That's the practice. That's the hardest practice in music.

## Structure: 20/25

The mirror logic is structure. I accept this. The two voices reflecting each other, the melodic voice moving through the scale while the tintinnabulus voice rings the triad -- that's a system, and the system generates the piece completely. I respect that.

What I push on: the mirror logic doesn't tell you what to do inside the system. It tells you the relationship between the two voices. But within that relationship, the piece makes one choice: slow. Very slow. And then it makes the same choice again. And again. And the question I always ask is: what were you looking for in that slowness? Not "why slow?" -- I know why slow. The question is: did the slowness find what it was looking for?

I think it did. I think the piece found what it was looking for in the sustained tone. I just want to push on whether it knows what it found.

I dock five points because the structure, while coherent, is one-dimensional. The mirror logic is the only structural principle; the slowness is the only tempo; the A major arpeggio is the only harmonic material. This is by design, and it is beautiful. But it creates a piece that does one structural thing and does it for ten minutes. The Crab Canon proves its palindrome in thirty measures. "So What" creates its modal space in thirty-two bars of head. "Spiegel" creates its mirror in ten minutes. I want to know what the extra eight minutes added to what the first two minutes established.

## Craft: 22/25

The choice of A major. The choice of 6/4. The choice of piano and violin. The tempo. These are all exactly right. Part has the same quality of choice I aspire to: not many choices, but exactly the right ones.

The violin's sustained tones: the performer has to find what is in each note. Not ornament it. Not demonstrate technique. Find what is in it. This is the hardest kind of craft -- the craft of restraint against the instrument's natural desire to fill. Every violinist who plays this piece has to suppress the instinct to vibrate, to slide, to vary. The craft requirement is negative: do less than you know how to do.

That is craft. I recognize it.

Three points off for the piano's monotony. The arpeggio is exactly right; it is exactly right for the entire ten minutes; it does not change. I wonder what would happen if it did change -- if one note of the arpeggio, very late in the piece, was slightly different. I am not saying this should happen. I am asking the question because the question reveals something about the piece: it doesn't want to change. And not wanting to change is a choice. But I need to note it.

## Originality: 13/15

The tintinnabuli method is original. The application of it to the mirror image is original. The specific combination of extreme slowness, A major, piano and violin, and the sustained violin tone as primary musical event: original.

Two points off because the sustained drone is ancient, and because "Fur Alina" (1976) already established the tintinnabuli technique in this specific form. "Spiegel" deploys the same technique in a specific image. The technique is original; the image is original; the deployment is the same as the preceding work.

## Resonance: 14/15

The piece does what I said it does: it creates the sensation of suspended time. For a listener who is willing to stay with it.

That caveat is real. "Spiegel" asks the listener to accept that nothing is going to happen, and that this is not a problem. Not every listener accepts this. The ones who do have the experience the piece is offering. The ones who don't get bored.

I don't think the piece fails for the bored listener. I think the bored listener hasn't arrived yet at the question the piece is asking. The question is: can you be present in a note? Not excited by it. Not entertained. Present. In that note, for as long as it lasts.

One point off: the piece asks this question but doesn't answer it for the listener. It holds the space. Whether the listener enters the space is their work, not the piece's. I respect that. I just want to note it.

## Economy: 19/20

Almost perfect economy. I dock one point because I still want to know what the extra time added to what the first two minutes established. Not enough to dock two points -- I'm not sure it doesn't add. But I'm not sure it does.

What I know: "So What" runs nine minutes and twenty-two seconds. It has solos -- multiple musicians making choices in real time. "Spiegel" runs ten minutes and has two voices making the same choices throughout. "So What" needs most of those nine minutes. "Spiegel" needs most of those ten minutes. But "Spiegel" needs them differently: not because more is being said, but because the saying requires the time.

## Innovation Note

Part was looking for something I was looking for. We found different things in the same direction. My stasis is the refusal to be pushed forward by chord changes -- the freedom to explore the space that opens when you're not required to move. Part's stasis is the practice of being fully present in the note you are playing. These are not the same stasis. The rubric does not currently distinguish between them. It should.
