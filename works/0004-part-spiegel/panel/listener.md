# The Listener on "Spiegel im Spiegel"

## First Hearing

I did not know what I was listening to. I did not know what "tintinnabuli" means or who Arvo Part is. I was told: piano and violin, ten minutes, A major, the piece has been used in many films about death and grief.

I began listening and immediately understood something. Not something I could put in words. I understood where I was. I was in a place where nothing needed to happen next.

The piano is playing constantly -- a slow, steady arpeggio that does not change. The violin is playing one note. Just one note, for a long time. Then it moves to the next note. Very slowly. The combination of these two things is not what I expected "piano and violin" to mean. There is no melody in the violin. There is no accompaniment in the piano. There is a piano that rings, and a violin that is present in the ring.

## What Held Attention

The piece is ten minutes long. I expected to lose the thread, the way I lose the thread in music that does not move. I did not lose the thread.

What held my attention: the moment when the violin changed notes. I found myself waiting for it. Not impatiently -- I was not in a hurry. But with a kind of alert stillness. When the violin moved from one note to the next, I felt it. Not loudly. But I felt it.

The piano never changed. The piano's unchanging was the context in which the violin's slow movement was an event. The event would not have been an event without the unchanging context.

## What I Remembered

The overall feeling more than any specific moment. The feeling of being held still while time moved around me rather than through me.

The moment the violin reached the top of its ascent -- the highest note before beginning to descend. That moment was the apex of something, even though nothing changed in the music's structure.

## The Emotional Register

The piece is contemplative. But that word is too mild. It is contemplative the way deep water is contemplative: it requires your attention before it will show you anything.

The emotional register is not sad. It is suspended. If I had to name the feeling: "before." Before whatever comes next. The music is permanently in the moment before whatever comes next, and "whatever comes next" is held in abeyance for the entire ten minutes.

I can understand why this piece is used in films about death. Death is the thing that comes next that nothing can prepare you for. "Spiegel im Spiegel" is the ten minutes before.

## The Patience Question

Some listeners will not stay for this piece. I understand why. Nothing happens, in the sense that events occur. If you need events, you will not find them here.

But if you can stay, what you find is not nothing. What you find is what is between the events -- the space that events usually pass through so quickly that you cannot inhabit it. This piece makes you inhabit the space between.
