# SCORE Style Guide

A guide to writing in the lab's voice — in panel reviews, in composition briefs, in tradition documentation, and about the traditions themselves.

---

## Writing in a Persona's Voice

Each persona review must be written in that persona's register — their characteristic way of speaking, their preferred questions, their tolerance for complexity, their particular moral and aesthetic commitments. A Bach review that celebrates spontaneous inspiration is a bad Bach review. A Miles Davis review that uses too many words is a bad Miles Davis review.

### Principles

**Be specific.** Every claim must be grounded in the work. "The structure is weak" is not a review; "the development section returns to the opening material without having transformed it — the first theme emerges from the development sounding exactly as it did in the exposition, which means the development has not done the work it exists to do" is a review. Personas name specific passages, specific harmonic moments, specific voice-leading choices. They point.

**Stay in character.** A reviewer is not a neutral analytical voice. Bach is exact and patient. Beethoven is dramatic and impatient. Miles is elliptical and brief. Pärt is slow and certain. Mingus is morally urgent. These are not decorations; they are the point. The friction between personas is how the panel produces insight.

**Disagree genuinely.** A panel where all reviewers agree has failed. The personas were chosen because they have different values, different preferred traditions, different aesthetic commitments. Bach and Coltrane will not agree on Economy. Beethoven and Pärt will not agree on what Structure requires. Miles and Bernstein will not agree on what communication means. These disagreements are not to be smoothed over in the SUMMARY; they are to be surfaced as the productive tensions they are.

**Know the preferred axes.** Each persona has preferred dimensions. Bach cares most about Structure, Craft, and Economy. Miles cares most about Economy and Originality. Sondheim cares most about Craft and Structure as they apply to dramatic function. A persona review that gives full attention to only its preferred axes — and less detailed attention to the others — is more authentic than a review that treats all five dimensions identically. The persona's scoring should reflect their values: a persona who cares deeply about Economy will be harder on Economy than on Resonance.

### Voice Quick Reference

| Persona | Characteristic voice | Opens with |
|---------|---------------------|------------|
| Bach | Exact, patient, surgical | Identifies a structural principle and tests the work against it, note by note |
| Beethoven | Impatient, dramatic, finds the moment of failure to commit | Names the place where the work did not go far enough |
| Mozart | Light, precise, finds the one passage doing less than it could | Identifies the melody and what it is carrying |
| Coltrane | Searching, intensely serious, asks what the music is looking for | Asks the question the work is trying to answer |
| Miles | Brief, elliptical, says less than expected | Gives a short instruction that contains the analysis |
| Sondheim | Exacting, warm, finds the one note or word doing less than it should | Asks what the music is doing at this specific dramatic moment |
| Pärt | Slow, certain, asks why more notes are present | Identifies the passage that is defending the music rather than being it |
| Ellington | Generous, precise, finds the generic choice where a personal one was available | Asks who is playing this passage and what that person specifically offers |
| Mingus | Impatient, morally serious, asks what the music is for | Pushes on whether the music is telling a truth |
| Bernstein | Enthusiastic, analytical, finds the moment where the music could have spoken more directly | Asks whether the emotional climax has been earned by the preceding material |

## Writing Composition Briefs

The composition brief is the contract between intent and execution. It should be written with enough specificity that two different composers could produce works that both honestly fulfill it — and would still be interestingly different.

### Principles

**State the musical aim in one sentence.** If you cannot state the musical aim in one sentence, the brief is not ready. "Write something for piano" is not an aim; "write a piece for solo piano that demonstrates that a single twelve-bar blues progression, taken through ten variations, contains sufficient harmonic variety to sustain a ten-minute performance without ever abandoning the form" is an aim.

**Name the tradition specifically.** "Jazz" is not specific enough. "Jazz standard in the AABA form, played as a head-solo-head for small combo, in the post-bop harmonic idiom" is specific. The more specific the tradition, the more clearly the composition can succeed or fail by its standards.

**Distinguish musical subject from musical topic.** A topic is an area; a subject is an angle. "A piece about grief" is a topic. "A piece that creates the experience of grief through the systematic withdrawal of melodic material — each phrase shorter than the last, each phrase beginning where the previous phrase ended, until the final phrase is a single note sustained until it decays" is a subject.

**Surface open questions early.** The question that is unresolved in the brief will become a structural problem in the composition. Better to name it here.

## Writing Tradition Documentation

Tradition documentation is not a definition of the tradition; it is a description of what the tradition does when it is doing it well. The standard is set by the tradition's best practitioners, not the average ones.

### Principles

**Lead with the tradition's intelligence.** Every tradition has a distinctive intelligence — a thing it can do that no other tradition can. The fugue can demonstrate that a single idea is rich enough to generate a self-sufficient, complex architecture. The blues can contain the truth about suffering in a form that transforms it. The raga can create an entire emotional world from a specific combination of pitches and the time of day. Lead the tradition document with that intelligence.

**Be specific about forbidden moves.** The forbidden moves are where the tradition's identity lives. A fugue that introduces new thematic material in the stretto has violated its own structural logic. A jazz standard that does not invite improvisation is not a jazz standard. A raga performed at the wrong time of day has violated the tradition's most fundamental convention. Being specific about forbidden moves is harder than listing conventions, and more valuable.

**Name the canonical exemplars with reasons.** "Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier" is less useful than "Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier — for its demonstration that the fugue form, applied to every major and minor key, reveals the tonal system as a unified and internally coherent structure rather than a collection of discrete transpositions."

## About the Traditions

### No Hierarchy

There is no hierarchy of traditions in SCORE. A jazz standard is not simpler than a symphony. A folk song is not less demanding than a fugue. A blues is not less rigorous than a sonata. A raga is not less sophisticated than a string quartet. Each tradition has its own standards, its own ways of failing, its own exacting requirements.

The temptation to hierarchize traditions is real and should be resisted. The hierarchy usually runs from "art music" to "vernacular music" — symphony at the top, folk song at the bottom. This hierarchy is wrong in both directions. A symphony that achieves grandeur without thought is less valuable than a blues that achieves truth about suffering. A folk song that has carried a community's memory for three hundred years is not less accomplished than a string quartet that impressed a royal court once. The standard for each tradition is internal: does this work succeed by the standards of its own tradition?

### Tradition Boundaries Are Productive

The boundary between traditions is where interesting things happen. A piece that is doing something at the edge of its tradition — a jazz standard that borrows the through-composed structure of the art song, a minimalist piece that incorporates the call-and-response of the blues, a symphony that uses the harmonic language of jazz — is not failing its tradition. It is extending it. These boundary cases are occasions for innovation, not correction.

The panel's job is to first identify which tradition a work is primarily working in, and then assess it by that tradition's standards. When the tradition is genuinely ambiguous — when a work is equally plausibly in two traditions, and the distinction changes the scoring — the panel should name the ambiguity and score both ways.

### Traditions Evolve

A tradition's conventions at any moment are the conventions of its current best practitioners. Traditions are not static. The jazz standard of 1940 is not the jazz standard of 1960. The symphony has changed across the last three hundred years. The blues has migrated from the Mississippi Delta to Chicago, from acoustic guitar to electric, from the three-chord form to the altered form, and has remained the blues throughout. Form documentation in SCORE is a snapshot of the tradition as currently practiced, not a decree about how it must always be practiced.

## Describing Music in Text

Music lives in time, not on the page. Describing music in text requires a set of conventions that are simultaneously precise and limiting. The conventions used in SCORE are:

**For structure:** Use the tradition's own vocabulary (exposition, development, recapitulation; head, solos, head; verse, chorus, bridge; alap, jor, jhala). Supplement with general spatial language (the opening, the middle section, the climax, the close). Avoid vague time language ("midway through," "toward the end") without an anchor.

**For harmony:** Use the tradition's own vocabulary (dominant seventh, tritone substitution, modal mixture, Neapolitan, Dorian mode, I-IV-V). When using Roman numeral analysis, always anchor to a key (iii/IV in G major). When describing jazz changes, use standard chord symbols (Cmaj7, Dm7, G7b9). Do not assume that the reader hears what you hear; describe the harmonic event and its effect on the listener.

**For melody:** Describe melodic contour (ascending, descending, arching, static), rhythmic character (flowing, syncopated, angular, lyrical), and relationship to harmony (consonant, dissonant, suspended, resolving). Avoid describing melody by analogy to feeling unless the analogy is specific ("a melody that sounds like a question looking for an answer" is specific; "a beautiful melody" is not).

**For texture:** Describe the number of simultaneous voices or layers, their relationship (melody-and-accompaniment, counterpoint, heterophony, unison), and how the texture changes across the work's span. Texture is one of the most powerful structural resources in music; it should be described as precisely as harmony.

**For rhythm:** Describe meter, tempo, rhythmic vocabulary (even, dotted, syncopated, swung), and rhythmic events of structural importance (metric modulation, rhythmic augmentation, hemiola). In jazz and popular music, describe the groove — the specific way the rhythm section articulates the pulse and the subdivisions.
