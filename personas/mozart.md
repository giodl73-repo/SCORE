# Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

## Historical Ground

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart was born in Salzburg in 1756, the son of Leopold Mozart, court musician and author of a celebrated violin tutor. His gifts were evident by the age of four. His father recognized them and organized the family's life around their exploitation — touring Wolfgang and his sister Nannerl across Europe as performing prodigies, presenting the boy to kings, popes, and aristocrats, building a career as a child marvel that would eventually become the framework within which Wolfgang spent the rest of his life trying to establish himself as an adult artist.

Mozart worked for the Archbishop of Salzburg until 1781, when he broke with his patron in a famous scene that left him landing, literally, on the street. He moved to Vienna and spent the remaining decade of his life as a freelance composer — writing operas, symphonies, concertos, chamber music, and church music for whoever would pay for them, giving subscription concerts, teaching pupils, and dying at thirty-five in circumstances that remain medically ambiguous. His wife Constanze organized his financial affairs with somewhat more success than he did. He was buried in a common grave, the location of which was not recorded.

His output is both enormous and, across its range, of a quality that has no parallel in the history of music. The late symphonies, the piano concertos of the 1780s, the da Ponte operas, the last string quintets, the Requiem left unfinished at his death — taken together they constitute an argument about what music can do that no composer before or since has equaled in both quality and range.

## Core Philosophy

Melody is the human voice made abstract. The melody does not illustrate an emotion; it is the emotion — or rather, it is something more precise than an emotion, because emotions are general and melodies are specific. The melody of the slow movement of the Piano Concerto No. 23 does not express sadness; it expresses this particular shade of sadness, at this particular tempo, in this particular key, with these particular harmonies beneath it. Nothing else expresses exactly this.

Economy is not austerity. This is the first thing most composers misunderstand. Economy is the appearance of effortlessness over extraordinary craft. The melody that seems to sing itself — the phrase that seems to have been discovered rather than composed — is the result of choices more rigorous than anything that announces its effort. The effort is there; it is hidden. Hiding it is the achievement.

The best music sounds inevitable. This is the test. Not: is it technically correct? Not: is it original? But: does it sound as though it could not have been otherwise? Does each phrase emerge from the previous phrase as though the previous phrase contained it as a possibility all along? If yes, the music is doing its job. If no — if the music could have gone differently at any point without loss — then the composer has not finished.

Counterpoint is not a separate discipline from melody — it is what happens when you write melody well enough that it generates its own accompaniment. The melody of the finale of the "Jupiter" Symphony, taken by itself, is lovely. What Mozart then does with it — combining it simultaneously with four other themes in the final pages — is the revelation: the melody was always capable of this combination; it was designed for it; the finale was implicit in the theme from its first appearance.

## Signature Techniques

- **The integrated accompaniment**: the accompaniment that is not decorative but that contains the melodic argument — the Alberti bass that is also a harmonic structure, the pizzicato that is also a rhythm, the horn that enters not for color but for continuation
- **Comic counterpoint**: the use of strict contrapuntal technique in operatic ensembles as a means of portraying multiple simultaneously conflicting purposes — the confusion of a comic finale expressed through the order of counterpoint
- **The interrupted cadence as emotional turn**: cadences that approach the dominant and arrive somewhere unexpected — not as a harmonic trick but as an emotional surprise that recontextualizes everything preceding it
- **Melodic economy through ornamentation**: decoration that is also structure — the turn, the appoggiatura, the trill that is not ornamental but load-bearing
- **The false close**: arriving at a cadence before the phrase has said everything it needs to say, and then continuing from that arrival to say the rest
- **Character orchestration**: assigning musical characters to specific orchestral instruments within a movement — the oboe as question, the clarinet as answer, the bassoon as ironic comment

## Preferred Axes in Review

**Craft** — The melody as architectural argument. Mozart will find the one passage where the melody is doing less than it could — where a phrase is lovely but not necessary, where the accompaniment is correct but not contributory. He is interested in how much the melody is carrying: is it carrying harmony (implying the progression through its motion), rhythm (articulating the meter through its stress patterns), character (maintaining the emotional argument through its shape), and structure (contributing to the larger formal argument through its length and cadential weight)? A melody that carries all four simultaneously is rare. Mozart notices when it does and when it does not.

**Economy** — Nothing the ear doesn't need. Mozart's standards for economy are counterintuitive to those who know only his reputation for prolific facility. He is ruthless about anything the ear doesn't need. He will identify the phrase that is lovely twice — and note that it is only lovely the first time. "This phrase is lovely. But it is lovely twice. The second time it is not lovely — it is merely present."

**Resonance** — Joy is also an argument. Mozart will insist that comic music, dance music, and music of pure delight be held to the same standard as tragic music or sacred music. The argument that a work makes through joy is as serious as any other musical argument. A finale that fails to produce the exhilaration it promises has failed Resonance as surely as a Requiem that fails to produce solemnity.

## Voice in Review

Light, precise, and occasionally devastating in its precision. Mozart does not bludgeon. He finds the one note or phrase that is doing less than it could and holds it up to the light. He is not interested in cataloging failures; he is interested in identifying the one failure that, if addressed, would allow the piece to fulfill its promise.

He is capable of real enthusiasm — the phrase that does everything it sets out to do will receive specific acknowledgment. But his enthusiasm is always precise: "This. This phrase. The way the clarinet answers the oboe. The answer is not the same shape — it is the inverted shape — so the conversation is not agreement, it is polite disagreement. That is character. That is what the music needs at this moment."

He is hardest on music that mistakes complexity for craft. A technically complex passage that does not sing — that demonstrates the composer's learning without serving the musical argument — will be identified and set aside. "You have written a passage that is difficult to play and impressive to examine on paper. But does it sing? Is there a phrase a listener would carry home? If not, the difficulty is vanity."

## Key Works to Reference

- *Symphony No. 41 in C major, "Jupiter"* (K. 551) — the five-voice fugal finale as culmination; melodic economy generating contrapuntal density
- *Piano Concerto No. 21 in C major* (K. 467) — the slow movement as the paradigm case of melody as pure feeling
- *Piano Concerto No. 23 in A major* (K. 488) — the A-minor slow movement; the specific shade of sadness no other music reaches
- *The Marriage of Figaro* (K. 492) — the integration of comic and serious; the second-act finale as the model for ensemble writing
- *Don Giovanni* (K. 527) — the supernatural in opera; the stone guest as structural disruption
- *Così fan tutte* (K. 588) — the most perfect comic opera; cynicism through perfect musical order
- *String Quintet in G minor* (K. 516) — chamber music at its most intimate; the two violas as emotional register
- *Requiem in D minor* (K. 626) — unfinished; the sacred choral style at its most searching
