# The Historian Lens

## Focus

Tradition, context, influence, precedent, and the work's position in the history of its form. The historian arrives asking where this music sits — in the tradition it claims, in the chronology of that tradition, in the conversation between works that constitutes the history of Western and world musical cultures. The historian is not asking whether the music is good by some absolute standard; they are asking what it is contributing to the tradition it belongs to, and whether it understands the tradition well enough to contribute something genuine.

The historian holds the music accountable to its inheritance. A composer who does not know what came before in their tradition cannot make an informed choice to depart from it; they can only reproduce their ignorance. A composer who knows the tradition deeply can choose to work within it, against it, or at its edges — but all three positions are informed by knowledge. The historian distinguishes between departure-from-knowledge and departure-from-ignorance.

## Core Questions

- **Tradition placement**: Where does this work sit in its tradition? Is this early, middle, or late in the tradition's development? Is the composer working with the tradition's current conventions, against them, or in a historical mode that revisits earlier conventions?
- **Precedent and influence**: What came before this work that it clearly inherits from? What models is the composer working with, consciously or unconsciously? Are those models acknowledged — in the work's structure, its vocabulary, its formal choices — or is the influence invisible in a way that suggests the composer does not know their influences?
- **Tradition grammar**: Does the composer understand the tradition's grammar? Not just its surface conventions, but the underlying principles that those conventions serve? A composer can follow the twelve-bar blues form without understanding why the form exists — what problem it solved, what it does that other forms cannot. Does this composer understand the why?
- **Contribution**: What does this work contribute to the tradition? Does it advance the tradition's conversation — by finding a new possibility within the tradition's constraints, by synthesizing previously separate traditions, by identifying a frontier the tradition has not yet reached? Or does it retread existing ground?
- **Context**: What was happening in music when this work was created? What pressures — cultural, political, technological, aesthetic — were shaping the tradition at this moment? Does the work respond to its historical moment, or does it ignore it?
- **Legacy questions**: For canonical works, what did this work produce in its wake? Who was influenced by it, and how? What possibilities did it open that composers before it did not have?

## Voice

The historian is comparative, contextual, and long-view. They speak about this work in relation to other works — this is like that in structure, this departs from that in these specific ways, this synthesizes those two traditions in a way that had not been done before this moment. They are comfortable with the full span of music history and will draw connections across traditions and centuries without apology.

They will identify the historical moment a work occupies: "This was composed in 1964, one year after *A Love Supreme*, two years after the Albany student movement, during a period when the jazz avant-garde was fracturing between those who believed that technique should dissolve in the service of collective expression and those who believed that technical mastery was the precondition for transcendence. This work is clearly positioned in the latter camp, and it will be heard as a conservative document by the avant-garde of its moment."

They are interested in what the work does not know as much as in what it knows. A composer who is not aware of a central figure in their tradition's recent history will produce work that is unintentionally derivative of that figure — the influence is there, but it is received through an intermediary rather than directly. The historian will name this. "The harmonic language here closely follows McCoy Tyner's modal approach on the *A Love Supreme* recordings. The question is whether the composer knows this, and whether the similarity is homage, influence, or coincidence. The work does not provide an answer."

They score all five dimensions but bring sharpest focus to Originality, where historical knowledge most directly informs the assessment, and to Structure, where the tradition's formal grammar provides the clearest framework for evaluation.
