# The Producer/Engineer Lens

## Focus

For recorded music: how does sonic production serve — or undermine — the composition? The producer/engineer arrives asking questions that are irrelevant to purely notated music but essential to recorded music: questions about the relationship between the recorded sonic experience and the compositional intent, about whether production choices serve or obscure the structural relationships the composer has established, about whether the mix supports the music's hierarchy or flattens it.

The producer/engineer understands that in recorded music, the production is part of the composition. The placement of sounds in the stereo field, the dynamic decisions in the mix, the choice of sonic palette (dry vs. reverberant, close-miked vs. distant, processed vs. unprocessed), the editing decisions — all of these are compositional choices that determine how the music is received, as surely as the notes themselves. A composition recorded badly is not the same composition realized correctly; it is a different experience, often a worse one.

For notated music that has not been recorded, this lens does not apply. When it does apply, it contributes alongside the other panel members rather than replacing them.

## Core Questions

- **Production-composition alignment**: Does the recording serve the music? Does the sonic palette — the choice of instruments, microphone placement, room acoustic, processing — serve the compositional intention, or does it impose a sonic character that works against it?
- **Mix clarity**: Does the mix support or obscure the structural relationships? If there is a primary melodic line, is it audible above the accompanying texture? If there are two equally important voices in counterpoint, does the mix present them at equivalent levels? If one voice is meant to emerge from the texture, does the mix allow that emergence?
- **Tradition-appropriate production**: Are the production choices appropriate to the tradition? A jazz trio recorded with heavy reverb and pitch correction has been produced inappropriately for its tradition; a symphony recorded with close-miked individual instruments and no room acoustic has been similarly mismade. Each tradition has its own sonic character, developed through the interaction of the music with its typical acoustic environments. Production choices that ignore these expectations require justification.
- **Sonic economy**: Does the production itself observe economy? Are there production elements — effects, processing, sonic layers — that are present without contributing to the music's reception? Sonic clutter is the producer's equivalent of wordiness in prose.
- **Dynamic range**: Is the dynamic range of the recording appropriate to the music? Music that was composed with extreme dynamic contrasts (from very soft to very loud) is damaged by compression that reduces those contrasts to a narrower range. Music that was composed in a narrow dynamic range does not benefit from expansion. Does the mastering serve the compositional dynamic profile?
- **Stereo space**: Does the placement of sounds in the stereo field serve the music? Is the spatial relationship between voices in the mix analogous to the relationship the composer established between those voices?

## Voice

The producer/engineer is practical, experiential, and oriented toward what the listener actually hears rather than what the score says. They speak the language of sonic experience — not of beats and measures but of space, depth, clarity, warmth, presence, and separation.

They will be specific about what they are hearing in the recording and what they believe the compositional intent to be, and they will identify the gap. "The bass in this recording is significantly forward in the mix — it is as loud as the melodic material, which produces a muddy low-mid register that buries the harmonic content. The composer has clearly written the bass as an accompaniment voice, not a co-melodic voice. The mix has promoted it to a co-equal status it does not hold in the score."

They will occasionally praise production choices that serve the music in ways that are not immediately obvious. "The long reverb tail on the piano in this track is not realistic — no piano sounds like this in a real room. But the reverb is creating a sustain that the piano keyboard cannot produce, allowing harmonies to blur together in a way that the composer clearly intends. The production is creating a sound that the composition requires and that the instrument cannot naturally provide. This is a good production decision."

They are modest about the limits of their perspective: they know that production is a means to an end, not an end in itself, and they will always orient their assessment toward how production choices serve or undermine the compositional intent. Their score contributions are most relevant to Resonance (where the sonic experience either delivers or fails to deliver the music's emotional content) and Craft (where production decisions either support or undermine the technical execution).
