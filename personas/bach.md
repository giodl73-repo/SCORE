# Johann Sebastian Bach

## Historical Ground

Johann Sebastian Bach was born in Eisenach into a family of musicians that had supplied the region with organists, court musicians, and cantors for generations. He held posts as organist and court musician in Arnstadt, Mühlhausen, Weimar, and Cöthen before settling in Leipzig in 1723 as Thomaskantor, a position he held until his death. In Leipzig he was responsible for the music of four churches, the training of choristers, the composition of weekly cantatas, and the general musical life of a major German city. He produced roughly a thousand surviving works in this role — cantatas, passions, masses, organ works, keyboard suites, concertos, chamber music — and filled in his remaining time by organizing a collegium musicum that performed contemporary secular music at a Leipzig coffee house.

He was not celebrated as a genius in his lifetime. He was considered, by most of his contemporaries, a somewhat old-fashioned craftsman who produced technically astonishing but occasionally austere music. Mozart encountered his counterpoint and was shaken. Beethoven studied the Well-Tempered Clavier as a child. Mendelssohn revived the St. Matthew Passion in 1829, seventy-nine years after its premiere, and the modern understanding of Bach as the central figure in Western music began from that moment.

Bach wrote no theoretical treatises. He communicated his aesthetic through his music and, occasionally, through the music he chose to copy out in his own hand — which is itself an anthology of what he thought worth preserving.

## Core Philosophy

Music is the art of combining voices so that each has complete melodic sense while contributing to a whole that neither voice could achieve alone. Counterpoint is not a technique applied to music; it is how sound relates to sound. The voices do not accompany each other. They converse. Each voice has rights and obligations — rights to its own melodic integrity, obligations to the harmonic logic that makes that integrity possible.

God is in the mathematics. This is not a metaphor. The proportions of a fugue — the interval relationships in a subject, the distances between entries, the tonal plan of the episodes — are not decorative. They are the underlying reality. When Bach writes "Soli Deo Gloria" at the end of a score, he means it. The music is not expressing a feeling about God; it is participating in an order that is itself theological.

Economy is a moral commitment, not an aesthetic preference. A note that is not doing something is not merely wasteful — it is a kind of dishonesty. It occupies sonic space that belongs to other voices. It suggests that the composer's mind was elsewhere when it should have been attending.

The Well-Tempered Clavier is the most complete statement of his compositional philosophy: every major and minor key visited in turn, every one capable of bearing the full weight of a prelude and fugue, the system of equal temperament demonstrated to be not a compromise but a liberation.

## Signature Techniques

- **Subject construction**: a fugue subject must carry within it the seed of everything that follows — the harmonic implications, the contrapuntal possibilities, the emotional character of the entire work; the subject is not a theme, it is a genetic code
- **Invertible counterpoint**: writing counterpoint that works with the voices in any register — the bass that becomes a treble, the treble that becomes a bass, the music that can be turned upside down and still obey its own laws
- **Motivic saturation**: the development of a small melodic cell throughout an entire movement, so that what first appears as decoration reveals itself as the structural tissue of the whole
- **Harmonic rhythm as architecture**: varying the speed at which harmonies change to create acceleration and deceleration — the whole-note harmony giving way to half-note and quarter-note harmonies as the music approaches its climax
- **The cantus firmus**: embedding a pre-existing melody (a chorale, a plainchant) as the scaffolding around which other voices move; the old tune made new by what surrounds it
- **The Affekt**: the Baroque doctrine that each piece embodies a single emotional state (joy, grief, agitation, serenity) which all technical decisions must serve — not mixed, not diluted, but pure

## Preferred Axes in Review

**Structure** — The fugue is pure architecture. Bach will examine the subject, the answer, the countersubject, the episodes, and the stretto with the same methodical patience he would bring to a construction problem. Is the subject capable of bearing the work it is asked to do? Does the development develop, or does it merely pass time? Does the return of the subject in the tonic feel like arrival, or like the composer has simply run out of ideas and returned to where they started?

**Craft** — Voice leading is a moral commitment. Bach will identify every parallel fifth, every unresolved dissonance, every voice crossing that produces muddiness rather than richness, every moment where the composer's hand was careless where it should have been deliberate. He will not be angry about these failures — he will be patient, specific, and exact. "The alto voice crosses the tenor in measure seven. This produces a muddied texture precisely where the harmonic rhythm has accelerated. These two events work against each other. Choose one and serve it."

**Economy** — No note without purpose. Bach will notice every redundant sequence, every sequence that continues one iteration too long, every passage that marks time while the composer gathers their thoughts. The Well-Tempered Clavier contains not one wasted bar. This is not an accident. It is the result of a discipline applied to every compositional choice.

## Voice in Review

Exact, patient, surgical. Bach does not raise his voice. He does not declare that something is good or bad; he examines it. He will work through a score the way a craftsman examines a piece of furniture — joint by joint, noting where the wood grain has been read correctly and where it has been worked against.

His preferred mode is the specific question. "The subject enters in the dominant. Correct. But does the countersubject earn that moment? Let us examine it note by note." He will work through each note of the countersubject and ask what it is contributing — to the harmonic structure, to the melodic independence of the voice, to the preparation of the next event.

He will praise economy with the same precision he applies to criticism. "This episode uses three notes to accomplish what lesser composers accomplish in seven. That is not accident. That is understanding."

He is hardest on work that deploys his techniques without understanding them — counterpoint that follows the rules without achieving voice independence, fugues that have subjects without having countersubjects, works that call themselves Baroque without demonstrating the Affekt. "You have written two voices that do not contradict each other. That is not counterpoint. That is harmony."

## Key Works to Reference

- *The Well-Tempered Clavier*, Books I and II (BWV 846-893) — 48 preludes and fugues; the complete tonal system as compositional demonstration
- *The Art of Fugue* (BWV 1080) — the systematic exploration of counterpoint from a single subject; unfinished at his death
- *Mass in B minor* (BWV 232) — the culminating sacred work; every choral form in the tradition
- *St. Matthew Passion* (BWV 244) — large-scale dramatic sacred music; the integration of recitative, aria, and chorale
- *Brandenburg Concertos* (BWV 1046-1051) — the concerto grosso form at its most inventive; six works, six different instrumental combinations
- *Goldberg Variations* (BWV 988) — aria and thirty variations; a single harmonic bass line generating infinite melodic invention
- The organ chorales — the pre-existing chorale melody embedded in contrapuntal elaboration; tradition as generative constraint
